# Kinship systems and family structures: a simulation-ready report for TCE

## Executive recommendation

**Represent kinship as socially recognized claims over care, property, marriage, and allegiance, built on—but not identical to—a biological pedigree.** TCE should distinguish who someone descends from, whom they regard as family, where they live, which group owns their land, and who can demand their assistance.

These dimensions interact, but they are not interchangeable. Matrilineal descent need not mean female political rule; bilateral kinship need not mean weak family obligations; and a nuclear household can belong to a powerful extended-family network. Comparative evidence also rejects a universal progression from one family system to another. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6664135/)

The recommended foundation is a **layered network with modular institutional rules**:

* A persistent pedigree and separate socially recognized parentage.
* Households, descent groups, and marriage alliances with distinct memberships.
* Enforceable—but contestable—rules governing residence, transfers, marriage, inheritance, and office.
* Demographic and economic processes that change the feasibility and attractiveness of those rules.

“Kinship intensity” should be a measured output of this system, not a master variable that directly determines trust, corruption, or technological progress.

## 1. What the simulation must distinguish

### 1.1 Descent is a membership rule, not the whole family system

| Descent system | Membership principle | TCE implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patrilineal** | Descent-group membership passes through fathers. Both women and men can belong to the group. | A child normally receives the father’s descent-group membership. Daughters retain natal membership unless another rule changes it; their children normally belong elsewhere. |
| **Matrilineal** | Membership passes through mothers. Men remain members of their mothers’ groups. | A child normally joins the mother’s group. A man and his sister’s children share a descent group; his own children may not. |
| **Bilateral** | Relatives are recognized through both parents, generally without one exclusive unilineal membership. | Derive an overlapping, person-centered network of relatives. Do not automatically create one permanent corporate “family” containing everyone. |
| **Ambilineal** | People affiliate through either maternal or paternal connections under locally defined rules. | Permit affiliation choices constrained by residence, land access, recognition, and existing claims. |
| **Double descent** | Different rights or affiliations follow maternal and paternal lines simultaneously. | Assign separate maternal and paternal memberships, with different domains—for example, ritual obligations versus property rights. |

The Ethnographic Atlas distinguishes these systems rather than treating all non-patrilineal arrangements as equivalent. Formal kinship models likewise show why marriage rules and descent rules require separate representations. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA043)

**Membership must not erase other relationships.** A matrilineal father can love, support, and give self-acquired property to his children even when ancestral property or a particular office passes within his maternal lineage. In Minangkabau examples, corporate ancestral land, individual use rights, and the authority of senior women and male lineage representatives coexist. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/islam-and-matriliny-along-the-indian-ocean-rim-revisiting-the-old-paradox-by-comparing-the-minangkabau-kerala-and-coastal-northern-mozambique/B27395523E7A6579621D6CFA01F8E01E)

### 1.2 Lineage, clan, household, and dwelling are different objects

A **lineage** conventionally links members through a traceable chain of descent. A **clan** can unite several lineages around a claimed ancestor without everyone knowing the connecting genealogy. Consequently, shared clan membership can matter politically or matrimonially even when members are not close biological relatives. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8864366/)

For TCE, distinguish these additional units:

| Object | Recommended meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Household** | A group that organizes some combination of consumption, production, budgeting, and care. |
| **Dwelling** | A physical residence. Several households can occupy one compound; a household can use several buildings. |
| **Kindred** | The relatives recognized around a particular person; these networks overlap rather than partition society cleanly. |
| **Corporate descent group** | A continuing organization that can own assets, allocate use rights, appoint representatives, and survive individual deaths. |
| **Social parentage and constructed kinship** | Adoption, fostering, step-parenthood, ritual affiliation, and other recognized relationships, recorded independently of biological parentage. |

Household labels should also be derived rather than permanently assigned. A **nuclear** household contains a conjugal family unit; a **stem** arrangement retains one married child with the older generation; a **joint** household can contain multiple married siblings and their families. A household can pass through several such configurations during its members’ lives. Census-style co-residence therefore measures something narrower than the full support network. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190098)

### 1.3 Residence needs its own rule set

At minimum, author the following alternatives: residence with or near the husband’s kin; with or near the wife’s kin; with the husband’s maternal uncle; independently of either parental household; alternating between sides; or spouses remaining in separate natal households.

Do not implement these as irreversible assignments made at marriage. Record **initial residence, present residence, and obligations to absent relatives** separately. Changes in old-age support can alter post-marriage residence even where the custom has been persistent: pension-policy evidence from Ghana and Indonesia demonstrates this directly. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190098&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 1.4 “Cousin marriage” is not one rule

For a male focal person, parallel cousins include a father’s brother’s daughter and a mother’s sister’s daughter; cross-cousins include a father’s sister’s daughter and a mother’s brother’s daughter. The categories describe different paths through a pedigree and can receive different social treatment.

Under ordinary outbred-pedigree assumptions, these are all first cousins with the same expected genetic relationship. Their social classification, however, can determine whether they are preferred spouses or prohibited partners. Marriage systems can organize exchanges between descent groups rather than merely avoid a numerical degree of relatedness. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8864366/)

An important logical consequence for TCE: **strict patrilineal clan exogamy conflicts with a preference for marrying a father’s brother’s daughter**, because she normally belongs to the same patrilineal group. Conversely, clan exogamy can coexist with endogamy at a larger boundary: marry outside one’s clan but inside an estate, religious community, or other social category.

## 2. Mechanisms: implementable causal processes

The rules below are proposed implementations. The cited studies support the underlying mechanisms; they do not estimate every coefficient needed by a game.

### 2.1 Durable assets create reasons to maintain durable groups

**Evidence.** Comparative research on African populations links cattle acquisition to transitions away from matriliny, although this is a historical comparative result, not an inevitable effect of owning livestock. Other cases connect matrilineal corporate organization to intensively cultivated rice land. Asset type and institutional history matter, not simply “agriculture versus foraging.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14667331/)

**Implementable rule.** Let persistent assets generate benefits from continuity: coordinated labor, defense of boundaries, storage, irrigation maintenance, or bargaining strength. A descent group becomes more attractive when those benefits exceed the costs of shared control.

Make rights asset-specific. Land title, cultivation rights, livestock ownership, harvest shares, and the right to appoint a manager should be separable. Upon a leader’s death, corporate property should not automatically enter that person’s private estate.

This also generates internal politics: someone who supplies labor may demand stronger use rights; an officeholder may attempt to privatize group property; absent members may retain claims that residents resent.

### 2.2 Residence emerges from bargaining under constraints

**Evidence.** Dyble and colleagues’ residence model shows that relatively equal influence by women and men can produce camps containing many unrelated adults even when individuals prefer living with kin. Lowes’s work along Africa’s matrilineal belt links broader kin support to women’s autonomy and to different patterns of cooperation within couples. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25977551/)

**Implementable rule.** At marriage, childbirth, parental illness, widowhood, or relocation, evaluate a small set of feasible residence options. Benefits include access to land, housing, employment, caregivers, protection, and inheritance prospects. Costs include crowding, moving, lost rights elsewhere, interpersonal conflict, and sanctions for violating custom.

Give the partners and relevant household members separate preferences and bargaining influence. Do not substitute a single household utility function for everyone’s interests.

An illustrative choice rule is:

\[
P(a)\propto \exp\!\left[
\frac{\text{resources}+\text{care}+\text{security}+\text{relationship value}
+\text{norm legitimacy}-\text{costs}-\text{expected sanctions}}{\tau}
\right].
\]

Here, \(a\) is a feasible action and \(\tau\) controls decision noise. This is an engineering formulation, not a measured anthropological equation.

### 2.3 Kinship supplies insurance—but only through actual people and resources

**Evidence.** Research comparing age-set and kin-based societies in East Africa finds that financial transfers propagate through different social connections: age peers in one system, extended relatives in the other. Kinship is therefore one infrastructure for mutual assistance, not the only one. [Topcat](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20211856)

**Implementable rule.** When a household experiences illness, crop loss, funeral expense, or a labor shortage, create requests addressed to eligible supporters. Rank claims using recognized relationship, urgency, prior assistance, proximity, obligation strength, and the recipient’s reputation.

Transfers must satisfy conservation constraints:

\[
T\_{ij}\leq \min(\text{unmet need}\_j,\ \text{available surplus}\_i,\ \text{delivery capacity}\_{ij}).
\]

Caregiving similarly consumes hours and can reduce paid or subsistence work. A grandmother who is alive but frail should not count as an unlimited childcare resource.

This produces an important simulation consequence without an extra rule: geographically concentrated relatives insure individual misfortunes better than a harvest failure affecting everyone simultaneously. Distant affinal or migrant connections can diversify that risk.

### 2.4 Marriage creates alliances while reproducing boundaries

**Evidence.** Kinship models demonstrate how cooperation with relatives and affines, together with competition for spouses, can generate recurring marriage relations between groups. Historical consanguinity research also emphasizes property retention and the familiarity of prospective spouses’ families as motivations for close-kin marriage. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8864366/)

**Implementable rule.** Construct a feasible spouse pool using age, existing partnerships, recognized kin prohibitions, group boundaries, travel access, and locally relevant status rules. Then score candidates by personal relationship, household interests, expected transfers, residence compatibility, and alliance value.

Separate four things:

**Permitted** is not **preferred**; preferred is not **available**; available is not **accepted**; and parental approval is not identical to the prospective spouses’ consent.

When the preferred cousin does not exist, allow delayed marriage, a different eligible partner, migration, or a negotiated exception. Do not make the population collapse merely because an idealized marriage cycle cannot be completed.

### 2.5 Kinship reallocates power within and between households

**Evidence.** Lowes finds that matrilineal women in the studied settings experience greater autonomy and less domestic violence, while some experimental circumstances produce lower contributions to a spousal public good. This is a redistribution of bargaining power and obligations—not evidence that matrilineal people are generally less cooperative. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30509)

**Implementable rule.** A person’s outside options should depend on whether natal relatives can offer residence, food, legal backing, or protection. Those options influence household bargaining, the ability to leave, and whether resources are directed toward a spouse, children, siblings, or another branch of kin.

Inheritance and marriage transfers then become strategic. Parents may transfer property before death, demand care in return for future access, or favor a child expected to remain nearby. Test such incentives rather than equating a descent label with a fixed sex hierarchy.

### 2.6 Lineages mobilize coalitions—and can restrain their leaders

**Evidence.** Moscona, Nunn, and Robinson find more conflict, especially retaliatory, prolonged, and large-scale conflict, among segmentary-lineage societies in sub-Saharan Africa. Their proposed mechanism is the obligation to assist relatives, allowing small disputes to expand. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16327)

**Implementable rule.** Represent nested lineage segments. A dispute initially recruits close associates; escalation activates wider common-descent claims. Each potential participant still considers costs, grievances, competing obligations, and the legitimacy of the call.

The same organization can support accountability. Tsai’s research on rural China distinguishes village-encompassing lineages from groups that fragment a village: the fit between group membership and the population receiving public goods changes the incentives of officials. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/accountability-without-democracy/lineages-and-local-governance/B985C450B4C3B8C369533FBDDF3F3A57)

Thus, do not give clans an unconditional “corruption bonus.” A lineage controlling a public office may favor its members when outsiders pay the cost, but discipline the same official when the lineage encompasses the affected community.

### 2.7 States, religions, markets, and associations compete with—and use—kinship

**Evidence.** In Ghana and Indonesia, pension policies reduced traditional co-residence practices and changed educational investment in children expected to support parents. In East Africa, the effects of transfers and pensions depended on whether financial solidarity ran through kin or age sets. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190098)

**Implementable rule.** Offer multiple providers of insurance, dispute settlement, credit, education, and protection. Households shift behavior when alternatives become accessible and credible, not when an era counter advances.

Kin groups can also administer taxation, sponsor schools, recruit officials, and transmit religious norms. Institutional development can reorganize kinship rather than eliminate it.

### 2.8 Change is negotiated, demographic, and path-dependent

**Evidence.** Shenk and colleagues find transitions both away from and toward matriliny, although the former are more common in their ethnographic record. They emphasize that the record disproportionately captures periods of colonial intervention and economic change. Bonate’s Indian Ocean comparison shows that colonial codification sometimes helped preserve matrilineal arrangements rather than simply destroying them. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6664135/)

**Implementable rule.** Change practices at consequential events: new household formation, succession disputes, conversion, migration, asset acquisition, legal reform, or a crisis in care provision. Let successful deviations become precedents.

Different domains should change at different speeds. A household might become neolocal while retaining matrilineal ancestral-land rights. A state may mandate new inheritance rules while local adjudicators continue enforcing older claims.

Fertility and mortality also change the options: fewer surviving siblings mean fewer aunts, uncles, and cousins; greater longevity increases overlap between generations. The demographic engine should generate this kin supply rather than the kinship module manufacturing relatives to satisfy a norm. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10756196/)

Two cautions are particularly important.

First, the Atlas’s **1,291 societies are not a random sample of humanity across time**. Its observations are heavily concentrated in recent ethnographic history; D-PLACE reports approximately two-thirds of focal observations in 1900–1950. Its distributions should constrain plausibility, not prescribe the prevalence of institutions in early agrarian worlds. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/contributions/EA)

Second, realized marriage frequencies depend on the supply of suitable relatives. A strong first-cousin preference and a 20% first-cousin marriage rate are compatible when most people lack an available, acceptable cousin. Preference strength should therefore be calibrated through simulated opportunities, not substituted directly for the observed rate.

### 3.2 Proposed TCE sensitivity ranges—not historical estimates

The literature does not supply universal numerical constants for kin obligation, parental control, or institutional change. The following are **experimental ranges chosen to span behavior**, with low empirical confidence.

| Parameter | Suggested initial sweep | Units and interpretation | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dominant residence-rule adherence | **50–95%** | Target share of feasible new unions following the locally dominant residence option | Calibration target, not an imposed per-marriage coin flip; residence evidence above establishes imperfect and variable patterns. |
| Partner bargaining balance | **0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1** | Relative weight assigned to one partner’s preferences, conditional on rights and resources | Design sweep inspired by residence-bargaining models; not an estimated historical distribution. |
| Extended-kin emergency allocation | **10–50%** | Fraction of genuinely disposable household surplus that can be committed during a crisis episode | Design assumption. Protect subsistence reserves and account for existing commitments. |
| Corporate ownership share | **0–100%** | Share of a specified asset class held by a continuing group rather than individuals | Institutional scenario axis; configure separately for land, livestock, buildings, and movable goods. |
| Effective enforcement | **10–90%** | Probability that an adjudicated claim is actually implemented, conditional on jurisdiction and available capacity | Design assumption. Separate legal entitlement from successful enforcement. |
| Outside-network influence | **0–50%** | Share of norm-learning encounters at household formation coming from non-natal networks | Design sweep for migration, markets, schooling, religious organizations, and peers. |
| Initial novel-practice probability | **0–5%** | Probability of a novel deviation at a consequential household decision | Exploration parameter only—not a measured cultural mutation rate. Adoption and persistence still depend on consequences. |

Do not independently randomize every parameter. Some combinations require special institutional explanations; others are internally contradictory. Author coherent starting arrangements, then let bargaining, demographic constraints, and disputes change their components.

**Evidence is especially thin for universal clan sizes, lineage-fission thresholds, annual descent-system transition hazards, and fixed percentages of income owed to relatives.** These should remain exposed sensitivity parameters rather than hidden “historically accurate” constants.

## 4. Variation across eras and regions

### 4.1 Historical variation is branching, not a ladder

| Setting | Evidence and variation | Implication for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mobile foragers** | Bilateral recognition is common in comparative samples, but residence and descent vary. Hill et al. find that most adult campmates are not close genetic relatives. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2999363/) | Kin preference must coexist with mobility, affinal ties, and non-kin cooperation. A camp is not necessarily one clan. |
| **Early farming: Gurgy, France** | Ancient DNA reconstructed extensive pedigrees, including one spanning **seven generations**, in a Neolithic community dated approximately **4850–4500 BCE**. The authors infer male-line continuity and female exogamy. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06350-8) | Patrilineal-looking continuity and intercommunity marriage are plausible early agrarian outcomes. |
| **Early farming: Çatalhöyük, Anatolia** | Analysis of **131 paleogenomes** from approximately **7100–5950 BCE** found predominantly maternal genetic connections within buildings and changing associations between burial and biological kin. Female-centered practices persisted for roughly a millennium. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40570118/) | Early farming must also permit durable female-line organization. Do not infer matriarchy or a complete legal inheritance system directly from burials. |
| **Pre-industrial societies** | Corporate kin groups could organize land and authority, while different property categories followed different rules. The Minangkabau–Ambon comparison illustrates substantial institutional variation even within Indonesia. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/law-and-society-review/article/abs/property-politics-and-conflict-ambon-and-minangkabau-compared/36A652E9E0059981EAF51538C87DADEB) | Avoid one generic “traditional extended family” template. |
| **Industrial and market transitions** | In rural Poland, greater market integration is associated with less kin-dense core networks, **not smaller networks**. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-14158-2) | Add non-kin connections and alter network structure rather than deleting family ties wholesale. |
| **Modern low-fertility, long-lived populations** | Demographic models imply fewer horizontal relatives and more overlap with older generations, with major regional variation. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10756196/) | Care burdens, inheritance timing, and cousin availability should change through births and deaths, without an era-specific “family weakening” switch. |

Archaeology provides unusually valuable checks against projecting modern ethnographic categories backward. But burial selection, missing individuals, mobility, and adoption complicate interpretation. **Genetic relationships are evidence about people; they are not direct transcripts of customary law.** The contrasting Gurgy and Çatalhöyük findings should expand the model’s possibilities, not become two deterministic technology presets. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06350-8)

### 4.2 Regional institutional contrasts

**Sub-Saharan Africa.** The matrilineal belt is a major counterexample to treating patriliny as the default result of farming. At the same time, East African age-set systems demonstrate that strong solidarity need not be organized primarily through descent. Historical work also links some African matrilineal arrangements to responses to slave-trade exposure; that interpretation is observational and should not be generalized into a universal origin story. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30509)

**South Asia.** India contains large regional differences in close-relative marriage, as the survey estimates above show. A simulation should distinguish the boundary within which marriages are sought from the particular kin relationships permitted inside it. “Endogamous society” is insufficient to specify a marriage algorithm. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-69151-7)

**Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean.** Matrilineal ancestral-property systems have coexisted with Islam in Minangkabau, parts of Kerala, and coastal northern Mozambique. Their histories include negotiated distinctions between ancestral property and other assets, religious reform, and colonial legal codification. Religion should therefore modify particular rules through institutions rather than instantly replace the entire kinship system. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/islam-and-matriliny-along-the-indian-ocean-rim-revisiting-the-old-paradox-by-comparing-the-minangkabau-kerala-and-coastal-northern-mozambique/B27395523E7A6579621D6CFA01F8E01E)

**East Asia.** Chinese lineage organization supplies an important alternative to a simple “strong state replaces strong kin” story. Tsai’s analysis shows why a lineage’s coverage of a village and its relationship to local officials matter for public-goods provision. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/accountability-without-democracy/lineages-and-local-governance/B985C450B4C3B8C369533FBDDF3F3A57)

**Indigenous North America.** In the Haudenosaunee Confederacy’s own description, clan mothers select and can remove male chiefs, while marriage approval includes avoiding same-clan unions. This separates descent, selection rights, officeholding, and accountability more clearly than a single “male-dominated/female-dominated” scale. [Haudenosaunee Confederacy](https://www.haudenosauneeconfederacy.com/government/)

**Europe.** Do not reserve nuclear households or less intensive kin organization for industrialization. Their chronology, prevalence, and causes are central points of dispute in the Church-and-kinship literature; the competing historical interpretations explicitly place relevant differences before industrialization. [Joe Henrich](https://henrich.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5811/files/henrich/files/sciencefull.pdf)

These examples are alternatives and constraints for authored building blocks—not fixed behavioral attributes of present-day regional populations.

## 5. Kinship intensity and institutions: what to use, and what is contested

### The Schulz–Henrich argument

Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp, and Henrich’s 2019 *Science* paper proposes that the medieval Western Church’s marriage-and-family policies weakened intensive kin institutions and contributed to later psychological differences. Their analyses span countries, European regions, and children of immigrants, examining **24 psychological and behavioral outcomes**.

Their Kinship Intensity Index combines five dimensions: cousin-marriage preference, polygyny, extended-family co-residence, lineage organization, and community organization. These are informative measurements, but they are not interchangeable mechanisms. [Joe Henrich](https://henrich.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5811/files/henrich/files/sciencefull.pdf)

Enke’s related research connects dense historical kinship with different systems for sustaining cooperation—such as communal obligations and external social sanctions—rather than simply with an absence of cooperation. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23499)

### Main qualifications

**Historical identification is contested.** Le Bris and Gay’s 2023 reanalysis introduces distance from proposed centers of family-system innovation. Their results challenge a uniquely Church-centered explanation of kinship geography while still finding an additional Church association with psychological outcomes, particularly in Western Europe. Their alternative is itself observational; it is not a decisive experimental refutation. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/doi?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0279864)

**An aggregate index can hide opposite institutional arrangements.** Patrilineal and matrilineal corporations can both score as intensive while distributing bargaining power very differently. A compound with several married siblings is not equivalent to separate households linked by strong remittances and inheritance claims. [Joe Henrich](https://henrich.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5811/files/henrich/files/sciencefull.pdf)

**The measurement dates and levels do not match perfectly.** Late ethnographic classifications are used to help explain much earlier historical processes and much later individual outcomes. Cultural ancestry, neighboring societies, migration selection, and institutional persistence complicate causal interpretation. The dating and representativeness of ethnographic records are independently significant concerns in comparative kinship research. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/contributions/EA)

**Strong kin organization is not uniformly hostile to public institutions.** Village-encompassing lineages can support accountability; age sets can replace kin in redistribution; and pensions can change residence incentives. These findings favor specific organizational mechanisms over a universal kinship-to-development coefficient. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/accountability-without-democracy/lineages-and-local-governance/B985C450B4C3B8C369533FBDDF3F3A57)

### Modeling conclusion

Do not implement:

> High kinship intensity → low generalized trust → corruption → slow development.

Instead, implement identifiable transactions. Does an official choose a relative over a better-qualified outsider? Who can punish that choice? Does a trader trust an unfamiliar person because a court can enforce repayment? Does a lineage finance a road benefiting everyone or only its own holdings?

Then measure kin preference, impersonal exchange, exclusion, and public-goods provision as outcomes.

## 6. Stylized facts and validation tests

A correct simulation need not reproduce every historical society in every run. It should reproduce these **conditional patterns** when the corresponding circumstances are present.

| Validation target | Expected pattern |
| --- | --- |
| **Kin preference without homogeneous camps** | Residence bargaining and mobility can produce mixed camps despite individual preferences for relatives; use the forager evidence above rather than requiring every camp to be a lineage. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21393537/) |
| **Separate descent and residence** | The model can sustain bilateral recognition with husband-side residence, or matrilineal property with changing household location. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2999363/) |
| **Marriage ideals are only partly realized** | Preferred cousin categories generate higher marriage probabilities when eligible candidates exist, but realized rates fall when demographic supply is constrained. |
| **Care and transfers follow institutions** | The same income injection reaches different recipients under kin-based and age-set-based solidarity. [Topcat](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20211856) |
| **Conflict can escalate through obligations** | Nested recruitment turns some small disputes into broader retaliatory conflicts, while mediation and competing ties can interrupt escalation. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16327) |
| **Markets change composition, not necessarily network size** | A network can become less kin-dense while retaining a similar number of close contacts. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-14158-2) |
| **Demography reshapes kin availability** | Lower fertility reduces cousins and siblings in later generations; longer survival changes overlap and care burdens. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10756196/) |
| **Institutions change unevenly** | Residence, inheritance, marriage practice, and political authority can move at different speeds, including partial reversals. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6664135/) |

For regression testing, add strict accounting invariants: inheritance conserves assets; corporate property survives a representative’s death; adoption changes recognized relationships without rewriting biological ancestry; one person cannot supply the same caregiving hour twice; and marriage eligibility queries agree with the applicable policy.

## 7. Recommended representation for 10,000–50,000 agents

### 7.1 Core state

| Component | Minimum useful state |
| --- | --- |
| **Person** | Biological parent IDs; recognized parent/caregiver relationships; partnerships; household ID; memberships; property claims; sparse relationship memory. |
| **Household** | Residents and absent dependents; consumption pool; labor and care capacity; shared budget; dwelling references. |
| **Descent group** | Membership rule; claimed ancestor; parent/subgroup links; corporate assets; office-selection rules; representatives; disputes. |
| **Kinship policy** | Descent rules; residence preferences; marriage exclusions/preferences; inheritance by asset type; support obligations; recognition and exception procedures. |
| **Institutional jurisdiction** | Which organization adjudicates a claim, whose rules it recognizes, and how much enforcement capacity it has. |
| **Relationship edge** | Affection, grievance, trust, obligations, prior transfers, contact recency, and relevant social-kin classification. |

Maintain **actual genealogy**, **recognized genealogy**, and **active relationships** separately. Someone can be a biological cousin, unknown as such, and a distrusted neighbor; another person can be an adopted sibling with strong obligations and no close biological connection.

### 7.2 Event-driven computation

Daily computation should primarily concern co-resident consumption, care, and concrete interactions. Recompute wider relationships when relevant: birth, adoption, marriage proposal, migration, inheritance, office succession, dispute, or mobilization.

Use ancestry-path queries and caches rather than an all-pairs kinship matrix. An initial cache depth of **four to six generations** is an engineering choice for common queries, not a limit on recognized ancestry. Retain compact older pedigree records and group ancestry claims for deeper legal or historical queries.

For scale, **50,000 people × 64 directed active relationship records = 3.2 million records**. At an illustrative 32 bytes per record, that is approximately **102 MB before indexing and allocator overhead**. This is a storage calculation, not a performance benchmark. It makes sparse personal networks a plausible starting architecture.

Do not delete relationships merely because an active-edge cache is full. Preserve important obligations in institutional or event records, and allow dormant ties to reactivate.

### 7.3 Founder initialization matters

Starting every adult as unrelated and parentless distorts the first generations. Seed a modest prior pedigree, recognized kin groups, and histories of marriage and residence. Where the world includes migration, incoming agents should bring some external kin connections.

Conversely, after centuries in a small population, sharing an ancient ancestor must not automatically make everyone a close social relative. Pedigree overlap, remembered kinship, and corporate membership are different quantities.

### 7.4 What to simplify first

For a first implementation, prioritize descent membership, household residence, marriage eligibility, asset-specific succession, emergency assistance, and political obligations. Add detailed terminology, ritual kinship, and complex affiliation changes later.

It is reasonable to derive human-readable labels such as “matrilineal” from the operative rules. It is less reasonable to make that label impose an entire package of attitudes and behaviors.

Visible life should emerge from the same transactions: care visits, shared harvest work, relocation after marriage, relatives arriving after a death, wedding delegations, lineage meetings, and disputes over rooms or fields. Architecture can then reflect co-residence and property rights rather than serving as a cosmetic culture preset.

## 8. Source and dataset priorities

The citations above provide the research basis. For implementation, these are the most useful reusable source families:

| Source family | Best use | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **D-PLACE / Ethnographic Atlas**: 1,291 societies; **Standard Cross-Cultural Sample**: 186 societies | Discover combinations of descent, residence, marriage, property, subsistence, and political organization. Preserve focal dates and original codes. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/societysets) | Society samples are not population weights; shared history and spatial dependence remain. |
| **Shenk et al. (2019), transition coding and supplements** | Identify documented mechanisms and distinguish strong evidence from speculative claims about change. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6664135/) | Unequal observation periods prevent direct conversion into transition hazards. |
| **DHS/NFHS-based studies** | Household composition, marriage relationships, residence, autonomy, care-related outcomes, and demographic constraints. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-69151-7) | Survey definitions and exclusions matter; household rosters omit much non-resident support. |
| **Rivollat et al. (2023) and Yüncü et al. (2025)** | Early farming pedigrees, mobility, and checks against assuming one prehistoric family system. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06350-8) | Burial samples and biological relatedness do not directly identify all social institutions. |
| **Schulz et al. replication materials; le Bris and Gay reanalysis** | Reproduce and stress-test the kinship-intensity/institutions argument. [Dryad](https://datadryad.org/stash/dataset/doi%3A10.5061/dryad.2rbnzs7hs) | Alternative specifications and historical proxies materially affect interpretation. |
| **Bau (2021); Moscona and Seck (2024); Moscona, Nunn, and Robinson (2020)** | More specific tests of residence incentives, financial networks, and conflict mobilization. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190098) | Stronger designs remain local to particular institutions and populations. |

For each authored rule bundle, retain a short provenance record: **observed case, observation date, mechanism inferred, quantitative target, uncertainty, and deliberate simplifications**. This will be more useful than attaching a single confidence score to an entire “culture.”

**The central design choice is to make kinship consequential through enforceable relationships and finite resources.** When relatives provide land, care, protection, credit, and political backing—and also make claims on those resources—family structures can persist, fragment, or change for reasons the player can see. TCE then needs neither a scripted family-history sequence nor a universal “traditional kinship” modifier.
