# Religion and ritual as social institutions: a simulation-ready report for TCE

## Executive recommendation

**TCE should model religion as a combination of cultural transmission, ritual practice, and durable organizations—not as a “faith” resource or a civilization-wide bonus.** A relatively small institutional layer would connect naturally to systems you already need: households, reputation, collective action, land ownership, employment, redistribution, and political legitimacy.

The essential distinction is between **what a person believes, what they practice, and which organizations they belong to**. These need not coincide. In Pew’s 2024 research, for example, 59% of religiously unaffiliated Japanese adults reported making offerings of food, water, or drinks to ancestors during the preceding year. An affiliation-only model would classify much of this religious life out of existence. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/07/01/rituals-honoring-deceased-ancestors-vary-widely-in-east-and-southeast-asia/)

For an early-agrarian start, I recommend inherited household traditions, occasional communal rituals, and part-time specialists as the initial configuration. Permanent clergy, temples, endowed estates, missionary networks, and state religions should become possible through ordinary organizational processes—not compulsory technological stages.

The central design principle is:

> **Religious institutions should change behavior through relationships, obligations, perceived authority, and actual services. Their economic effects should pass through real labor, goods, property, and contracts.**

# 1. Mechanisms: evidence translated into implementable rules

## 1.1 Origins: beliefs and practices can precede formal religious organizations

Comparative research on 33 hunter-gatherer societies reconstructed animism as an early feature, with other elements—such as ancestor veneration, shamanism, and high gods—having different evolutionary histories. This does **not** establish a universal historical sequence: the reconstruction depends on ethnographic coding and phylogenetic assumptions, and contemporary or recently documented foragers are not unchanged prehistoric populations. It does show why “organized monotheism” is an inappropriate starting definition of religion. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12110-016-9260-0)

**TCE rule:** allow beliefs and rituals to exist without temples, scriptures, professional clergy, or a creator god. A household can maintain obligations to ancestors; a settlement can share a seasonal ceremony; an individual can acquire a reputation as a diviner or ritual expert.

Represent the emergence of a specialist as an ordinary occupational process:

\[
\text{recognition} \rightarrow \text{requests for services}
\rightarrow \text{gifts or support} \rightarrow \text{specialization}.
\]

Permanent specialization becomes feasible when recurring support covers the person’s subsistence and opportunity cost. Support might come from many households, a lineage, or one powerful patron. A minimum settlement population should not be required.

Belief transmission should depend partly on credible exemplars. Henrich’s **credibility-enhancing displays**, or CREDs, theory proposes that learners place more weight on teachings when instructors behave as though those teachings genuinely matter. This is a cultural-learning mechanism, not proof that every expensive ritual increases belief. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1090513809000245)

**Implementation:** caregivers, respected elders, successful specialists, and trusted peers transmit practices through repeated interaction. A teacher who consistently follows an inconvenient obligation is more credible than one whose conduct contradicts their teaching. Agents should also have intrinsic attachments and perceived supernatural obligations; they should not all be cynical calculators of material benefits.

## 1.2 “Big gods”: distinguish supernatural monitoring from the origin of states

The strongest behavioral version of the hypothesis is that belief in knowledgeable, punitive supernatural agents can make some people more willing to behave fairly beyond their immediate social circle.

Purzycki and colleagues studied 591 participants in eight communities, using allocation tasks to examine fairness toward distant coreligionists. A subsequent expanded study covered 2,228 participants across 15 societies; it incorporated the earlier sample rather than constituting a wholly independent replication. Its results support conditional relationships between moralizing-god beliefs and impartiality toward distant coreligionists—not a universal increase in generosity toward everybody. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26863190/)

The macrohistorical claim—**moralizing gods caused large complex societies**—is much more contested. The influential 2019 Seshat article was retracted in 2021 following problems involving missing-data treatment. A revised analysis, published online in 2022, continued to argue that major increases in social complexity generally preceded moralizing gods. Separately, research on 96 Austronesian cultures distinguished broad supernatural punishment, which its model placed before political complexity, from moralizing high gods, which it did not. These are different hypotheses and different coding choices. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03656-3)

**TCE rule:** represent at least three separate attributes:

| Attribute | Behavioral interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Supernatural monitoring | Does the agent believe otherwise-unobserved behavior can be detected? |
| Moral domain | Which acts attract supernatural concern: theft, oath-breaking, ritual neglect, sexual conduct, obligations to kin? |
| Scope of obligation | To whom does the relevant duty apply: household, lineage, settlement, coreligionists, strangers, everyone? |

Apply these beliefs to decisions within the relevant domain. Do not grant every believer the same cooperation multiplier.

**Do not implement:** a population threshold that automatically creates moralizing gods; a requirement for religion before urbanization; or an automatic progression toward universal morality.

## 1.3 Ritual: coordination, repetition, emotional salience, and public commitment

Ritual can affect social relations through several separable channels: repeated interaction, synchronized action, shared emotional experiences, visible participation, and common knowledge about who belongs.

A meta-analysis of interpersonal synchrony found a moderate average effect on prosocial behavior: **Hedges’ \(g=0.45\)** across 35 behavioral studies, with considerable heterogeneity. This is a standardized experimental effect—not “45% more cooperation,” and certainly not a permanent productivity increase. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137339/)

Cross-cultural research covering **645 rituals in 74 cultures** found an association between ritual frequency and emotional arousal: frequent practices tended to be less intense, while highly arousing rituals tended to be rarer. These are tendencies, not two mutually exclusive religious types. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1090513810001029)

**TCE rule:** a ritual event should have a schedule, audience, participants, duration, resource requirements, degree of synchrony, emotional intensity, and public visibility.

Its effects should be limited and conditional:

* Strengthen some relationships among participants or witnesses.
* Make membership and commitments more visible.
* Reinforce practices through repetition.
* Consume actual time, food, materials, or wealth.

A successful festival might improve local coordination while interrupting production and consuming grain. A spectacular ceremony can fail to create durable loyalty if later interactions involve exploitation or betrayal.

Avoid all-purpose “ritual happiness.” A funeral, initiation, healing ceremony, harvest feast, and royal coronation should have different audiences, meanings, and consequences.

## 1.4 Costly signaling: informative commitment, not “more suffering is better”

**Costly signaling and CREDs are related but distinct.** A signal tells observers something about the performer’s commitment or reliability. A CRED makes an observer more confident that a teacher genuinely believes what they teach.

Power’s field research in two South Indian villages found that different religious activities communicated different reputational qualities. Regular worship and costly festival participation did not simply produce identical “prosociality” effects. A study of 83 nineteenth-century American communes also connected costly requirements with organizational longevity, but the evidence is observational and does not justify treating expense itself as a cause of success. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513816300472)

Iannaccone’s strict-church model offers an additional mechanism: demanding rules can discourage free-riding and improve the value of participation for committed members. The same demands can exclude potential recruits or impose excessive burdens. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/230409)

**TCE rule:** evaluate a signal by its **cost to this individual**, observability, voluntariness, and cultural interpretation.

A rich donor’s contribution may be less revealing than a poor household’s smaller sacrifice. Forced attendance is weak evidence of sincere commitment. Costly participation can increase trust among insiders while reducing recruitment.

Consequently, group success should be **non-monotonic in strictness**. High-cost groups can be cohesive without being large, prosperous, tolerant, or demographically sustainable.

## 1.5 Temples and clergy: economic organizations with heterogeneous functions

Historical religious institutions were not one economic type.

| Setting | Documented institutional activity | Implication for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Old Babylonian Mesopotamia | Temple lending included both distress-related and business transactions; terms and purposes varied. | Lending is a contract system, not automatically charity. [Iris](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1729911) |
| First-millennium Babylonian temples | Institutions such as Sippar’s Ebabbar had differentiated administration, personnel, and economic operations. | Separate clergy, managers, craftspeople, laborers, tenants, and dependants. [NINO Leiden](https://www.nino-leiden.nl/publication/the-neo-babylonian-ebabbar-temple-at-sippar) |
| Medieval South India | Temple endowments connected religious purposes with agrarian resources and local economic organization. | Land grants and recurring claims can support a durable institutional economy. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abs/economic-function-of-a-medieval-south-indian-temple/E2F758606AC539F385D65797F9A88025) |
| Buddhist monastic traditions in South Asia | Schopen documents rules for lending, preserving endowed capital, and recording loan agreements. | Religious renunciation does not imply an institution has no financial assets. The source includes normative rules, not just observed transactions. [NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-EPT/gre.htm) |
| Ottoman cash waqfs | Endowed capital generated revenue for specified purposes; Bursa records document recurring returns and long-lived funds. | Distinguish protected capital from spendable income and respect beneficiary restrictions. [Muslim Heritage](https://muslimheritage.com/ottoman-cash-waqfs-revisited-bursa/) |
| Bali | Water-temple networks coordinated irrigation and cropping decisions in an ecological system involving water and pests. | Benefits should emerge from better scheduling and resource coordination, not a sacred-building yield bonus. [AnthroSource](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/aa.1993.95.1.02a00050) |

**TCE rule:** religious organizations use the same economic machinery as other corporations, households, and governments.

Record separately:

**Property rights.** Ownership, tenancy, usufruct, rent claims, and tax privileges are not interchangeable.

**Revenue.** Delivered offerings, endowed rents, compulsory levies, service payments, and loan income are different flows.

**Expenditure.** Staff support, ceremonies, maintenance, aid, construction, and lending compete for resources.

**Beneficiaries.** An institution can support members, a particular lineage, travelers, the poor, officeholders, or a founder’s descendants. Redistribution is not necessarily universal.

Waqf scholarship illustrates both the durability of founder-directed commitments and debates about their adaptability. The broader claim that such institutions necessarily obstructed economic development is contested and should not become an engine rule. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/law-and-society-review/article/abs/provision-of-public-goods-under-islamic-law-origins-impact-and-limitations-of-the-waqf-system/6653A7019DB49C8AE0542E017FDCEF54)

Long-lived religious assets can also become politically contested. Research on the English dissolution of monasteries links the transfer of former monastic property to subsequent economic changes. This makes confiscation and changes in property rights relevant mechanisms—not evidence for a universal productivity penalty on religious land. [Kellogg School of Management](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2021/the-long-run-impact-of-the-dissolution-of-the/)

## 1.6 Conversion and competition: networks, households, institutions, and demographic turnover

Conversion should not be modeled as exposure to a contagious label alone.

In a study of **70 Austronesian societies**, conversion to Christianity typically took less than 30 years. Smaller populations and the presence of political leadership predicted faster conversion; inequality did not reliably predict conversion time. This is a specific historical setting involving missionary contact and political change, not an annual global conversion constant. [The Australian National University](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/christianity-spread-faster-in-small-politically-structured-societ/)

**TCE rule:** separate several processes that can produce superficially similar population changes:

| Process | Agent-level event |
| --- | --- |
| Intergenerational transmission | Children acquire practices and affiliations through caregivers and surrounding households. |
| Network adoption | Repeated exposure through trusted contacts makes a practice or organization credible and accessible. |
| Elite adoption | A patron changes sponsorship, access, incentives, or public expectations. |
| Institutional recruitment | An organization offers ceremonies, education, assistance, identity, or opportunities to participate. |
| Coercive conformity | Public behavior changes under sanctions, potentially without a corresponding private belief change. |
| Migration and demographic turnover | The population composition changes without anyone converting. |

Agents should be able to add compatible practices before changing affiliation. A household can continue an ancestor rite after joining a new organization; a declared convert can remain uncertain about its teachings.

Competition should affect actual service quality, recruitment effort, rules, patronage, and exit opportunities. Do not hard-code “more religious competition means more religion”: influential empirical claims about pluralism and participation were challenged over the mathematical relationship between the measures used. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/000312240206700203)

## 1.7 Schisms: organizational division, not random doctrine mutation

Research on American Protestant denominations from 1890 to 1990 treats schism as an organizational process. Structural changes and denominational consolidation mattered; purely theological disagreement was not an adequate explanation. A companion study examined mergers, emphasizing organizational identity, centralization, and changing institutional circumstances. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111%2Fj.1468-5906.2004.00226.x?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Succession is another plausible trigger. Qualitative research on three African Initiated Churches in Zimbabwe connects succession disputes with family relationships, inheritance claims, and economic interests. Its regional and qualitative scope should be preserved. [Journals](https://journals.co.za/doi/10.25159/2412-4265/15651?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**TCE rule:** a viable schism requires more than disagreement:

\[
\text{persistent dispute}
+\text{organized following}
+\text{alternative leadership}
+\text{means to operate}.
\]

Trigger evaluation after succession, doctrinal reform, centralization, scandal, disputed property allocation, or intervention by rulers. Outcomes can include compromise, local autonomy, expulsion, organizational division, or merger.

Keep **organizational genealogy** separate from **doctrinal genealogy**. Two institutions can split while retaining almost identical beliefs; one institution can tolerate substantial internal variation.

## 1.8 Political legitimacy: reciprocal support and constraints

Religious authority can contribute to political legitimacy by making a ruler’s office appear rightful, obligatory, or cosmologically significant. Political authorities can reciprocate through patronage, privileges, appointments, enforcement, or land. Comparative work documents associations between divine legitimation and political institutions, but does not establish a portable numerical coefficient for “religion’s effect on obedience.” [LUSEM](https://www.lusem.lu.se/gunes-gokmen/publication/9b4a9d59-95e4-4f87-b0bd-5cb64d7d1e87)

Religion can also reinforce domination. Research on 93 Austronesian cultures found ritual human sacrifice more prevalent in highly stratified societies and used phylogenetic models to investigate the relationship. This is evidence against treating religious cohesion as synonymous with social welfare; it is not a universal causal law about religion or hierarchy. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17159)

**TCE rule:** legitimacy should be audience-dependent and conditional on trusted intermediaries.

An endorsement matters to a person when they respect the endorsing institution and accept its claim to authority. It can lose force when a ruler violates obligations that the same tradition treats as binding. Religious leaders can therefore support rulers, constrain them, or lend credibility to opposition.

Do not equate obedience under threat with legitimacy, or institutional endorsement with unanimous believer support.

# 2. Parameters: empirical anchors versus engineering priors

## 2.1 Empirical anchors

The following are **observations and calibration targets**, not universal simulation coefficients.

**Confidence:** **High-context** means useful descriptive evidence for the stated population or archive; **Moderate** indicates restricted samples, observational designs, or heterogeneous effects; **Low-transfer** means it should not be generalized into a cross-civilizational causal parameter. These labels are judgments about use, not statistical confidence intervals.

| Quantity | Value, units, and scope | Appropriate use | Confidence and source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Synchrony effect on prosocial behavior | **Hedges’ \(g=0.45\); 95% CI 0.30–0.60**, 35 behavioral studies | Plausibility of a temporary behavioral effect; not a percentage production bonus | Moderate; heterogeneous experiments and potential bias. Rennung & Göritz 2016. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137339/) |
| Frequency–arousal evidence | **645 rituals, 74 cultures**; generally inverse association | Validate the joint distribution of ritual frequency and intensity; no universal slope established | Moderate, observational. Atkinson & Whitehouse 2011. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1090513810001029) |
| Historical conversion timescale | **Typically <30 years**, 70 Austronesian societies | A scenario-level rapid-conversion target under missionary contact and political/network change | Moderate; low transfer to other settings. Watts et al. 2018. [The Australian National University](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/christianity-spread-faster-in-small-politically-structured-societ/) |
| Retention of childhood affiliation | **91%** of adults aged 18–54 retained their broad childhood category; approximately **99% Hindu, 99% Muslim, 83% Christian, 78% Buddhist, 93% unaffiliated** within the respective upbringing groups | Modern cohort-level calibration | High-context; retrospective, broad-category measure, **not annual switching rates** and not within-Christian denominational turnover. Pew 2025. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/06/26/globally-1-in-10-adults-under-55-have-left-their-childhood-religion/) |
| Practice without affiliation | **59%** of unaffiliated Japanese adults reported ancestor offerings in the preceding **12 months** | Validate separation of membership from ritual behavior | High-context for this survey, not all societies. Pew 2024. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/07/01/rituals-honoring-deceased-ancestors-vary-widely-in-east-and-southeast-asia/) |
| Endowed-fund nominal returns | Bursa cash-waqf records for **1667–1805** report returns generally around **9–12% of capital per year** | A regional financial-contract calibration; preserve risks and contract forms | Moderate archival evidence; not a universal real return or modern bank rate. Çizakça. [Muslim Heritage](https://muslimheritage.com/ottoman-cash-waqfs-revisited-bursa/) |
| Institutional longevity | **At least about 20%** of studied Bursa cash waqfs survived **over 100 years** | Long-lived institutions should be possible, but not guaranteed | Moderate; archival survival and observation-window limitations. Çizakça. [Muslim Heritage](https://muslimheritage.com/ottoman-cash-waqfs-revisited-bursa/) |
| Ritual coercion and stratification | Human sacrifice recorded in **5/20 egalitarian**, **17/46 moderately stratified**, and **18/27 highly stratified** Austronesian cultures: approximately **25%, 37%, 67%** | A regional joint-pattern test, not a policy-effect estimate | Moderate description; low-transfer causality. Watts et al. 2016 and the authors’ university release. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17159) |

Three quantities remain especially weakly constrained across history: **clergy-to-population ratios, annual conversion hazards, and annual schism hazards**. For these, deriving activity from budgets and networks is more defensible than presenting an invented universal historical average.

## 2.2 Suggested TCE starting priors

These are **engineering choices to explore in sensitivity analysis**, not estimates extracted from the studies above.

| Parameter | Initial test range | Interpretation and guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private-belief learning rate | **0.001–0.03 per meaningful monthly update** | Fractional movement toward trusted observed teaching; no update without relevant experience |
| Ordinary affiliation-change hazard | **\(10^{-4}\)–\(10^{-2}\) per exposed adult-year**, before contextual modifiers | Background openness only; household decisions, intense recruitment, and political shocks can create much faster change |
| Immediate shared-ritual tie reinforcement | **0–0.03** on a normalized relationship scale per event | Include zero-effect runs; cap cumulative contribution from ritual alone |
| Decay half-life of temporary ritual reinforcement | **30–180 days** | Repeated interaction can maintain a relationship; autobiographical memories need not decay on this schedule |
| Routine communal event duration | **0.5–4 hours per event** | Separate from household practices, travel, preparation, and rare multi-day festivals |
| Routine communal event frequency | **1–52 events per year** | An exploration range, not a claim about historical universality or a mandatory seven-day week |
| Formation reserve for a staffed institution | **6–24 months of expected operating expenditure** | Can be replaced by credible continuing patronage; household shrines need no such reserve |
| Conditional organizational split hazard | **0.01–0.05 per institution-year after a qualifying dispute and viable alternative organization** | Zero without enabling conditions; deliberately provisional |

**Do not convert \(g=0.45\) into the relationship increment above.** The experimental statistic does not identify that mapping. Fit the mapping against observable outcomes and test whether the mechanism is necessary at all.

Likewise, derive the number of supported specialists from:

\[
\text{affordable staffing}
=
\frac{\text{resources allocated to staff}}
{\text{local cost of maintaining one full-time worker}},
\]

then constrain it by service demand, skills, and recruitment. This is preferable to “one priest per thousand residents.”

# 3. Variation across eras and regions

These should be **possible institutional configurations**, not fixed historical stages.

| Context | Historically relevant variation | TCE treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Foraging societies** | Local spirits, ancestors, ritual experts, and communal ceremonies can exist without permanent estates or centralized priesthoods. Comparative reconstructions show different histories for different religious traits. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12110-016-9260-0) | Household and kin-based transmission; portable ritual objects; part-time specialists; shared ceremonies without mandatory buildings |
| **Early farming and settlement aggregation** | Archaeology at Göbekli Tepe connects large-scale ritual activity and feasting with the transition toward Neolithic communities. It does not establish that a mature farming economy or state had to precede monumentality—or that religion alone caused agriculture. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/role-of-cult-and-feasting-in-the-emergence-of-neolithic-communities-new-evidence-from-gobekli-tepe-southeastern-turkey/A1AA4FB20657599F859860D94CCD090E) | Seasonal gatherings and patron-supported construction can precede permanent bureaucratic institutions; storage and settlement stability make additional organizational forms feasible |
| **Pre-industrial agrarian states** | Mesopotamian temple households, South Indian endowments, Buddhist monasteries, and Islamic waqfs had distinct legal and economic arrangements. “The temple economy” is not a single universal system. [NINO Leiden](https://www.nino-leiden.nl/publication/the-neo-babylonian-ebabbar-temple-at-sippar) | Different charters, property regimes, revenue sources, succession rules, and relationships with rulers |
| **Industrializing societies** | Industrial-era religious change did not move uniformly toward declining participation. Research on US church affiliation during 1850–1930 examines substantial organizational expansion, while cross-national cohort research finds different patterns across the United States, Netherlands, and Japan. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0049089X9290001W?utm_source=chatgpt.com) | Urban migration, occupational change, literacy, service substitutes, and new associations alter transmission and recruitment; do not apply automatic technological secularization |
| **Modern societies** | Cohort replacement contributes to disaffiliation in cases such as Switzerland; substantial revivals also occur, as in post-Soviet Georgia. Identity, attendance, and personal practice can change differently. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/4/493?utm_source=chatgpt.com) | Track distinct dimensions and allow both decline and resurgence, conditioned on institutions and social experience |

**Regional layering matters as much as era.** Pew’s 2010 research across 19 sub-Saharan African countries found that Christian and Muslim identification could coexist with beliefs and practices involving ancestors or other spiritual forces. Its 2014 Latin American research likewise documented combinations of Christian affiliation with indigenous and Afro-descended traditions. These are dated regional observations, not timeless continental traits. [Pew Charitable Trusts](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2010/04/15/tolerance-and-tension-islam-and-christianity-in-subsaharan-africa?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For TCE, the implication is to author **compatibility and exclusivity rules**, rather than forcing every population into mutually exclusive religious blocs from the beginning. Exclusive membership should itself be a variable institutional claim.

# 5. Modeling recommendation for individual agents and institutions

## 5.1 A compact representation

The following is a proposed TCE architecture, not an empirical claim that religion naturally decomposes into exactly these fields.

| Entity | Minimum useful state |
| --- | --- |
| **Individual** | A small set of belief confidences; ritual habits; identity salience; memberships; trusted exemplars; obligations; relevant memories |
| **Tradition or local doctrinal variant** | Supernatural or cosmological claims; moral concerns and their scope; ritual repertoire; compatibility/exclusivity rules; authority claims |
| **Local institution** | Charter; members and adherents; officials; succession rules; sites; assets; contracts; recurring commitments; service capacity |
| **Ritual event** | Occasion; organizer; eligible participants; witnesses; duration; capacity; required goods; emotional and social characteristics |
| **Translocal relationship** | Recognition, pilgrimage, teaching, appointment, financial support, or organizational subordination |

Keep three identities separate: the **tradition**, the **organization**, and the **building**. A tradition can survive the loss of its temple; a building can change owners; an organization can change teachings.

A sacred site should be a place with claims and meanings attached to it, not necessarily a constructed service building.

## 5.2 Membership change: event-driven, network-mediated, and reversible

For an agent \(i\) considering organization \(g\), an initial design could use:

\[
\lambda\_{ig}
=
a\_{ig}\lambda\_0
\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{x}\_{ig}\right),
\]

where \(a\_{ig}\) represents meaningful access or exposure, and \(\mathbf{x}\_{ig}\) includes trusted contacts, doctrinal fit, relevant experiences, services, personal commitments, participation costs, and sanctions.

For mutually exclusive alternatives, use competing hazards:

\[
P\_i(\text{any change during }\Delta t)
=
1-\exp\left[-\Delta t\sum\_g\lambda\_{ig}\right].
\]

Conditional on a change, choose the destination proportionally to its hazard. This prevents several independent probability checks from assigning contradictory affiliations in the same update.

However, **joining an organization, adding a practice, and revising a belief require different update processes**. Compatible practice adoption should not necessarily remove an existing membership. Coercive compliance should primarily alter visible behavior until other learning processes operate.

Update when something meaningful happens: a marriage, migration, bereavement, apprenticeship, repeated contact, receipt of aid, a scandal, an institutional dispute, or an influential person’s change of affiliation. Avoid reconsidering every agent’s whole worldview every day.

## 5.3 Institutional accounting

For each stored resource, use actual flows:

\[
S\_{t+1}
=
S\_t+\text{delivered receipts}
-\text{delivered expenditures}
-\text{losses}.
\]

Maintain separate accounts for assets, receivables, liabilities, and restricted funds.

A loan disbursement reduces available stock and creates a claim. Principal repayment restores the stock and extinguishes that claim; it is **not profit**. An endowed field remains an asset while producing a stream of rent or output. A promised donation cannot feed staff until it is delivered.

This lets familiar dynamics emerge without bespoke bonuses: wealthy institutions can lend, provision ceremonies, support dependants, survive poor harvests, or become targets of confiscation. Poor institutions can lose staff, reduce services, merge, or close.

## 5.4 Legitimacy and cooperation should use existing systems

Religious endorsement should enter the same decision system as reputation, law, patronage, kin obligations, and fear—not multiply all of them.

For example, an agent’s willingness to obey an official can depend on how much they trust the endorsing institution and whether they think the official is fulfilling sacred duties. A dispute over an official’s conduct can therefore divide supporters rather than shifting an entire settlement’s “religious loyalty” meter.

Similarly, use existing relationship memory for ritual effects. Avoid separately awarding “same religion trust,” “ritual trust,” “community cohesion,” and “institutional legitimacy” for the same underlying relationship without testing for double-counting.

Equivalent social mechanisms should also be available to secular associations. This makes it possible to test what supernatural beliefs add beyond repeated contact, assistance, and shared identity.

## 5.5 Performance and scope for 10k–50k people

A practical implementation would schedule physical participation daily while evaluating most cultural changes less often.

| Process | Suggested execution pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Attendance, travel, preparation, offerings | Ordinary scheduled activities |
| Immediate witnessed events and relationship changes | Event-driven |
| Belief and affiliation reconsideration | Event-driven, with staggered monthly opportunities |
| Staffing and operating budgets | Monthly or seasonal |
| Endowments, succession, reform, division | Relevant events plus periodic institutional review |

Do not construct a complete social graph among everyone attending a large ritual. Sample interactions and update existing ties, while recording participation in the shared event separately. A thousand attendees should not require nearly half a million new pairwise relationships.

For **v1**, I would include household transmission, ritual calendars, specialist roles, membership networks, real offerings, and institutional ownership. Add detailed doctrine disputes, missionary strategies, endowed-credit contracts, and translocal hierarchies later.

To support endless histories, institutions must be able to inherit assets without becoming immortal. Death, succession failure, conquest, declining support, merger, confiscation, and loss of economic purpose should all remain possible.

## 6.2 Claims to keep explicitly uncertain

**Historical causal ordering.** The relationship between moralizing religion and large-scale political organization remains disputed. Competing definitions of supernatural punishment, dating uncertainty, and missing-data handling change conclusions. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03656-3)

**Experimental transportability.** Laboratory synchrony, allocation games, and contemporary field studies establish plausible mechanisms, not permanent effects of ancient ceremonies on city-level output. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137339/)

**Institutional prescriptions versus behavior.** A rule permitting or restricting lending does not measure how frequently institutions actually lent, to whom, or with what losses. Schopen’s analysis is especially useful because it makes the complexity of textual and contractual evidence visible. [NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-EPT/gre.htm)

**Organizational success versus social benefit.** A durable institution may benefit its members while excluding others, accumulating rents, or supporting coercion. Survival should not be the engine’s definition of welfare. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1069397103037002003)

**Cross-cultural dependence.** Related societies are not independent experimental observations. Language ancestry, migration, conquest, and diffusion can generate apparent relationships between religious and political traits. The Austronesian studies explicitly attempt to address parts of this problem through phylogenetic methods. [Open Research Repository](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/05a18f79-059f-4033-b530-cb1ba1593eab)

## 6.3 Recommended validation strategy

Calibrate several outputs jointly: household transmission, affiliation turnover, participation, relationship clustering, institutional finances, organization size, survival, and political alignment.

Then run mechanism-removal experiments: remove supernatural monitoring, remove ritual reinforcement, remove religious economic privileges, or replace an institution with a secular association providing the same services. These comparisons reveal whether religion is doing explanatory work or merely duplicating effects already produced by kinship, wealth, and repeated contact.

Hold out entire regions or related cultural groups where feasible rather than randomly splitting closely related observations. Preserve alternative historical codings instead of averaging them into false precision.

**Bottom line:** the most defensible TCE model is neither “religion makes civilization possible” nor “religion is decorative culture.” It is a system of inherited meanings, observable commitments, social networks, and organizations that own resources and bargain over authority. Its consequences should emerge from those mechanisms—and can include solidarity, care, coordination, exclusion, extraction, reform, and revolt.
