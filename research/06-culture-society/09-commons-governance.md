# Governing the commons: community institutions for TCE

## Central finding

**TCE should model commons as resource-specific institutions—not as the absence of property, and not as an early stage that inevitably disappears when a state emerges.** Communities have governed shared resources through membership rules, harvesting rights, work obligations, monitoring, sanctions, and negotiated relations with neighboring communities and governments. The relevant contrast is often **managed common property versus open access**, rather than communal versus private ownership. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

A useful starting distinction is between a resource’s physical characteristics and its ownership. A common-pool resource is difficult to exclude people from, while one person’s use reduces what remains for others. That resource can nevertheless be governed communally, privately, by a state, or through overlapping arrangements. Rights to **enter, withdraw resources, change management rules, exclude others, and transfer rights** need not belong to the same actor. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

For TCE, this suggests a village with individually cultivated fields, shared grazing, household-specific water turns, and collectively maintained canals—not one village-wide “communal ownership” switch.

The evidence is strongest on the existence and operating mechanisms of such arrangements. It is substantially weaker on universal numerical probabilities of their emergence, durability, or collapse. The parameter recommendations below therefore distinguish **documented rules and measured outcomes** from **explicitly proposed simulation settings**.

## 1. Mechanisms: rules a simulation can implement

### 1.1 Ostrom’s principles are interacting mechanisms, not a success checklist

Cox, Arnold, and Villamayor-Tomás reviewed **91 studies** of Ostrom’s design principles and found substantial support, while also identifying important qualifications. Their findings support using the principles as a diagnostic framework—not assigning a guaranteed survival bonus for each principle present. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/fe453645-ec0f-4a71-91ad-19abd9634dbc/download)

The right-hand column below is a proposed TCE implementation.

| Principle | Operational interpretation for TCE |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Defined users and resource boundaries** | Specify eligible households, admitted guests, resource patches, and seasonal exceptions. Allow contested boundaries and unauthorized entry. |
| **2. Rules fit local conditions; benefits and burdens correspond** | Allocate water, grazing, or harvest shares alongside maintenance duties. Let proportionality mean equal household shares, land-area shares, herd shares, or another locally accepted formula. |
| **3. Users can participate in changing rules** | Give affected actors a route to propose, contest, or amend rules. Participation can be direct, delegated, unequal, or restricted. |
| **4. Accountable monitoring** | Assign actual people to inspect resources and conduct. Users can replace monitors, challenge reports, or discover favoritism. |
| **5. Graduated sanctions** | Distinguish mistakes, emergency violations, repeated evasion, and organized appropriation. Escalate responses contextually rather than mechanically. |
| **6. Accessible conflict resolution** | Provide a local hearing or mediation process whose travel, delay, and participation costs are much lower than prolonged retaliation. |
| **7. Recognition of self-organization** | Where larger authorities exist, track whether they recognize, tolerate, undermine, or override local rules. |
| **8. Nested organization where needed** | Let canal branches manage local turns while a larger association coordinates the intake; let villages negotiate watershed or grazing agreements. |

The principle labels summarize Ostrom’s framework as evaluated by Cox and colleagues; the specific mechanics are design proposals. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/fe453645-ec0f-4a71-91ad-19abd9634dbc/download)

### 1.2 Institutional formation requires both a shared problem and a workable coalition

Comparative research rejects simple explanations such as “small groups cooperate” or “scarcity creates institutions.” Group size, inequality, market access, resource characteristics, and outside authority interact. Larger groups may have higher coordination costs but also greater capacity to finance monitoring; inequality may supply organizers or enable domination. [U-M Personal Web Server](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~arunagra/papers/Common%20Property%20Institutions%20and%20Sustainable%20Governance%20of%20Resources.pdf)

**Recommended formation process:**

A person notices a recurrent problem: animals enter fields, the canal silts up, downstream plots receive too little water, or a valuable harvest is taken prematurely. That person proposes a limited remedy from TCE’s authored rule vocabulary. Other actors assess its expected effects on their own households, not an omniscient estimate of village welfare.

Support depends on expected benefits, obligations, trust in the proposer, anticipated compliance by others, and the ability to exclude or bargain with nonparticipants. Adoption then requires the locally available decision procedure: agreement among users, an elders’ decision, a coalition’s effective control, or another institutionally permitted route.

Start with small arrangements. A shared opening date or a two-household maintenance agreement can precede a standing council. An institution should become more elaborate because repeated problems generate demand for additional rules—not because settlement population crosses a civilization-level threshold.

**Important implementation distinction:** an arrangement can become operational without universal consent. Record who accepted it, who merely complied, who was excluded, and who remains capable of resisting it.

### 1.3 Separate taking from maintaining

Two different collective-action problems arise:

**Appropriation:** Who gets how much, where, and when?

**Provision:** Who maintains the infrastructure, habitat, monitoring, and adjudication that make appropriation possible?

These problems are linked but not interchangeable. Ostrom and Gardner’s irrigation analysis shows why downstream users may withhold maintenance when upstream users capture the water, and why bargaining over work and distribution can improve outcomes. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/4b6a7c34-81ae-4932-a20d-60640fe460e9/download)

For TCE, maintain separate records for:

* Resource entitlements and actual withdrawals.
* Work, material, and financial obligations and actual contributions.
* Benefits actually received—not merely promised.

A household that fulfills its work quota but receives no water should react differently from one that receives its full share while avoiding maintenance.

### 1.4 Compliance is a decision under imperfect information

A useful **proposed**, uncalibrated choice model is:

\[
\Delta U\_i =
U\_i(\text{extra resources now})
-
p\_{D,i}p\_{E,i}\,U\_i(\text{sanction cost})
-
E\_i(\text{future access and relationship losses})
-
N\_i
\]

Here, \(p\_D\) is perceived detection probability, \(p\_E\) is perceived enforcement probability conditional on detection, and \(N\_i\) is the agent’s internal cost of violating a accepted norm. All terms must be expressed consistently in utility units. Expected future losses already incorporate their likelihood.

Use this as a decomposition, not a claim that people consciously solve this equation. Agents can use routines, imitate peers, misjudge detection, and change behavior after experience.

Several consequences follow for implementation:

**Detection and enforcement must be separate.** Everyone may know that a powerful herd owner is exceeding the limit while nobody can safely punish them.

**Subsistence urgency changes incentives.** An agent facing immediate hunger may violate a rule that they usually support.

**Rules must be physically violable.** An unauthorized woodcutter can enter a forest unless someone or something actually prevents entry. The rule creates consequences, not an invisible wall.

**Successful institutions need not eliminate violations.** McKean’s Japanese cases included minor infractions and their handling; functioning governance was not synonymous with universal obedience. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download)

### 1.5 Communal labor is not one institution

Netting distinguishes collective work on productive commons from work for wider community purposes and assistance to particular households. His cases include scheduled maintenance, emergency repair, reciprocal work parties, paid substitutes, and contributions in cash. These arrangements should not all be collapsed into a generic “corvée” action. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/cd632882-96a7-4ce2-8ccb-e30276014cec/download)

A practical TCE classification is:

| Labor arrangement | Who benefits? | What motivates or compels participation? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocal work party | A particular household now; participating households over time | Expected reciprocation, hospitality, reputation, social pressure |
| Commons maintenance | Users of a particular resource | Benefit-linked obligations, continued access, agreed sanctions |
| Rotating public service | A settlement or neighborhood | Turn-taking, customary duty, compensation, exemption rules |
| Compulsory communal or state service | A community, ruler, estate, or mixed beneficiaries | Authority-backed obligation; possibly weak consent and unequal benefits |

For each work event, schedule actual agents, travel, tools, food, and foregone private work. Track exemptions, substitute workers, deferred duties, and arrears. A nominally equal household obligation can impose very unequal burdens on households with different numbers of able workers.

A useful ethnographic example comes from **Kofyar communities in Nigeria**: hosts provided food and millet beer, and a prevailing expectation limited requests for neighborhood assistance to roughly once or twice annually. This was a social expectation, not a universal statutory quota. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/cd632882-96a7-4ce2-8ccb-e30276014cec/download)

### 1.6 Resource ecology determines which rules make sense

The following are proposed modeling distinctions, rather than four interchangeable “commons” skins.

| Resource | Essential physical process | Useful institutional building blocks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pasture** | Seasonal forage growth, animal consumption, trampling, drought, winter fodder constraints | Grazing dates, stocking rights, herd supervision, rested areas, access agreements |
| **Forest** | Slow tree growth, age structure, regeneration, deadwood, multiple products | Separate timber/fuelwood/litter rights, harvest zones, cutting cycles, tool restrictions, regeneration duties |
| **Irrigation** | Directional flow, withdrawals upstream of others, conveyance losses, maintenance bottlenecks | Water turns, proportional shares, gatekeepers, canal-cleaning obligations, intake–branch federations |
| **Fishery** | Recruitment, harvest mortality, movement, breeding habitat, fishing effort | Entry limits, territories, seasonal closures, gear restrictions, harvest limits, intercommunity agreements |

Do not require villagers to understand ecological dynamics perfectly. They can recognize declining catches or delayed water turns while misidentifying their causes. Rule changes should depend on perceived conditions and political interests; resource recovery depends on actual conditions.

## 2. Parameters: evidence-backed anchors and explicit design assumptions

### 2.1 Documented rules and measured outcomes

**Confidence below refers to the finding in its documented context.** It does not imply that the number transfers unchanged to another resource, region, or era.

| Quantity | Value and units | Appropriate use in TCE | Evidence and confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpine grazing entitlement tied to winter support | Summer animals permitted **no greater than the number supportable over winter** from private holdings; ratio ≤1 | An authored entitlement formula linking private fodder production to shared pasture access—not an ecological carrying-capacity estimate | McKean’s comparative discussion of Swiss arrangements. **High for the reported rule; low as a universal rule.** [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download) |
| Short communal harvest openings | Sometimes **1–2 days per opening**; other products had much longer seasons | Seasonal permission windows, with duration chosen by product and conditions | Japanese village cases in McKean. **High descriptive; context-specific.** [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download) |
| Household harvesting effort limit | In reported arrangements, **one able-bodied adult and one packhorse per entitled household** | Effort restrictions as an alternative to weighing every bundle harvested | McKean. **High descriptive; not a population-wide average.** [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download) |
| Irrigation benefits and maintenance shares | In Huaynacotas, rights and work obligations were tied to **irrigated area**, with common irrigation frequency | Use a household’s fraction of entitled hectares to allocate a corresponding fraction of work | Trawick 2001. **High for institutional description; not an experimentally estimated coefficient.** [Bisses du Valais](https://bisses-valais.ch/app/uploads/2020/06/Trawick-Paul-B.-Successfully-governing-the-commons-principles-of-social-organization-in-an-Andean-irrigation-system.pdf) |
| Water monitoring cadence | A **10 km irrigation ditch patrolled daily** by a paid guard in Törbel | A concrete patrol-workload scenario; detection still depends on visibility and timing | Netting 1993. **Moderate–high descriptive; conference paper.** [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/cd632882-96a7-4ce2-8ccb-e30276014cec/download) |
| Pastoral seasonal closure | Approximately **three months** prescribed for the Yagour spring closure; effective enforcement began later than the formal date | Distinguish announced dates from actual compliance; vary timing with seasonal growth | Dominguez et al. 2012, Moroccan High Atlas. **High for this observed arrangement.** [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259925997_Diverse_Ecological_Economic_and_Socio-Cultural_Values_of_a_Traditional_Common_Natural_Resource_Management_System_in_the_Moroccan_High_Atlas_The_Ait_Ikiss_Tagdalts) |
| Tail-end irrigation adequacy | Adequate tail water in **88% versus 46%** of farmer- versus agency-managed systems during monsoon; **24% versus 8%** in spring | A case-specific validation target for distribution and seasonal stress, not a fixed management bonus | Ostrom & Gardner 1993, Table 1. **Moderate comparative evidence; observational.** [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/4b6a7c34-81ae-4932-a20d-60640fe460e9/download) |
| Community-monitoring intervention effect | Pooled effect **−0.09 standardized units**, standard error **0.04**, on resource-use/degradation measures; **747 communities**, **12–15 months** | Validate that added monitoring can have modest, heterogeneous effects | Slough et al. 2021. **Strong causal design for the intervention package; limited historical transfer.** This is **not a 9% reduction**. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2015367118) |
| Engineered shellfish habitat productivity | Approximately **4× butter-clam density**, **>2× littleneck-clam density**, and **1.7× juvenile littleneck growth** in studied clam gardens versus comparison beaches | Demonstrate that collective habitat investment can raise production, not merely restrict extraction | Groesbeck et al. 2014. **Strong site-specific measurements; not equivalent to sustainable catch multipliers.** [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0091235) |

These anchors are heterogeneous deliberately: some are **institutional rules**, some are **workload observations**, and others are **outcomes to reproduce**. They should not all become scalar coefficients.

### 2.2 Initial sensitivity ranges for TCE

The literature does not establish a transferable “typical commons” with a standard membership size, annual labor burden, fine, or failure rate. Research has specifically criticized attempts to turn contextual conditions into universal lists and prescriptions. [U-M Personal Web Server](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~arunagra/papers/Common%20Property%20Institutions%20and%20Sustainable%20Governance%20of%20Resources.pdf)

The following are **proposed prototype settings and sensitivity ranges—not historical estimates or evidence-derived confidence intervals**.

| Simulation setting | Initial setting | Exploratory range | Units and implementation notes | Evidential status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Size of one local user association | 50 | 5–500 | Entitled households; test nested organization rather than imposing a maximum | TCE test grid; no universal optimum |
| Routine maintenance demand | 6 | 2–20 | Adult-equivalent workdays per entitled household per year, initially; replace with engineering-derived bills where possible | Low confidence; placeholder |
| Dedicated monitoring allocation | 1% | 0–5% | Share of member households’ available adult labor; charge travel and reporting time | Low confidence; test input |
| Detection under different monitoring regimes | 0.30 | 0.05–0.80 | Probability per violation opportunity, conditional on location, duration, and witnesses | Low confidence; preferably emergent from patrols |
| Enforceability | 0.70 | 0.20–1.00 | Probability an imposed sanction is actually executed, conditional on evidence and relative power | Low confidence; preferably actor-dependent |
| First material penalty after a warning | 0.5 | 0.25–3 | Local unskilled workday-equivalents of goods or money, excluding restitution | Low confidence; test proportional and regressive burdens |
| Ordinary rule-review interval | 1 year | 1–12 months | Calendar interval, supplemented by drought, conflict, succession, and infrastructure events | Design cadence, not a universal historical frequency |
| Expected continuity of resource access | 10 | 1–30 | Years used in agents’ approximate valuation of future benefits; update with migration and political risk | Low confidence; behavioral sensitivity parameter |

Define an “adult-equivalent workday” explicitly in the engine. An eight-hour accounting unit is convenient, but should not be presented as a historical constant. Actual working capacity should come from the person’s age, condition, tools, travel, season, and other obligations.

**Avoid double calibration:** once detection emerges from actual observation opportunities and patrol schedules, do not independently multiply it by a “good governance” bonus.

Also avoid a universal forest growth rate, fish renewal rate, or pasture stocking density. Those belong to the ecology and production modules, not the governance module.

## 3. Historical and regional variation

### 3.1 There was no universal progression from communal to private property

Many famous “traditional” commons were documented through relatively recent fieldwork. They demonstrate institutional possibilities, but do not directly reveal the constitutions of prehistoric settlements. The cases below should therefore supply **building blocks and constraints**, not era-wide presets.

| Setting | What the evidence shows | Implication for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Coastal Indigenous societies of the Pacific Northwest** | Clam gardens used constructed intertidal terraces to improve shellfish habitat. Resource management included productive landscape engineering, not only gathering from untouched nature. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0091235) | Non-cereal food economies can sustain investment-intensive shared resources. Do not gate communal infrastructure behind farming technology. |
| **Early farming, northern Peru** | Dillehay, Eling, and Rossen reported small gravity canals dated to approximately **5,400 radiocarbon years BP**, with an older candidate more uncertain. Infrastructure supports an inference of organized construction and maintenance, not a particular voting or sanction system. [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/27205762/Preceramic_irrigation_canals_in_the_Peruvian_Andes_By_Tom_D_Dillehay_Herbert_H_Eling_Jr_and_Jack_Rossen?utm_source=chatgpt.com) | Small-scale irrigation need not await a centralized state. Leave the exact governing arrangement open to emergence. |
| **Swiss alpine communities** | Netting documented complementary tenure: common alpine pasture, forests, water, and access routes alongside privately controlled fields, meadows, and buildings. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01531217) | Different property arrangements should coexist within the same household economy. |
| **Japanese mountain villages** | McKean’s cases used product-specific restrictions, household entitlements, opening dates, rotating or allotted harvest areas, and collective oversight. Villages differed substantially in wealth and hierarchy. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download) | Generate distinct local institutions from a common rule vocabulary; “communal” does not imply egalitarian. |
| **Nepalese irrigation** | Farmer organization could connect water allocation with maintenance and bargaining between head- and tail-end users. Physical improvements did not automatically solve distributional conflict. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/4b6a7c34-81ae-4932-a20d-60640fe460e9/download) | Hydraulic position and dependence on others’ labor must influence political power. |
| **Bali, Indonesia** | Water-temple coordination linked cropping schedules across irrigation groups. Lansing and Kremer modeled a trade-off: wider synchronization reduced pests but could increase simultaneous water demand. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/LansingKremer.BaliWaterTemples.pdf) | The relevant governing scale can be intermediate—not simply individual plots or one watershed-wide schedule. |
| **Andean irrigation, Huaynacotas, Peru** | A contiguous irrigation sequence, common frequency, and area-linked obligations made entitlements comparatively transparent and connected efficient use to a faster return of everyone’s next turn. [Bisses du Valais](https://bisses-valais.ch/app/uploads/2020/06/Trawick-Paul-B.-Successfully-governing-the-commons-principles-of-social-organization-in-an-Andean-irrigation-system.pdf) | Spatial order and mutual observability can make compliance cheaper than a large enforcement staff. |
| **Moroccan High Atlas pastoralism** | Agdal arrangements coordinated seasonal closures, movement between grazing areas, collective decisions, and culturally meaningful opening events. Formal prohibitions and actual behavior did not perfectly coincide. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259925997_Diverse_Ecological_Economic_and_Socio-Cultural_Values_of_a_Traditional_Common_Natural_Resource_Management_System_in_the_Moroccan_High_Atlas_The_Ait_Ikiss_Tagdalts) | Boundaries can be seasonal permissions and negotiated access, not permanent fences. |
| **Maine fisheries, nineteenth–twentieth-century arrangements** | Harbor groups exercised locally enforced fishing rights alongside state ownership and licensing. Motorization changed access and the cost of defending territorial arrangements. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf) | Distinguish legally recognized rights from effective local control; transport technology can destabilize either. |
| **Modern Mexican community forestry** | Community forest enterprises combined communal rights with commercial production, organizational investment, and state support. [UAPress](https://uapress.arizona.edu/book/mexicos-community-forest-enterprises) | Markets and modern firms need not eliminate common ownership. A commons can become a commercial enterprise. |

Across eras, the changing variables should be production technology, transport, population, administrative capacity, markets, and political power. “Era” itself need not cause institutional change.

### 3.2 Communal labor can change form without disappearing

A growing wage economy raises the opportunity cost of compulsory work. One possible response is evasion and institutional decline; another is paying a substitute, collecting dues, or hiring a permanent worker. Netting explicitly describes this possibility of financial pooling replacing some direct labor without necessarily dissolving the association. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/cd632882-96a7-4ce2-8ccb-e30276014cec/download)

For TCE, permit an institution to evolve from:

`household work rotation → work-or-payment obligation → dues-funded specialist`

But make this a contested rule change. Cash-poor members may lose influence or access when dues replace labor, while labor-poor households may benefit.

### 3.3 Enclosure changes power and distribution as well as production

English enclosure should not be implemented as “privatization researched: +20% agricultural output.”

Allen’s *Enclosure and the Yeoman* argues that open-field smallholders accounted for substantial productivity improvements and that enclosure’s gains were concentrated among landowners, with limited aggregate benefits in his South Midlands analysis. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/34575?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Heldring, Robinson, and Vollmer’s **2022 working paper**, covering parliamentary enclosure acts from **1750–1830** across roughly **15,000 parishes**, finds increased yields and inequality. Its reported wheat-yield estimates differ sharply by method: about **3.4% in ordinary least squares versus 45% in the instrumental-variable estimate**. These are different estimands with different identification assumptions, not a credible universal “3–45% enclosure bonus.” The authors also discuss regional differences from Allen’s results. [Becker Friedman Institute](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/BFI_WP_2022-30.pdf)

**Recommended transition mechanics:** calculate separate consequences for consolidation of scattered plots, removal of collective restrictions, fencing and access costs, investment incentives, loss of grazing or gathering rights, compensation, and bargaining power. Track the households that gain and those that lose.

Allow several transition routes rather than a single upgrade:

`commons → private parcels`, `commons → leased concession`, `commons → state administration`, and `commons → commercial cooperative`

Also allow reversal, restoration, or overlapping co-management.

A particularly important TCE possibility is **nominal takeover without effective replacement**: a larger authority suppresses local enforcement but fails to supply its own. The resource can then become effectively open access even while its legal owner is clearly specified. This is an implementation implication of separating rights, organization, and actual enforcement—not a proposed automatic outcome of state ownership.

## 4. Stylized facts a correct simulation should reproduce

These are validation targets at different levels. The numerical findings apply to their respective study settings, not every generated world.

| Pattern | Empirical anchor | What to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shared ownership can coexist with productive private holdings** | Swiss mixed-tenure systems. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01531217) | A household can invest in private fodder or fields while remaining dependent on common grazing and irrigation. |
| **Institutions matter through combinations, not isolated labels** | A study of **130 co-managed fisheries in 44 countries** found leadership, social cohesion, and incentives associated with better outcomes; the analysis was observational. [Monitoring Matters](https://monitoringmatters.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Gutierrez.pdf) | Monitoring without enforceability, or formal participation without effective rights, should often underperform coherent combinations. |
| **Effective governance improves distribution as well as aggregate supply** | The Nepalese seasonal tail-water differences reported above. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/4b6a7c34-81ae-4932-a20d-60640fe460e9/download) | Measure head–tail delivery gaps and reliability, not merely water entering the system. |
| **Monitoring can have modest effects, no effect, or displacement** | In the **110-village Uganda trial**, monitoring did not improve the main forest-use index, while loss increased in some unmonitored areas; displacement was a tentative interpretation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/) | Follow harvesters into neighboring patches. A protected patch improving is not sufficient evidence of landscape-wide success. |
| **Collective investment can increase the resource base** | Clam-garden growth and density differences. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0091235) | Maintenance and habitat engineering should sometimes increase future yields, rather than institutions only reducing extraction. |
| **Ecological and livelihood success need not coincide** | Fischer et al.’s study of **314 forest commons in 15 countries** found distinct combinations of carbon, tree diversity, and livelihood outcomes. [Indian School of Business](https://www.isb.edu/faculty-and-research/research-directory/community-forest-governance-and-synergies-among-carbon-biodiversity-and-livelihoods) | Report multiple outcomes. A forest can accumulate biomass while poor households lose fuel access. |
| **Institutional durability does not imply equality or unanimity** | Japanese village arrangements varied in hierarchy and distribution. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/5c2aacd0-bd49-4020-b5e5-f7b346b11522/download) | Stable institutions can generate grievances, excluded residents, and unequal benefits without immediately collapsing. |
| **Technological improvements can disturb political bargains** | Nepalese headworks analysis and changing territorial enforcement in Maine. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/4b6a7c34-81ae-4932-a20d-60640fe460e9/download) | Recalculate dependence and power after infrastructure and transport changes; do not assume every improvement benefits every user. |

A useful negative test is equally important: **neither cooperation nor collapse should be inevitable**. Under otherwise similar ecological conditions, different histories of organization, conflict, leadership, and outside intervention should sometimes produce different outcomes.

## 5. Modeling recommendation for individual agents and institutions

### 5.1 Represent the institution as an organization with records—not an aura

A compact proposed architecture is:

```
ResourceSystem
    patches_or_flow_network
    stocks_and_regeneration
    infrastructure_condition
    access_points
    environmental_shocks

UserAssociation
    member_households
    admitted_guests_and_excluded_claimants
    resource_rights
    withdrawal_rules
    contribution_rules
    decision_procedure
    offices_and_officeholders
    dispute_procedure
    sanction_rules
    treasury_and_stores
    relations_with_other_associations_and_states

HouseholdMembership
    entitlement_basis_and_quantity
    promised_and_completed_contributions
    benefits_received
    arrears_and_disputes
    membership_history

Person
    activity_schedule
    knowledge_and_beliefs
    relationships_and_reputation
    institutional_roles
    willingness_and_capacity_to_comply
```

Keep household membership separate from individual participation. An entitled household may send one person to cut grass, another to a meeting, and another to canal maintenance. Its internal distribution of benefits and burdens remains part of TCE’s household simulation.

Crucially, `resource_rights` should include separate rights rather than one owner ID. A settlement’s ruler, resource association, cultivating household, and visiting herder may hold different claims over the same land.

### 5.2 Use a small rule grammar

The authored building blocks should express rules such as:

```
WHO: entitled household, guest, officeholder, or specified category
ACTION: enter, graze, cut, divert, fish, build, inspect, contribute
WHERE: resource, patch, canal branch, or agreed territory
WHEN: season, turn, opening event, emergency, or ecological condition
LIMIT: quantity, effort, equipment, frequency, or share
OBLIGATION: labor, goods, money, reporting, or restoration
CONSEQUENCE: warning, restitution, fine, suspension, expulsion, conflict
REVIEW: decision procedure, appeal route, and amendment conditions
```

This supports both simple customary arrangements and later written constitutions without requiring natural-language reasoning for every action.

Distinguish **rules as known**, **rules as officially declared**, and **rules actually enforced**. Early oral institutions need not be less effective merely because they lack documents; the engine can model memory and transmission without a literacy prerequisite.

### 5.3 Couple rules to physical accounting

For a stored resource, use a conservation-consistent update:

\[
S\_{t+\Delta t}
=
S\_t
+
G(S\_t,E\_t,M\_t)\Delta t
+
I\_t-O\_t-H\_t
\]

Here \(G\) is net local growth or regeneration, \(E\) environmental conditions, \(M\) management inputs, \(I\) and \(O\) physical inflows and outflows over the interval, and \(H\) actual harvest. Constrain actual withdrawals to available material.

This is bookkeeping, not a recommendation to give every resource logistic growth. Forests may need age classes; fish require recruitment and movement; pasture needs seasonal growth and damage. Water usually needs directed network accounting: the same cubic meter cannot be independently allocated to two users unless return flows are explicitly modeled.

For infrastructure, derive maintenance needs from dimensions, materials, wear, and damage where feasible. A failed intake and a slightly overgrown path should not share the same linear “maintenance percentage” response.

### 5.4 Let feedbacks—not governance bonuses—produce outcomes

Consider a **proposed illustrative scenario**, not an empirical estimate:

Thirty households share a canal. Its annual maintenance requires 180 adult-equivalent workdays, initially six per household. Downstream users repeatedly receive less water than promised. They withhold work. The canal deteriorates, and delivery becomes even less reliable.

Several paths are possible. An organizer negotiates enforceable turns; upstream users accept because they need downstream labor. A wealthy household finances repairs and demands extra rights. Downstream households leave. A larger authority intervenes. Or the system partially fails.

All outcomes arise from the same accounting and agent decisions.

Similarly, successful monitoring should improve information and change incentives. It should not directly add biomass. Forest recovery must occur through changed extraction and regeneration.

### 5.5 Schedule institutional activity at several timescales

For 10k–50k people, I recommend:

**Daily or activity-level:** harvesting, grazing, water diversion, patrols, observed disputes, work-party attendance, and infrastructure use.

**Weekly or monthly:** account reconciliation, arrears, monitor reports, minor hearings, and updating beliefs about compliance.

**Seasonal or event-triggered:** opening dates, quota revision, major maintenance, elections or rotations of office, membership disputes, and negotiations between associations.

**Longer-term:** succession, inheritance of rights, migration, enclosure proposals, commercial restructuring, and changes in relations with states.

Use sparse household–institution and person–person links. Resolve most routine decisions locally; avoid having all users reconsider every rule every day. Institutions should become computationally expensive mainly when something consequential happens.

For visible daily life, this architecture naturally produces concentrated canal-cleaning days, guarded gates, collective forest openings, livestock movements, meetings, and disputes. Those events should be consequences of simulation state, not decorative schedules disconnected from production.

### 5.6 What to simplify—and what not to simplify

**Reasonable v1 simplifications:** a few entitlement formulas; small sets of sanction and decision procedures; approximate perceived stock conditions; household-level obligation accounting; stylized hearings; and aggregated fish or forest cohorts.

**Keep explicit from the start:** physical withdrawals, maintenance labor, membership, unequal entitlements, imperfect detection, actual enforcement capacity, and access to alternative resource patches. These are the pathways through which governance changes outcomes.

Avoid a single “social capital” number doing everything. At minimum, separate trust that others will contribute, confidence in officeholders, willingness to sanction, and the organization’s ability to exclude outsiders.

## 6. Sources, datasets, and evidential limits

### Core scholarly foundation

The most useful foundation is **Ostrom’s *Governing the Commons* (1990)**, read alongside **Schlager and Ostrom’s “Property-Rights Regimes and Natural Resources” (1992)** for the rights architecture. Ostrom’s **“Beyond Markets and States” (2010)** provides the broader argument for overlapping and polycentric governance. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/governing-the-commons/A8BB63BC4A1433A50A3FB92EDBBB97D5)

For evaluating claims rather than adopting a checklist, use **Cox, Arnold, and Villamayor-Tomás (2010)** and **Agrawal’s “Common Property Institutions and Sustainable Governance of Resources” (2001)**. The latter is especially useful on conditional relationships, sampling, and the difficulty of identifying general causal mechanisms. [Digital Library of the Commons](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/fe453645-ec0f-4a71-91ad-19abd9634dbc/download)

For implementable ethnographic detail, prioritize **Netting on Swiss tenure and communal labor; McKean on Japanese commons; Trawick on Andean irrigation; and Ostrom and Gardner on Nepalese irrigation**. These are more useful for writing rules than a generic coefficient relating “community management” to sustainability. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01531217)

### What remains uncertain or contested

**Prehistoric institutional detail is thin.** A canal, terrace, or managed shellfish habitat establishes investment and coordination more readily than it establishes voting rights, household quotas, or sanction schedules. Do not convert archaeological infrastructure into a confidently specified constitution. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7484696_Preceramic_irrigation_canals_in_the_Peruvian_Andes?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Much comparative evidence is observational and selected.** Well-documented enduring institutions are not a denominator from which to estimate how often all attempted commons succeeded. Leadership and participation may improve outcomes, but successful resources can also help sustain leadership and participation. [U-M Personal Web Server](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~arunagra/papers/Common%20Property%20Institutions%20and%20Sustainable%20Governance%20of%20Resources.pdf)

**Modern experiments have stronger causal identification but narrower scope.** Monitoring trials usually include training, equipment, reporting, or incentives and cover relatively short periods. Their results should constrain short-run responses, not dictate century-long institutional survival. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2015367118)

**Enclosure’s productivity effects and distributional interpretation remain contested.** Preserve competing pathways in the model rather than choosing a universal privatization multiplier. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/34575?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**“Success” must remain multidimensional.** Ecological persistence, aggregate output, household security, equality, political inclusion, and institutional survival are different outcomes. The engine should report them separately rather than hide their trade-offs inside one governance score. [Indian School of Business](https://www.isb.edu/faculty-and-research/research-directory/community-forest-governance-and-synergies-among-carbon-biodiversity-and-livelihoods)

## Recommended v1 scope

Implement **resource-specific user associations, household entitlements, real maintenance work, seasonal allocation rules, imperfect monitoring, contextual sanctions, and rule amendment**. Let associations bargain with one another and later with states.

That is enough to generate the central dynamics: cooperation without centralized government, stable but unequal commons, failures caused by broken bargains, conflicts over newcomers, commercial adaptation, and enclosure driven by identifiable winners and losers—without scripting which outcome history must produce.
