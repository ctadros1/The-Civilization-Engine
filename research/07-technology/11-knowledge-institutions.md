# Institutions of knowledge: a simulation-ready report for TCE

**Central recommendation:** represent knowledge institutions as organizations that finance, select, connect, and reproduce knowledgeable people—not as buildings that generate “science points.”

TCE should distinguish six processes: **discovery, verification, preservation, transmission, adoption, and realized productivity**. An institution can perform one well and another poorly. A closed workshop can train excellent successors while preventing outsiders from learning. A library can preserve a method that nobody currently knows how to execute. A prestigious school can expand administrative competence without producing much technical novelty.

The historical evidence supports these distinctions much more strongly than it supports a universal numerical relationship between institutional size and invention. The quantitative studies below generally measure publications, patents, downstream applications, or educational outcomes—not a civilization’s complete rate of discovery.

## 1. Mechanisms: rules a simulation can implement

### 1.1 Knowledge survives through people, records, and repeated practice

Separate **knowing that a method exists**, **understanding its explanation**, and **being able to perform it**. Apprenticeship transmitted practical skills through participation, observation, correction, and experimentation; written descriptions were not complete substitutes for competent practitioners. Training arrangements also had to solve economic problems: masters incurred instructional costs, apprentices supplied labor, and either party could behave opportunistically. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/craft-guilds-apprenticeship-and-technological-change-in-preindustrial-europe/4B18A7808BACBFA40D76475FBCB665E0)

**TCE rule:** each method should have a *tacit component* and a *codified component*. Reading can increase conceptual familiarity and reduce the difficulty of reconstruction, but practical proficiency may still require a teacher, tools, materials, and successful repetitions.

Preservation consequently has two different production chains:

> Practitioner → supervised learner → competent successor  
> Record → maintained collection → discoverable and readable record → interpretation or reconstruction

A society can retain its books while losing operational competence, or retain a craft without possessing a written explanation.

### 1.2 Guilds combine training and internal cooperation with selective exclusion

Guilds were not uniformly innovation-blocking organizations. Epstein emphasizes apprenticeship, transferable skills, and arrangements supporting technological transmission. Ogilvie emphasizes entry restrictions, political privileges, rent extraction, and evidence that claimed public benefits could be supplied by alternatives. This is a substantive historical disagreement—not a reason to assign every guild an average positive or negative modifier. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/craft-guilds-apprenticeship-and-technological-change-in-preindustrial-europe/4B18A7808BACBFA40D76475FBCB665E0)

**TCE rule:** give associations independently adjustable functions: training contracts, certification, quality inspection, mutual assistance, entry restrictions, production rules, and secrecy obligations.

An association might simultaneously:

* Improve training within its membership.
* Restrict who can become a master.
* Share techniques internally but punish disclosure externally.
* Oppose a labor-saving method that threatens incumbent incomes.

Model the consequences through actual contracts, access, and incentives. “Guild membership” should not itself change an agent’s inventive ability. Courts, household reputation, employers, and other associations should sometimes substitute for guild enforcement.

### 1.3 Schools reproduce capabilities; research is a separate activity

Educational institutions need not primarily pursue new knowledge. Sargonic scribal education supported administrative competence. Medieval European universities also supplied legal expertise relevant to commerce and institutions, rather than operating like modern research laboratories. These are economically important outputs even when they produce no new technological node. [Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East](https://hmane.harvard.edu/publications/scribal-education-sargonic-period)

**TCE rule:** education should produce specific competencies: literacy in a particular script, numeracy, legal interpretation, surveying, medicine, rhetoric, or other authored skills.

Research requires additional conditions: a problem or question, relevant knowledge, time, tools, materials, and some reason to investigate. A school becomes research-active because particular people undertake those activities—not merely because the building reaches a higher level.

Admission rules determine whose potential becomes usable. Financial assistance, household obligations, status restrictions, language requirements, and personal sponsorship should affect entry and completion. Put these differences in access and life histories, not in innate attributes assigned to cultures.

### 1.4 Patronage buys time and resources—but also directs the agenda

Patronage can support work that has no immediate market return. It can also direct attention toward a patron’s prestige, religious commitments, military needs, medical concerns, or administrative problems. David’s account of early modern open science emphasizes competitive patronage and reputation: public evaluation could help patrons judge scholars whose expertise they could not assess directly. This is a historical explanation of a particular institutional development, not a universal origin story for intellectual openness. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/icc/article-abstract/13/4/571/718486)

Modern evidence likewise suggests that funding arrangements matter beyond the amount of money. Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Manso found that the HHMI funding system—combining investigator freedom, tolerance of early failure, and longer-term rewards—encouraged influential research and exploration relative to their comparison group. It does not establish that every patron-funded institution will outperform every competitive funding system. [MIT Open Scholarship](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/8ad96999-7c50-42d8-aedf-778272c766de)

**TCE rule:** track funding horizon, agenda restrictions, failure tolerance, patron succession, and scholars’ ability to change sponsors.

An endowment should be an actual income-producing asset with spending rules. It can sustain an institution across a patron’s death, but cannot pay salaries when its rents or agricultural revenues disappear.

### 1.5 Openness is a collection of permissions, not one slider

For TCE, distinguish:

| Dimension | Question answered |
| --- | --- |
| Admission | Who may join or study? |
| Teaching | Who may receive instruction, and at what price? |
| Reading and copying | Who may consult, borrow, translate, or reproduce records? |
| Material access | Who may use instruments, specimens, workshops, or experimental inputs? |
| Disclosure | Must findings be published, shared internally, or kept secret? |
| Practice rights | Who may commercially use a disclosed method? |
| Criticism | Who may challenge accepted claims or authorities? |

Access to physical research materials can matter independently of access to texts. Research on biological resource centers and genetically engineered mice finds that institutional changes facilitating material access increased subsequent research; the mouse study particularly emphasizes entry by new researchers and exploration of new directions. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.101.5.1933)

**TCE rule:** publishing a recipe does not automatically provide the necessary instrument, specimen, skilled assistant, or permission to use it. Each is a separate possible bottleneck.

### 1.6 Disclosure incentives and commercial exclusion can coexist

A patent is not equivalent to secrecy. It can disclose information while restricting particular uses. Conversely, a trade secret can be legally unpatented but practically inaccessible. Historical patent systems also influenced *which* industries attracted invention; Moser’s nineteenth-century exhibition evidence is especially useful here because it observes innovations outside patent records. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F0002828054825501)

**TCE rule:** model privileges as scoped claims with a territory, duration, holder, enforcement mechanism, and possible licenses. Track disclosure separately.

Private incentives should arise from expected rewards: sales, commissions, temporary exclusivity, prizes, reputation, patronage, or the practical value of solving a problem. The same institution that raises those rewards can restrict follow-on experimentation. Its net effect should emerge from those competing channels.

### 1.7 Censorship changes participation and topic choice—not only surviving output

Censorship can remove texts, but it can also discourage prospective authors, redirect careers, alter publishers’ incentives, and reduce the supply of future teachers. Blasutto and de la Croix explicitly model occupational selection alongside restrictions on books. Research on Renaissance Venice additionally links censorship exposure to changes in publishing firms, entry, survival, and creative direction. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/133/656/2899/7230362)

**TCE rule:** anticipated punishment should affect whether an agent studies a field, records a finding, teaches it, publishes it, conceals it, or moves elsewhere. Enforcement requires information and resources; it should not be omniscient.

Apply restrictions to particular subjects, claims, people, or audiences. A patron might support astronomical calculation while forbidding criticism of political authority. An institution may preserve knowledge in one domain and suppress it in another.

Fewer observed prohibited works can therefore mean either successful intimidation or genuine tolerance. TCE’s reporting must distinguish them.

The strongest lesson is not a single multiplier. Different interventions change different margins: who enters, who learns, which questions receive attention, and whether existing findings can be used.

### 2.2 Starting parameters where historical measurement is thin

The following are **proposed TCE engineering priors**, not measured historical averages. Their purpose is to make the system implementable and expose sensitivity—not to disguise missing evidence.

| Parameter | Suggested initial range or test values | Units and implementation | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Concurrent hands-on apprentices | **1–3** per active master | People; divide scarce individual instructional attention among learners. | Design prior; low empirical confidence as a general range. |
| Instructional cohort | **5–30** per instructor | Learners per session; distinguish lectures, recitation, demonstrations, and supervised practice. | Design prior; institution- and task-specific. |
| Protected investigative time | **0–10%** for ordinary craft workers; **25–75%** for supported scholars | Share of available work time; count problem-solving embedded in production without charging the same hour twice. | Design prior, not a historical occupational average. |
| Financial reserve | **0.5–5 years** of recurring costs | Operating-cost years, preferably denominated internally in wages, food, materials, and maintenance obligations. | Design prior; stress-test dependence on one patron. |
| Succession stress tests | **0, 1, 2, 5** successful successors per practitioner’s career | Compute the result from actual teaching; use these as diagnostic outcomes, not automatically generated successors. | Model diagnostic. |
| Independent archival locations | **1, 3, 10** | Separately exposed collections; ten copies in one burning building are not ten independent safeguards. | Model diagnostic, not a claim about typical collections. |
| Access, disclosure, and enforcement | **0–1**, conditional on person and domain | Probabilities or policy weights; distinguish legal rules from effective implementation. | Model representation rather than an empirical estimate. |

Do not assign a universal annual manuscript-loss rate. Use TCE’s existing hazards—fire, damp, warfare, abandonment, theft, material deterioration—and actual conservation labor. Likewise, derive institution costs from the simulation’s food, wages, buildings, writing materials, and equipment rather than using an invariant “university costs 500 coins.”

**The weakest historical parameters are precisely the ones most tempting to invent:** inventions per scholar-year, a general “secrecy penalty,” optimal class size, and a universal relationship between library size and innovation. These should remain visibly provisional.

## 3. Variation across eras and regions

These are overlapping configurations, not mandatory stages. Forager teaching networks, household production, religious instruction, state schools, private tutors, and commercial research can coexist.

| Context | Historically important arrangements | Implication for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Forager societies** | Research with contemporary Agta communities documents multilevel social networks and uses them in simulations of cumulative cultural evolution. Inter-camp connections help recombine knowledge. This is not a direct measurement of prehistoric invention rates. [Complexity Science Hub](https://csh.ac.at/publication/hunter-gatherer-multilevel-sociality-accelerates-cumulative-cultural-evolution/) | Begin with household, specialist, camp, and inter-community teaching—not “no institutions until writing.” Mobility and overlapping affiliations matter. Contemporary foragers should not be treated as unchanged prehistoric populations. |
| **Early farming and early states** | Household instruction can preserve practical skills without writing. By the Sargonic period, approximately **2335–2150 BCE**, Mesopotamian scribal education had an important administrative role. [Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East](https://hmane.harvard.edu/publications/scribal-education-sargonic-period) | Separate widespread practical knowledge from narrow literate specialization. Schools may first emerge around accounting, administration, ritual, or other recurrent demands rather than abstract research. |
| **South Asia: monastic scholarship** | Nalanda’s principal monastic educational development is dated to approximately the **5th–13th centuries CE**; its archaeological remains show substantial residential and educational infrastructure. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1502/) | Support residential scholars, donors, teaching, collections, and long-distance intellectual connections without requiring a European-style university charter or modern degree system. Avoid legendary enrollment figures as capacity parameters. |
| **Islamicate societies, roughly 800–1700** | Teaching and scholarship operated through homes, courts, hospitals, madrasas, family connections, and travel. The place of mathematics and other sciences varied across institutions and regions. [Brepols](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503574455-1) | Do not make “madrasa” synonymous with either universal scientific flourishing or universal restriction. Author curricula, teachers, sponsors, and local networks separately; avoid a scripted civilizational shutdown date. |
| **Medieval and early modern Europe** | Monastic collections such as St Gall preserved manuscript traditions. Universities supplied teaching and professional expertise; research on medieval universities connects legal training and institutional change to commercial development. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/268/) | A monastery may be an archive, school, landholder, and patron simultaneously. A university can improve legal and administrative capacity before becoming a major source of experimental research. |
| **Late imperial China** | Civil examinations strongly shaped elite education, curricula, and routes to official status. Educational merit operated within substantial social and institutional constraints. [Harvard University Press](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674724952) | Examination rewards redirect study time. They can expand common scholarly competence and administrative recruitment while drawing effort away from poorly rewarded subjects. Model this as incentives, not an inherent cultural resistance to invention. |
| **Edo-period Japan** | Domain and other schools educated samurai, while terakoya and private schools served broader groups. The prestige of classical learning could stimulate demand for schooling while imposing costs on functional literacy. [Journals](https://journals.uni-lj.si/as/article/view/7383) | Distinguish prestigious textual competence from practical reading, calculation, and occupational knowledge. Different school types can serve different markets and status groups. |
| **West Africa and Timbuktu** | Manuscript scholarship formed part of wider African intellectual traditions, including Arabic and African-language writing in Arabic scripts. Written and oral scholarship should not be treated as mutually exclusive systems. [OpenUCT](https://open.uct.ac.za/bitstream/handle/11427/2421/Meanings_Timbuktu.pdf) | Represent teachers, collections, copying, interpretation, and travel without forcing every scholarly center into the modern category “university.” Surviving manuscripts are an incomplete sample of knowledge production. |
| **Mesoamerica** | Sources on the Mexica calmecac describe education associated with priestly and elite responsibilities, including the interpretation and performance of knowledge linked to books. [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/calmecac) | Allow specialized education using different relationships between graphic records, memorization, interpretation, and performance. Alphabetic writing is not the only route to institutional learning. |
| **Early modern academies and learned societies** | The Royal Society combined correspondence, meetings, experimental activity, and publication; *Philosophical Transactions* began in **1665**. [Royal Society](https://royalsociety.org/about-us/history/) | Model communication infrastructure and organized verification. A learned society can connect people already employed elsewhere; it need not own all the scholars or laboratories it coordinates. |
| **Industrial and modern settings** | Industrial innovation operated under different patent regimes, while modern research combines public disclosure with proprietary data, materials, and commercial rights. Nineteenth-century exhibitions and modern access studies expose different parts of this mixed system. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F0002828054825501) | Add specialized research employment and larger experimental inputs without replacing the older mechanisms. Apprenticeship, secrecy, patronage, teaching, and personal networks remain relevant alongside formal research organizations. |

Across these cases, institutional labels are unreliable shortcuts. “Religious,” “private,” “state,” or “corporate” ownership does not uniquely determine openness, competence, or willingness to support investigation.

## 4. Stylized facts a plausible simulation should reproduce

These are patterns the model should be **capable of generating under appropriate conditions**, not outcomes that every world must exhibit.

**Rebuilding a scholarly community can take much longer than rebuilding its rooms.** Waldinger’s German evidence finds persistent effects from personnel losses, while the measured effects of physical destruction faded substantially sooner. TCE should permit replacement buildings with empty teaching positions, weak successor cohorts, and missing specialties. [fabianwaldinger](https://www.fabianwaldinger.com/_files/ugd/0d0a02_8ad0f20d431f4702815a0d57d0cce230.pdf)

**Opening access can change who participates, not merely how much incumbents produce.** The mouse-access study emphasizes new researchers and new directions. A good calibration experiment should therefore examine entrant shares and topic diversity alongside total discoveries. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.20140062)

**Innovation occurs without patent statutes, but its composition can differ.** Moser’s exhibition dataset contains roughly **15,000 innovations**, covering **12 countries in 1851** and **10 in 1876**. Countries without patent laws concentrated more strongly in industries where alternative protection mechanisms were effective. TCE should not equate “no patents” with “no invention.” [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F0002828054825501)

**Restrictions can reduce subsequent use even after the original discovery exists.** The genome study’s approximately **20–30%** downstream reduction concerns access to already-produced information. Accordingly, TCE must distinguish the stock of discovered knowledge from the rate at which agents can exploit it. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/669706)

**Political suppression can make apparent conformity rise through selection.** Fewer dissenting scholars may reflect fewer people entering a field, migration, or self-censorship—not greater intellectual agreement or more accurate knowledge. The censorship model provides a concrete example of this mechanism. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/133/656/2899/7230362)

**Knowledge migration can benefit a destination while devastating its source.** The émigré chemistry result is a field-specific destination effect. It must not become a “persecution increases science” rule: it does not measure the losses suffered by expelled people or the total consequences for origin and destination combined. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.104.10.3222)

**Network structure matters beyond population size.** The Agta-network research supports investigating connected local groups, mobility, and recombination rather than assuming that every additional person contributes equally to one universally accessible knowledge stock. [Complexity Science Hub](https://csh.ac.at/publication/hunter-gatherer-multilevel-sociality-accelerates-cumulative-cultural-evolution/)

## 5. Modeling recommendation for individual agents and institutions

### 5.1 Use modular institutions and several distinct knowledge states

A compact representation could use the following entities.

| Entity | Important state |
| --- | --- |
| **Person** | Sparse domain proficiencies; known methods; conceptual familiarity; languages and literacy; confidence in claims; teachers and collaborators; available time; household constraints; reputation and affiliations. |
| **Method or claim** | Domain; prerequisites; tacit versus codified content; required materials and equipment; testing procedure; applicability; known variants; evidence status. |
| **Knowledge carrier** | Person, document, prototype, specimen, or organizational routine; location; custody; condition; language; accessibility; links to the knowledge it can transmit. |
| **Institution** | Members and roles; budgets and assets; admission rules; curricula; teaching capacity; active projects; collections; patrons; domain-specific permissions; continuity and governance rules. |
| **Relationship or contract** | Apprenticeship, employment, patronage, correspondence, collaboration, license, access agreement, or sanction; obligations, costs, duration, and enforcement. |

Keep **reputation separate from ability**. A celebrated person may be a poor teacher; an obscure artisan may possess indispensable expertise. Institutions should sometimes hire or reward imperfectly because their decision-makers observe credentials, affiliation, and reputation more easily than true competence.

Institutional forms should be compositions of functions. A temple can possess an archive, school, workshop, and observatory. A guild can run a school. A household can support a private tutor. A learned society can connect members without employing them.

### 5.2 Make discovery a project process, not a periodic population roll

Use the same basic process for a potter investigating kiln failures and a salaried scholar investigating a theoretical question:

> Encounter a problem → search accessible knowledge → assemble people and inputs → attempt a solution → test → interpret → disclose or conceal → teach or deploy.

The system should distinguish a **proposal** from a **validated capability**. Someone may formulate an explanation without possessing the equipment needed to test it. An institution can preserve that proposal until the necessary resources become available.

A practical project-level success model is:

\[
P(\text{success during interval})
=1-\exp(-\lambda\_r H\_{\mathrm{eff}})
\]

where:

* \(\lambda\_r\) is the authored hazard for project or recipe \(r\), measured per qualified hour.
* \(H\_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is effective effort during the interval, measured in hours.
* Effective effort depends on relevant skills, usable equipment, and whether the collaborating team actually has the complementary knowledge required.

Do not give a team access to the union of everything known by its city. Knowledge contributes only through a person, record, or artifact that the project can actually access.

For an explicitly **illustrative design choice**, a project with a 50% success probability after 10,000 effective hours would use:

\[
\lambda\_r=\frac{\ln 2}{10{,}000}
\approx 6.93\times10^{-5}\;\text{hour}^{-1}.
\]

This is a transparent calibration parameter, **not a historical estimate**. Different methods need different effort scales and different definitions of success. Improvements in recipe reliability should often be more frequent than discoveries of wholly new capabilities.

Openness should usually change accessible inputs, collaborators, and applications—not directly multiply \(\lambda\_r\).

### 5.3 Account for learning and institutional reproduction

Allocate actual time to demonstrations, supervised practice, reading, discussion, and independent attempts. A master cannot provide unlimited individual attention, although a demonstration can benefit several learners simultaneously.

Track a useful continuity diagnostic:

\[
R\_K=\text{successful successors produced per practitioner before exit}.
\]

In a closed stochastic teaching lineage, values below one signal attrition; values above one provide a buffer, not immunity from common shocks. Calculate this from actual agent histories rather than generating successors automatically.

This produces several desirable emergent outcomes: an aging specialist searches for apprentices; an excluded group has fewer trained successors; a patron’s death interrupts a cohort; an immigrant master can restore an otherwise lost practice.

Record loss and practical-skill loss must remain separate. A surviving manual should make reconstruction easier without guaranteeing it.

### 5.4 Model correlated preservation risks

Redundancy should depend on geography and institutional independence.

For an illustrative archive model, let \(p\) be the probability that an individual copy is lost during a specified risk window, conditional on no shared catastrophe. Let \(c\) be the probability of a catastrophe destroying all \(n\) locations. Then:

\[
P(\text{all copies lost})=c+(1-c)p^n.
\]

With hypothetical \(p=0.10\), three independent copies have an all-loss probability of 0.1%. Adding a 5% shared-catastrophe risk raises it to approximately 5.1%. These are mathematical examples, not manuscript-loss estimates.

The implication is useful: distributing copies across independently governed settlements can protect knowledge better than enlarging one collection. But preservation still requires readers, maintenance, and practical reconstruction capacity.

### 5.5 Let institutions emerge from existing economic and political behavior

Founding decisions should originate with agents and coalitions.

A merchant sponsors arithmetic instruction because reliable clerks are valuable. Masters organize because they want enforceable training contracts or restricted competition. A ruler funds surveying for taxation. A religious community maintains instruction and records for its own purposes. Scholars establish correspondence because distant specialists possess useful knowledge.

These are proposed agent motivations, not fixed historical scripts. Whether an institution survives depends on revenues, legitimacy, member incentives, and its ability to replace key people.

Policies should also be endogenous. Members may vote to restrict admission when incomes fall. A patron may demand a new curriculum. Competing sponsors may improve scholars’ exit options. An institution may become more open to attract talent or more restrictive to preserve rents.

Do not require the sequence:

> household teaching → guild → university → academy → modern laboratory.

Require capabilities and resources instead.

### 5.6 Keep implementation bounded at 10k–50k agents

Use sparse knowledge records and explicit local relationships rather than all-to-all interactions.

Daily schedules can supply visible teaching, copying, practice, workshop experiments, and discussions. Aggregate their consequences into monthly learning and project updates. Review budgets, recruitment, curricula, and institutional succession less frequently or when relevant events occur.

Maintain indices of available teachers, accessible collections, and active specialties. Recompute opportunities when meaningful conditions change: a specialist arrives, a manuscript is translated, a patron funds equipment, a restriction is lifted, or a workshop acquires a missing input.

The Rust kernel should own authoritative knowledge and institutional state. UE5 can render a selected subset of the underlying activities without making simulation outcomes depend on which scholars happen to be on screen.

**World scale needs explicit treatment.** A world containing 10,000–50,000 people should not silently be calibrated to the aggregate discoveries of historical humanity. Either model external scholarly contacts and imports, define the represented population scale, or calibrate discovery at the smaller-world level. Distinguish “new to this settlement,” “new to this society,” and “first known in the simulated world.”

### 5.7 Existing models and games worth borrowing from

**De la Croix, Doepke, and Mokyr’s apprenticeship model** provides a formal treatment of how family, guild, and market arrangements change learning opportunities and incentives. Borrow its attention to *who can learn from whom*. Do not import its modeled growth rates as measured historical parameters, or turn its broad regional comparisons into fixed civilizational identities. [Northwestern Faculty](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/mdo738/research/delaCroix_Doepke_Mokyr_QJE_2018.pdf)

**Migliano and colleagues’ network simulations** connect observed social organization to cumulative cultural recombination. Borrow multilevel networks and bounded transmission opportunities, while preserving the distinction between observed networks and simulated evolutionary outcomes. [Complexity Science Hub](https://csh.ac.at/publication/hunter-gatherer-multilevel-sociality-accelerates-cumulative-cultural-evolution/)

**Dwarf Fortress’s documented scholarly and library development** offers an embodied-game precedent: scholars discuss, mentor, read, write original works, and produce copies. TCE can borrow the visible person-and-object representation, while separately modeling economic costs, institutional permissions, and links from knowledge to production. The cited documentation concerns the 2015 system development, not a claim about every current implementation detail. [Bay 12 Games](https://www.bay12games.com/dwarves/dev_2015.html)

### What is firm, contested, and poorly measured

The evidence supports modeling **specific access barriers, teaching relationships, selection into scholarship, and personnel continuity**. It is much less suitable for assigning a single causal coefficient to “religion,” “universities,” “guilds,” or “openness.” Even the stronger studies concern particular outcomes and settings; the faculty-dismissal studies, for example, yield different results for students and established nearby researchers. [fabianwaldinger](https://www.fabianwaldinger.com/_files/ugd/0d0a02_7a3bbda63f5a4bc799787c8338bc5fd3.pdf)

Guilds’ net contribution remains contested. Grand explanations in which a whole region becomes permanently innovative—or permanently stagnant—because of one institutional label are especially poor foundations for TCE.

For validation, fit several observable outcomes separately: training completion, successor production, entrant shares, access delays, field diversity, practical adoption, and recovery after shocks. A model that reproduces a publication total for the wrong reasons is not well calibrated.

**For v1, prioritize teacher succession, usable access to knowledge, protected problem-solving time, specific collaboration networks, and institutional survival.** Those mechanisms can produce flourishing schools, secretive but capable workshops, prestigious yet unproductive establishments, resilient traditions, and genuine knowledge loss—without scripting any of them as the inevitable next stage of history.
