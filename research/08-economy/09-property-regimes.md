# Property rights regimes and transitions: a simulation-ready report for TCE

## Executive recommendation

**Represent property as overlapping rights and obligations over assets—not as one ownership category or a productivity modifier.** A community may control admission to its territory, households may inherit cultivation rights, livestock owners may graze harvested fields, and a temple may receive part of the crop. Those arrangements can coexist on the same land. The foundational distinction is between *who may do what* and *who can make or enforce the rules*. This is the approach developed by Schlager and Ostrom and reflected in FAO’s treatment of tenure systems. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

For TCE, the principal consequences should emerge through **investment horizons, retained returns, access to resources, coordination costs, political power, and distribution**. Changing ownership should not directly change the fertility or productivity of an asset.

Three distinctions are especially important:

* **Common property is not open access.** A community can exclude outsiders and enforce rules.
* **Collective ownership is not collective production.** Families can operate separate farms on collectively held land.
* **Secure possession is not unrestricted saleability.** A tenant or customary cultivator can have durable rights without being able to sell or mortgage the land. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

These distinctions let TCE generate mixed economies without requiring a progression from “communal” to “feudal” to “private.”

## 1. Mechanisms: rights, incentives, and transitions

### 1.1 The rights that actually matter

Use the following as an implementation vocabulary. The first five closely follow the established property-rights framework; the remaining distinctions are useful extensions for TCE.

| Right or rule | Operational question | Example of an independently configurable rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Who may enter or traverse the asset? | Members may cross the pasture; outsiders need permission. |
| Withdrawal | Who may remove products? | A household harvests crops; other members collect fallen wood. |
| Management | Who chooses production and maintenance? | Cultivators choose crops, but an irrigation council sets watering schedules. |
| Exclusion | Who determines eligibility? | A lineage council admits new users. |
| Alienation | Which rights may be sold, leased, or otherwise transferred? | Cultivation may be leased but underlying land may not be sold. |
| Succession | What happens at death, marriage, household division, or extinction? | Children inherit use rights; the village recovers abandoned holdings. |
| Revenue and duties | Who receives output, rent, taxes, or labor services? | A landlord receives a crop share; the state separately levies a tax. |
| Duration and termination | When can a right expire or be revoked? | A lease lasts five years; customary occupancy continues while specified obligations are met. |
| Adjudication and recognition | Whose decision makes a claim effective? | A local council recognizes one claim while a royal court recognizes another. |

The critical implementation choice is to allow these rights to belong to **different parties**. A “landowner” need not be the cultivator, manager, beneficiary, or authority that resolves disputes. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

### 1.2 Claim formation: possession, investment, membership, and power

**Historical mechanism.** Rights can become stronger through recognized occupation, investment, membership, political influence, and formal recognition. Causality runs both ways: secure rights encourage investment, but investment can also strengthen a claim. Besley’s Ghana study explicitly addresses this endogeneity; treating observed investment as the consequence of an externally given title can therefore be misleading. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/262008)

**Implementable rule.** When an agent clears land, plants trees, builds irrigation, inherits membership, or receives an institutional grant, create or strengthen a claim according to the locally recognized rules. Do not automatically grant universal ownership merely because someone performed work.

A frontier settlement might recognize first cultivation. A lineage institution might instead recognize membership and allocate cultivation internally. A conquering government might recognize military grants while local inhabitants continue to recognize prior claims.

For endogenous change, let increasing land value raise the benefits of stronger exclusion—but also raise resistance from existing users. Scarcity should increase political pressure, not automatically select private ownership.

### 1.3 Security changes the investment horizon

**Historical mechanism.** In Ghana, Goldstein and Udry found that politically influential cultivators could leave land fallow longer without losing it. Less secure users faced incentives to cultivate sooner, sacrificing soil recovery. Security here concerned a particular practice—leaving land unused—not simply possession of a document. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/595561)

**Proposed investment rule:**

\[
NPV\_{i,a}
=
-C\_{i,a}
+
\sum\_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{
q\_{i,a,t}\,s\_{i,a,t}\,E[\Delta V\_{a,t}]
}{
(1+r\_i)^t
}
+
PV(\text{compensation or salvage})
\]

Here:

* \(q\_{i,a,t}\) is the agent’s perceived probability of enjoying the return, personally or through intended successors.
* \(s\_{i,a,t}\) is the share of the incremental return retained after relevant obligations.
* \(r\_i\) represents time preference and financing constraints.
* \(T\) depends on the improvement: annual crops, orchards, drainage, buildings, and irrigation have different horizons.

For illustration—not as a historical estimate—a constant annual loss probability of 1% gives a ten-year survival probability of about **90.4%**; 10% gives **34.9%**.

Consequently, short leases can support annual cultivation while discouraging orchards or permanent buildings. Renewal expectations and compensation for improvements can offset this. Inheritance rules also matter: an elderly farmer may invest when descendants benefit.

**Do not assign security solely by regime.** Track it by claimant, asset, and right. An enclosure can strengthen a proprietor’s exclusion rights while destroying another household’s grazing rights.

### 1.4 Contracts determine effort, risk, and surplus distribution

**Historical mechanism.** West Bengal’s Operation Barga changed the tenant’s retained crop share and protection against eviction without transferring unrestricted landownership. This illustrates how changing a few rights can alter incentives and bargaining power without replacing the entire regime. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/338744)

**Implementable rule.** Separate production from the contract governing its proceeds.

For an additional unit of effort, compare the expected retained product with the worker’s opportunity cost. Fixed rent, crop shares, wages, household residual income, and collective work-point systems should produce different calculations.

Also model risk allocation. A fixed payment can leave the operator with strong marginal incentives but a dangerous obligation after a bad harvest. A crop share spreads harvest risk between parties. Neither contract deserves an unconditional efficiency bonus.

For collective production, distinguish output pooling from contribution measurement. Cooperation, monitoring, repeated interaction, and credible sanctions can sustain effort. Do not automatically divide productivity by the number of members.

### 1.5 Commons work through governance, not communal sentiment

Ostrom’s research identifies institutional arrangements under which shared resources can be governed successfully: meaningful boundaries, locally appropriate rules, monitoring, sanctions, dispute resolution, and arrangements operating at more than one organizational level. It does not establish that all communities govern well or that collective action is costless. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.100.3.641)

**Implementable rule.** Give common-pool resources an ecological state and a governing institution. Resource users choose extraction; the institution determines eligibility, seasons, quotas, monitoring effort, and penalties.

Governance consumes labor and resources. Its effectiveness depends on whether violations can be observed, whether decisions are considered legitimate, and whether powerful members can evade sanctions.

Useful failure modes include outsider intrusion, privileged overuse, weak monitoring, disputes over membership, and boundaries that prevent adaptation to environmental variation. Successful governance should emerge from functioning rules rather than a “communal stewardship” modifier.

### 1.6 Land markets require more than permission to sell

**Historical mechanism.** Mexico’s certification program reduced the need to remain physically present to preserve land rights. Households became more likely to have a migrant member, while cultivated area changed little, consistent with land continuing to be used through consolidation and reallocation. Stronger rights can therefore facilitate departure rather than tie people to the land. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20130853)

**Implementable rule.** Model sales and leases as separate markets. A society may prohibit permanent alienation while permitting temporary transfers of use.

A potential transaction requires:

\[
\text{buyer value} - \text{seller reservation value}
>
\text{transaction and approval costs}
\]

But willingness to pay is not purchasing power. The buyer also needs funds or credit; the seller’s reservation value includes subsistence, housing, identity, inheritance intentions, and alternatives.

Use local search, not an omniscient auction of every parcel. Include approval requirements, disputed boundaries, valuation costs, and restrictions on eligible counterparties.

A title should improve borrowing capacity only when the right is pledgeable, foreclosure is enforceable, a lender is available, and the asset has a realizable market value. Lawry and colleagues’ systematic review found no evidence of improved credit access in its included studies, despite other positive outcomes in some settings. **Titling is not a credit-creation switch.** [Campbell Collaboration](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/property-rights-interventions-investment-agriculture/)

### 1.7 Inheritance connects property to long-run inequality

A comparative study of 21 small-scale populations found greater wealth persistence and inequality in the agricultural and pastoral samples than in the foraging and horticultural samples. Material wealth—particularly land and livestock—was important, but the study measured several kinds of wealth and does not establish universal outcomes for entire historical eras. [Max Planck Evolutionary Anthropology](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/AAAS/Borgerhoff-Mulder_Intergenerational_Science_2009_2189437.pdf)

**Implementable rule.** On succession, apply the actual inheritance institution: equal division, preferential inheritance, indivisible corporate ownership, institutional reallocation, or another authored rule.

Then allow consequences to emerge. Division may fragment operating units; indivisibility may leave some heirs landless; corporate ownership may preserve an estate while creating internal allocation disputes. These outcomes should depend on household formation, rental opportunities, migration, and enforcement—not inheritance alone.

Keep individual interests inside households visible. “The household owns land” must not silently imply that every member has identical control, inheritance rights, or protection after separation.

### 1.8 Political transitions are distributions of gains and losses

For TCE, treat a reform as a **political proposal followed by a capacity-constrained implementation process**.

Agents evaluate expected changes in income, security, status, obligations, and family prospects. Institutions aggregate those preferences through their actual rules: councils, officeholders, assemblies, courts, or coercion. Officials may also have interests of their own.

An implementation sequence should be:

**Proposal → authorization → identification of claims → adjudication → compensation or cancellation → reassignment → enforcement.**

This sequence can stall. A legal reform without registration, protection, or enforcement may have little practical effect. Operation Barga’s combination of political mobilization and registration is particularly instructive: changing an unenforced rule on paper is not equivalent to changing effective rights. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/30521124_Empowerment_and_Efficiency_Tenancy_Reform_in_West_Bengal)

### 1.9 Four major transition families

| Transition | What changes | Mechanisms TCE should expose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Enclosure and individualization** | Common or overlapping rights are extinguished, reassigned, or converted into exclusive holdings; physical parcels may also be consolidated. | Reduced coordination costs and greater control over improvements can coexist with loss of subsistence access, litigation, fencing costs, displacement, and concentration. English parliamentary enclosure combined rights changes with consolidation, making a pure “private ownership effect” difficult to isolate. [Becker Friedman Institute](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/BFI_WP_2022-30.pdf) |
| **Redistributive land reform and tenancy reform** | Holdings above a ceiling may be transferred; alternatively, tenants receive stronger occupancy, inheritance, or crop-share rights. | Redistribution changes bargaining power and income immediately, but production changes depend on capital, skills, incentives, and timing. Japan’s postwar reform redistributed land while restricting transfer; one study finds little next-season productivity or investment response. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deve.12309) |
| **Collectivization and decollectivization** | Decision rights, residual income, labor organization, and sometimes title move between households and collective institutions. | Pooling can support shared infrastructure, but weak effort accounting and poor information can damage performance. China’s household-responsibility reforms restored household production incentives without requiring unrestricted private landownership. Lin’s analysis attributes about half of 1978–84 agricultural output growth to decollectivization. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v82y1992i1p34-51.html) |
| **Nationalization and privatization** | State, private, worker, or other corporate holders acquire assets or equity. | Ownership transfer may change investment, extraction, patronage, management, and access to finance. Outcomes also depend on competition and budget constraints; these are distinct variables in the transition-economy literature. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F002205102760273788) |

For collectivization, **separate agricultural production from procurement and household access to food**. Meng, Qian, and Yared’s analysis of China’s Great Famine emphasizes procurement that did not adjust adequately to actual production. TCE should allow severe deprivation through the interaction of harvest shocks, extraction, information failures, and restrictions—not require total food production to vanish first. [Pierre Yared](https://pierreyared.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/famines.pdf)

For privatization, implement several possible procedures—sale, restitution, insider transfer, or distribution of shares—rather than one toggle. These are design options with different initial distributions. Physical equipment should remain where it is unless a separate action moves, sells, or destroys it.

## 2. Parameters: empirical anchors and simulation assumptions

### 2.1 Empirical calibration anchors

These are **case-specific observations, legal provisions, or estimated effects**. They are useful targets for matching a comparable scenario, not coefficients to apply whenever a regime changes.

“High” confidence below means confidence in the stated rule or descriptive measurement. “Medium” indicates an empirical causal interpretation with meaningful identification assumptions. Transferability is generally lower.

| Quantity | Value and units | Setting and source | Confidence and correct interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tenant’s legally retained crop share | **75%** when the tenant supplies the relevant nonlabor inputs; **50%** when the landlord supplies all nonlabor inputs | Operation Barga; Banerjee, Gertler & Ghatak, 2002. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/338744) | **High for the contractual rule.** Actual compliance must be modeled separately. Landlord shares are correspondingly 25% or 50%. |
| Growth contribution of tenancy reform | Approximately **28% of subsequent agricultural productivity growth** | West Bengal; Banerjee, Gertler & Ghatak, 2002. [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=303306) | **Medium within the case.** This is not a 28% increase in every treated farm’s yield. |
| Growth contribution of decollectivization | About **one-half of agricultural output growth during 1978–84** | China; Lin, 1992, *Rural Reforms and Agricultural Growth in China*. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v82y1992i1p34-51.html) | **Medium.** Attribution from historical economic analysis; not a 50% production bonus. Other reforms and inputs mattered. |
| Migration response to certification | **28% relative increase** in the probability that a household has a migrant member | Mexico, certification rollout 1993–2006; de Janvry et al., 2015. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20130853) | **Medium.** A relative probability change, not 28 percentage points. Cultivated area changed little. |
| Housing investment response to titling | Renovation rate increased by **more than two-thirds of its baseline level** | Peru; Field, 2005, *Property Rights and Investment in Urban Slums*. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1162/jeea.2005.3.2-3.279) | **Medium.** An increase in renovation incidence/rate, not necessarily spending or property value. Most investment was financed without credit. |
| Enclosure and wheat yield | Ordinary regression: approximately **0.03 log points**. Instrumental-variable estimate: **0.45 log points**, standard error **0.14** | English parishes; Heldring, Robinson & Vollmer, 2022 working-paper version, Tables 2 and 4. [Becker Friedman Institute](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/BFI_WP_2022-30.pdf) | **Contested and specification-sensitive.** These are different estimands, not a plausible universal range. The larger estimate concerns a particular subset affected by the instrument. |
| Comparative wealth inequality | Weighted Gini: foragers **0.25 ± 0.04**; horticulturalists **0.27 ± 0.03**; pastoralists **0.42 ± 0.05**; agriculturalists **0.48 ± 0.04** | Borgerhoff Mulder et al., 2009, 21 small-scale populations; reported uncertainty is standard error. [Max Planck Evolutionary Anthropology](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/AAAS/Borgerhoff-Mulder_Intergenerational_Science_2009_2189437.pdf) | **Medium as sample description; low as a world preset.** These combine different wealth classes. They are not land-only or income Ginis. |
| Perceived tenure insecurity | **19% of adults**, population-weighted, reported likely or very likely involuntary loss of rights within **five years** | Prindex 2020, surveys in **140 countries**, collected 2018–20. [Prindex](https://www.prindex.net/documents/612/PRINDEX-Comparative-2020-DIGITAL_3.pdf) | **High descriptive confidence with survey limitations.** This is prevalence of a perception, not an observed annual eviction probability. |

The enclosure estimate illustrates why copying a headline percentage is dangerous. The literature contains substantially different conclusions about efficiency, including Allen’s earlier work emphasizing distributional consequences. Historical sample, treatment definition, and identification strategy materially change the answer. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/92/368/937/5220405?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2.2 Suggested initial sensitivity ranges

There is no defensible universal table of “communal insecurity,” “feudal rent,” or “private transaction cost” spanning millennia. Start with explicit scenario sweeps:

| Simulation parameter | Suggested exploration values | Units and implementation | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expected loss of a particular right | **0%, 1%, 5%, 10%** | Annual probability in investment test scenarios | **Design assumptions; low empirical confidence.** In full worlds, derive expectations from institutions and experienced events. |
| Fixed duration | **1, 5, 20, 99 years**, plus indefinite/inheritable | Years remaining; renewal is separate | **Design sweep**, not an era classification. |
| Operator’s marginal retained share | **0.25–1.00** | Fraction of incremental output value | **Design sweep.** The Barga values provide specific legal anchors, not justification for the entire range. |
| Transfer friction | **0–25%** | Fraction of transaction value, supplemented by actual administrative labor | **Design sweep.** Separate market costs from taxes and bribes. |
| Detection of a punishable violation | **0.10–0.95** | Probability per violation exposed to the monitoring process | **Design sweep.** Depends on visibility and monitoring resources. |
| Successful enforcement after detection | **0.10–0.95** | Conditional probability per attempted enforcement action | **Design sweep.** Do not merge with detection or legitimacy. |
| Reform compensation | **0–100%** | Share of recognized claim value paid | **Design sweep.** Recognition itself determines who receives compensation. |
| Reform processing capacity | **1–20%** | Share of initially eligible claims processed per year | **Design sweep.** Also impose labor, travel, treasury, and dispute-processing constraints. |
| Proportional extraction | **0–50%** | Share of gross output, with separately specified payment priority | **Stress-test range**, not a historical rent estimate. Model fixed quotas separately. |

Do not independently roll an arbitrary “expropriation hazard” when the same risk is already generated by simulated disputes, wars, defaults, and political decisions. That would count the danger twice.

The most useful parameter combinations are interactions: short tenure with compensation; high dues with reliable insurance; communal ownership with household residual income; strong documentation with weak enforcement; and productive improvements that themselves strengthen claims.

## 3. Variation across eras and world regions

**These are configurations to support, not stages through which every society must pass.**

| Setting | Historical variation relevant to TCE | Modeling implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Foraging societies** | Possession and wealth transmission varied among the sampled populations; foragers were not uniformly without material property or inequality. Productive resources, personal possessions, and social wealth need not follow identical rules. [Max Planck Evolutionary Anthropology](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/AAAS/Borgerhoff-Mulder_Intergenerational_Science_2009_2189437.pdf) | Separate ownership of tools and stores from access to territories and resource sites. Do not initialize all foragers with an undifferentiated communal inventory. |
| **Early farming** | Bowles and Choi model farming and property institutions as potentially coevolving, rather than one mechanically preceding the other. Their result is an evolutionary model, not proof of a single historical sequence. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1212149110) | Let storage, fixed improvements, household continuity, and institutions reinforce each other. Avoid a mandatory “private property technology.” |
| **Ancient Near East** | The published ancient land-tenure corpus includes hundreds of land-sale documents from early Mesopotamian settings. Land transactions therefore long predate industrial capitalism, though surviving documents do not establish unrestricted markets throughout society. [UChicago ISAC](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/oip/earliest-land-tenure-systems-near-east-ancient-kudurrus) | Permit transactions among eligible parties alongside household, institutional, and other claims. Do not make land markets exclusively modern. |
| **Medieval Europe** | Reynolds’ work challenges treating “feudalism” as a single coherent, uniformly applied legal system. Relations among fiefs, authority, and tenure differed across places and periods. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/47420/chapter/422386524) | Compose obligations, inheritance, jurisdiction, and transfer restrictions separately. Keep labor mobility and personal status outside the land-tenure label. |
| **Islamic endowments** | Waqf arrangements could dedicate an asset’s revenue to specified beneficiaries or purposes, with administration entrusted to a manager and disposition constrained by the endowment. [Sites@Duke Express](https://sites.duke.edu/timurkuran/files/2016/10/waqf-2001-1.original.pdf) | Support an enduring institution, a manager who does not personally own its assets, beneficiaries, and restrictions on sale or repurposing. Do not equate religious property with state property. |
| **European craft towns** | Guilds governed economic activity and membership; they were institutions, not simply a uniform ownership arrangement for every workshop. Their economic effects remain debated. [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv3s8sm9) | Give guilds regulatory rights and optional corporate assets. Member workshops may remain individually or jointly owned. |
| **African customary tenure** | Local authority, kinship, gender, and political position can affect effective security. Rwanda’s regularization pilot improved some women’s rights and conservation investment, showing that formalization’s consequences depend on which claims are recognized. [WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/environmental-and-gender-impacts-land-tenure-regularization-africa-0) | Keep membership, cultivation, inheritance, and household bargaining distinct. Registration must adjudicate existing claims, not merely attach a deed to the current household head. |
| **Inner Asian pastoral systems** | Sneath’s comparative work treats pasture outcomes as connected to policy and institutional arrangements across Inner Asia, rather than simply communal versus private ownership. [Queen Elizabeth House](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/publications/state-policy-and-pasture-degradation-inner-asia) | Model seasonal access and movement across multiple resource areas. A parcel’s usefulness depends on access to complementary pasture and water. |
| **Twentieth-century East Asia** | Japan combined redistribution with transfer restrictions; China’s decollectivization changed household operating incentives without requiring fully alienable private land. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deve.12309) | Allow substantial reform through changes in use, residual income, and duration while overarching ownership remains unchanged. |
| **Latin America** | Mexico demonstrates that certification, migration, continued cultivation, and consolidation can coexist. Security reforms need not produce either immobility or wholesale abandonment. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20130853) | Connect tenure to migration and rental markets. Remove “must remain present” conditions independently of sale restrictions. |
| **Modern Indigenous and community property** | Community ownership remains a substantial modern institution. RRI’s comparative database distinguishes land owned by communities from land merely designated for their use; Māori research also documents collective owners pursuing multiple objectives. [Rights and Resources Initiative](https://rightsandresources.org/publication/who-owns-the-worlds-land-2nd-ed/) | Communities can own commercial assets, lease land, and undertake development without dissolving into individual freeholders. |

Across these cases, **temple, guild, lineage, cooperative, corporation, and state are types of organized holders**. Their internal constitutions determine who makes decisions and receives benefits. They should reuse the same underlying asset-and-rights machinery, with different governance rules.

## 4. Stylized facts and validation targets

A correct simulation should reproduce **conditional patterns**, not guarantee a preferred regime’s success.

| Pattern | What a useful TCE test should show |
| --- | --- |
| **Investment responds to expected enjoyment of returns.** Ghana’s fallow evidence demonstrates the importance of practice-specific security. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/595561) | Increasing protection against loss during fallow should permit longer fallow where its agricultural return justifies it—not increase every crop’s yield directly. |
| **Security can improve without unrestricted ownership.** Tenancy reform changed incentives in West Bengal. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/338744) | Strengthening occupancy and retained returns can change labor and investment while the landlord remains the underlying holder. |
| **Titles need not create a credit boom.** Peru’s housing response was largely financed without borrowing. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1162/jeea.2005.3.2-3.279) | Investment can rise through savings or labor allocation while loan volumes remain little changed. |
| **Immediate production effects can be small.** The Japanese study finds little next-season response. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deve.12309) | Reform changes income distribution immediately, while physical investment and production adjust with realistic delays. |
| **Communal governance has multiple possible outcomes.** Ostrom’s work rejects a universal prediction of unavoidable collapse. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.100.3.641) | Identical resource ecology should permit success or failure depending on monitoring, allocation, sanctions, and legitimacy. |
| **Distribution and aggregate output are separate.** English enclosure research studies both productivity and inequality because improvement in one does not settle the other. [Becker Friedman Institute](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/BFI_WP_2022-30.pdf) | A reform can raise marketed output while harming households that lose access or bargaining power. |
| **Food access can fail despite remaining production.** Procurement institutions mattered during China’s famine. [Pierre Yared](https://pierreyared.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/famines.pdf) | Households can become severely deprived when quotas, transfers, and mobility restrictions prevent access to available food. |

Measure more than GDP or yield. TCE’s diagnostic view should expose operator income, landlessness, effective commons access, rents, investment by horizon, dispute frequency, processing delays, migration, institutional revenue, and household food availability.

For pragmatic testing, use paired scenarios that alter one rule at a time. For example, compare a prohibition on leasing with leasing permission while keeping ownership, weather, and initial holdings fixed. These tests evaluate the mechanism; they should not demand exact replication of a historical percentage.

## 5. Recommended representation for individual agents and institutions

### 5.1 Core entities

A compact design needs the following records:

| Entity | Essential contents |
| --- | --- |
| `Asset` | Parcel, building, water facility, resource stock, equipment, or financial claim; geometry and physical condition are separate from ownership. |
| `Party` | Person, household, or institution capable of holding a claim. |
| `Claim` | Holder; asset or spatial scope; right; resource and season; duration; succession conditions; obligations; recognizing authority; evidence and contested status. |
| `Institution` | Membership, offices, decision procedure, treasury, assets, enforcement capacity, and accountability rules. |
| `Contract` | Parties, use permissions, payment schedule, output allocation, maintenance duties, termination, and compensation. |
| `Dispute` | Competing claims, relevant authority, evidence, stakes, procedural stage, and enforcement outcome. |
| `RuleTemplate` | Reusable authored definitions for eligibility, transfer, inheritance, extraction, adjudication, and reform. |

**Keep three layers distinct:**

1. **Recognized claims:** what different authorities say the rights are.
2. **Actual control and use:** who occupies, cultivates, guards, collects, or excludes.
3. **Agent beliefs:** what each person expects will happen.

The first need not match the second, and neither should automatically become common knowledge.

### 5.2 A composable village example

The following is a proposed TCE configuration, not a reconstruction of one named society:

| Component | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Underlying territory | Held by the village institution |
| Cultivation | Assigned to households; inheritable while eligibility continues |
| Crop ownership | Belongs to the operator, subject to specified dues |
| Post-harvest grazing | Available to eligible village livestock owners |
| Permanent sale | Prohibited |
| Temporary leasing | Permitted to eligible members with council approval |
| Improvements | Built and maintained by the operator; compensation rule applies on reassignment |
| Succession | Household division triggers allocation review rather than automatic geometric subdivision |
| Dispute resolution | Council judgment, with an optional higher authority |
| Religious estate | Separate institution holding designated parcels and collecting their revenue |

Now enclosure can revoke grazing and create alienable holdings. Tenancy reform can change crop shares and eviction conditions. Collectivization can pool operational decisions and output. None requires replacing every economic entity.

### 5.3 Reform should be an event sequence with conservation rules

When a reform executes, update explicit claims and contracts. Record who gains, who loses, which obligations survive, and which claims remain disputed.

Important invariants include:

* Changing title does not create or destroy land, buildings, equipment, crops, or money.
* Compensation debits a payer and credits recipients.
* Transferring one right does not silently transfer every other right.
* Existing crops and improvements receive an explicit treatment.
* Household members and institutions do not disappear when their claims are extinguished.

Retain the history of revoked claims. A later restoration, court decision, or political reversal may revive demands, but should not magically reverse intervening construction or ecological change.

### 5.4 Keep computation local and event-driven

For 10k–50k individuals, I recommend a sparse, indexed rights graph rather than checking every agent against every parcel.

Cache effective permissions for ordinary daily actions. Invalidate the affected cache entries on marriage, succession, contract renewal, institutional membership changes, judgments, or reform. Perform land-market search through bounded local candidate sets. Run agricultural allocation at seasonal decision points; process disputes according to the actual availability of officials.

Do not require a detailed trial simulation for every disagreement. A small number of adjudication procedures—customary mediation, landlord decision, council hearing, state court—can differ through eligibility, cost, delay, evidence, bias, and enforceability.

For the visual simulation, rights should generate observable behavior: fences, seasonal grazing, rent collection, guarded entrances, survey work, court visits, eviction, and abandoned improvements. Unauthorized actions should remain possible where agents can attempt them, with expected consequences rather than an invisible universal prohibition.

## 6. Sources, datasets, and limits of the evidence

### 6.1 Best starting sources

For the conceptual architecture, prioritize **Schlager and Ostrom (1992), “Property-Rights Regimes and Natural Resources,”** and **Ostrom (2010), “Beyond Markets and States.”** For investment mechanisms, use **Besley (1995)** and **Goldstein and Udry (2008)**. Together they support a rights-based representation while warning against assuming that formal title, effective security, and economic incentives are identical. [ISU Sites](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf)

For empirical calibration, the strongest starting case set is **West Bengal tenancy reform, China’s household-responsibility reforms, Mexico’s certification program, Peru’s urban titling, Rwanda’s regularization pilot, and Japan’s postwar redistribution**. They change different components of the rights bundle and produce different outcomes—precisely the variation TCE needs.

### 6.2 Datasets to use

| Dataset or corpus | Useful content | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |

[PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0158391) | Societies are historically and culturally related; observations are not independent samples of “human eras.” Preserve dates, locations, and variable definitions. |
| **Prindex** | Individual perceptions of tenure security, documentation, and differences among tenure and demographic groups. [Prindex](https://www.prindex.net/documents/612/PRINDEX-Comparative-2020-DIGITAL_3.pdf) | Perceptions are not observed eviction rates. The 2020 report is noncommercially licensed; check the terms of any specific material before bundling it with a commercial product. |

### 6.3 What remains contested or thin

**There is no well-supported universal ranking of regimes.** Lawry and colleagues’ review found heterogeneous effects across regions and interventions, with a limited high-quality evidence base. Its findings do not justify transplanting an average titling effect into every customary system. [Campbell Collaboration](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/property-rights-interventions-investment-agriculture/)

**Historical reforms are usually bundles.** Enclosure may include consolidation and roads; decollectivization may accompany price changes; redistribution may accompany credit or irrigation programs. Assigning the entire observed effect to ownership alone is generally unsafe.

**Short-run and long-run outcomes differ.** The Japanese next-season result cannot establish that redistribution had no later consequences. Conversely, a later improvement does not prove that ownership transfer alone caused it. [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deve.12309)

**Distribution is often better established than a universal efficiency effect.** It is usually possible to identify which claims a reform transfers; estimating the counterfactual production path is much harder.

**Early-period numerical parameters are especially uncertain.** Archaeological and documentary evidence can support particular institutional configurations, but rarely provides defensible annual eviction probabilities, enforcement rates, or transaction-cost distributions.

### Bottom line

For TCE, **a property regime should be a recognizable configuration of rights, contracts, institutions, and enforcement—not a mutually exclusive economic mode**.

Start with household cultivation, communal resource access, corporate estates, leases and crop shares, inheritance, compulsory dues, and disputes. Then let political transitions rewrite selected rights while agents respond through investment, effort, migration, cooperation, and resistance. That architecture can produce enclosure, land reform, collectivization, and privatization as consequential historical processes without predetermining either their arrival or their success.
