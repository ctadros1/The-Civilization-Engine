# Gleaning: what the harvest leaves, who may take it, and what it was worth, for The Civilization Engine

## Executive conclusion

**Gleaning is well documented as a custom, and thinly as a quantity.** This short note was written for one gap (plan §8, question 16, item 5): what a household's harvest leaves on the field, and whether others may gather it.

1. **Who gleaned, and by what right.**
   - The poor of the place gleaned, mostly women and children.
   - The oldest written rule leaves the field's corner, the fallen ears and the forgotten sheaf "for the poor and the stranger" (Leviticus, Deuteronomy).
   - In England it was a strong local custom. A court ruled in 1788 that it was no right at common law, only a privilege at the farmer's leave.
   - In France it was reserved for the poor and regulated by law.
2. **When, relative to the harvest.** Only after the sheaves were carried, by daylight, and only for the days before the stubble was grazed or ploughed. Village bylaws and later penal codes are consistent on this.
3. **Who was allowed or excluded.**
   - Medieval English bylaws barred those able to earn harvest wages: in 1407, anyone who could earn a penny a day.
   - Outsiders were barred.
   - The custom belonged to the parish's own poor.
4. **How it was policed.** By village bylaws enforced in the manor court, by a bell that opened and closed the gleaning day, and later by prosecutions for trespass.
5. **What it was worth.**
   - One 1787 Cambridgeshire estimate puts gleaning at half a bushel an acre: about 10 bushels for each of 40 families.
   - A secondary source says up to one-eighth of a labouring household's yearly earnings.
   - As a share of the crop it was small, a few percent at most (*derived, uncertain*).
   - To the families who gleaned it was large: a season's gleaning could feed a family for weeks to months (*derived*).

**Conclusion for TCE:** what reaping leaves on the ground is a grounded quantity of the right order (a few percent of the crop, *design prior*). Gathering it is ordinary work that people choose by what it brings their food. Who may do it is a matter of custom, which the polity may regulate. That custom is not built yet.

Throughout, **measured** or primary findings are kept apart from **estimates**. Several sources were reached only through search-engine summaries in this session. They are marked (*search summary*), and none is load-bearing.

## 1. Who gleaned, and by what right

**Primary texts.**
- Leviticus 19:9–10 and 23:22 forbid reaping a field to its edges, or gathering the gleanings, the fallen fruit of the vineyard and its unharvested remainder: "you shall leave them for the poor and the stranger".
- Deuteronomy 24:19–21 leaves the forgotten sheaf, and what remains on the olive tree and the vine, "for the stranger, the fatherless and the widow".
- Ruth 2 shows a widow gleaning behind the reapers with the owner's leave.
- ([Sefaria: Leviticus 19](https://www.sefaria.org/Leviticus.19), [Deuteronomy 24](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.24), [Ruth 2](https://www.sefaria.org/Ruth.2).)

**The Mishnah sets a floor for the corner.** Mishnah Peah 1:2: "They should not leave peah of less than one-sixtieth [of the field]"; and the amount "depends upon the size of the field, the number of poor people, and the extent of the yield" ([Sefaria, Mishnah Peah 1:2, trans. J. Kulp](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Peah.1.2)). The corner is a share deliberately left, apart from the gleanings, which are what reaping drops.

**England.**
- **Medieval village bylaws:** gleaning was the custom of the poorest, often landless, who collected "remnants of grain from the floor of the fields after the harvesting of grain". Late-medieval bylaws narrowed it.
  - At Islip (1407), those who could earn a penny a day were barred.
  - At Wymeswold the threshold was two pence a day.
  - The aim was to keep able-bodied labour for the harvest wage.
  - Sources: Leicestershire and Rutland archaeological and historical society, "The early fifteenth-century Wymeswold bye-laws revisited" (2024), citing Ault, *Open-Field Farming in Medieval England* (1972), p. 32, and Harvey ed., *Bye-laws of the manor of Islip* (1959), pp. 104–105 ([LAHS](https://lahs.org.uk/blog/the-early-fifteenth-century-wymeswold-bye-laws-revisited)).
- **1788, *Steel v Houghton et Uxor*:** the Court of Common Pleas held that "no person has, at common law, a right to glean in the harvest field". Gleaning on another's land without leave was trespass. Lord Loughborough would not turn charity into an obligation (1 H Bl 51, 126 ER 32; *search summary*: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_v_Houghton), [vLex](https://vlex.co.uk/vid/steel-against-houghton-et-803958005)).
- **After 1788, practice:** Peter King's work on 1750–1850 describes the custom persisting in practice. The title of his chapter speaks of "the failure of legal sanctions" by farmers against it (its argument was not read in this session).
  - Gleaning "played an increasingly central part" in descriptions of women's work in East Anglia, as spinning and other by-employments were lost.
  - Sources: King, "Customary rights and women's earnings: the importance of gleaning to the rural labouring poor, 1750–1850", *Economic History Review* 44 (1991): 461–476 ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ehsrev/v44y1991i3p461-476.html)); King, "Gleaners, farmers and the failure of legal sanctions in England 1750–1850", in *Crime and Law in England 1750–1840* (Cambridge, 2006) (*search summary*). The article's own figures were not read in this session.

**France.**
- The right to glean was reserved for the poor and enforced by law (Vardi, "Construing the harvest: gleaners, farmers, and officials in early modern France", *American Historical Review* 98 (1993): 1424–1447; *search summary* via [Wikipedia, "Gleaning"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleaning)).
- The penal code later punished gleaning in fields "not yet entirely cleared" of their harvest, and before sunrise or after sunset. The provision has since been repealed (*search summary*: [Assemblée nationale, written question 14-99384](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/questions/detail/14/qe/99384/(vue)/pdf)).

## 2. When

- **After the sheaves are carried.** The French code's "not yet entirely cleared" and the English practice agree on this (*search summary*). Ruth gleans behind the reapers only by the owner's leave, which marks that as an exception.
- **By daylight, at set hours.** In small English villages the sexton rang a church bell at eight in the morning and seven in the evening to open and close the gleaning day (Cowie, *Dictionary of British Social History*, 1996, p. 130; *search summary* via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleaning)).
- **For a short season.** Gleaning ran from the carrying until the stubble was grazed or ploughed. No figure for its length was found in this session (*uncertain*; a few weeks at most).

## 3. How much was left, and what it was worth

**One measured estimate.** "An estimate relating to gleaning", Co. Cambridge, 1787: "880 acres of all sorts of grain, produced to 40 families, gleaning fairly and unexceptionably", "half a Bushel per acre 400 Bushels … at the Average of 4s. a Bushel … 80l."; with straw and chaff "the whole of the Gleanings might be worth 100l." ([Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/n3275y8a)). That is about **10 bushels per gleaning family** in one parish's year.

**A secondary figure.** As much as **one-eighth** of labouring households' yearly earnings came from gleaning in the 18th and 19th centuries (Hussey, "'The last survivor of an ancient race': the changing face of Essex gleaning", *Agricultural History Review* 45 (1997): 61–72; *search summary* via Wikipedia). It is not load-bearing.

**Derived, uncertain.**
- **As a share of the crop:** half a bushel an acre against grain yields of perhaps 15–25 bushels an acre in late-18th-century England is about **2–3 %** of the crop. The yield range is not confirmed in this session: wheat yields rose about a quarter in the 18th century (*search summary*). The Mishnah's floor for the corner, a share deliberately left in addition to gleanings, is 1/60, about **1.7 %**.
- **As food:** a bushel of wheat is about 27 kg (60 lb), so 10 bushels is about 270 kg of grain. At about 3,300 kcal/kg that is roughly 900,000 kcal, a family of five's needs at 2,000 kcal a day for about 90 days. That is of the order of King's and Hussey's "significant share", if the parish's grain was mostly wheat (*derived*).
- **As a rate:** if two or three of a family gleaned for about twelve working days of ten hours, 270 kg is about **1 kg an hour** (*derived, highly uncertain*). No measured gleaning rate was found.

**What reaping leaves depends on how it is done.** Research 08-02 §2.2 separates what a crop grew from what is collected: "improved harvesting tools should first change collection speed and losses". The sickle and the scythe, binding and carrying, and later horse-raking of the stubble before gleaners were let in all changed what was left (*qualitative*). No figure was found here.

## 4. How it was policed

- **Village bylaws** in the manor court: who may glean (not those who could earn the harvest wage), and when (after carrying, not at night) (Ault; the Islip and Wymeswold bylaws, §1).
- **A bell** opening and closing the day (§2).
- **Prosecution for trespass** after 1788, which by King's account did not end the custom (§1; *search summary*).
- **In France, penal sanctions** for gleaning early or by night (§1).

## 5. What it depended on (qualitative)

- **The harvest and how it was taken.** More is left by a hurried or poorly equipped reaping, less by raking.
- **Who else had claims on the stubble:** grazing animals, and the farmer's own raking.
- **Who counted as the place's poor,** and whether wage work was to be had at the same time.
- **The polity's or the landholder's leave,** which custom could grant, restrict or withdraw.

## 6. TCE rules (proposed)

- **Reaping leaves a share of the crop on the field.** It is a crop's content value, a *design prior* of about 2 % (§3: 2–3 % derived from one parish estimate; the Mishnah's corner, 1/60, is of the same order). What reaping leaves is what gleaning can recover. Left alone it is lost, like ripe grain left standing.
- **Gleaning is gathering.** It is chosen like any other gathering of food, by what it brings a household's food for the hours. Its rate is a *design prior* (§3: about 1 kg an hour, highly uncertain). It is done only after the crop is carried and only by daylight, as all gathering is.
- **Who may glean is custom.** In the first slice anyone in reach may, as under the oldest rule. Restricting it to the poor (the bylaws' wage test), or barring it, is for a later slice. It would come as a polity's law or a landholder's claim, not authored, and the observer would record it.
- **What is not built:** the corner deliberately left (peah), which is a gift, not a loss; raking by the farmer; grazing the stubble.
