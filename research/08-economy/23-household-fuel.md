# Household fuel: gathering, buying, going without, and fuel against food, for The Civilization Engine

## Executive conclusion

**Household fuel is well documented as a burden and as a budget item, and thinly as a choice set against food.** This short note was written for one gap (plan §8, question 18, item 5). The fuel-at-market slice (§7, "Fuel offered at market") left two lines of "Not yet" that need grounding the index lacked: fuel weighed with hunger when food waits on a fire, and fuel sold beyond the household (coal and charcoal to works). Research 08-16 covers cities' fuel hinterlands, and 03-06 covers woody biomass and collection rates. Neither covers how a household chooses between gathering, buying and going without.

1. **Gathering is costly work, done mostly by women and children.**
   - Reported collection times range from about 7 hours a week to several hours a day, by place and season.
   - Distance and time are poor measures of scarcity. Where woodland degrades, households can switch to wood from their own trees instead of walking further.
2. **Buying is common where time is dear or the commons is closed.**
   - Household economics models fuel demand by income, price and the opportunity cost of collecting time, usually proxied by a wage.
   - Many households hold several fuels at once ("stacking") and do not climb a single "ladder".
3. **Poor households were protected by custom and charity, not markets alone.**
   - In England, enclosure awards often set aside fuel allotments (turbary, furze, "poor's firing land") for the settled poor.
   - Parishes used the rents to cart or buy fuel for those who could not cut it themselves.
   - Late-18th-century labourers' budgets carry fuel as a distinct, modest item, partly gathered free.
4. **When fuel is short, households change how they cook before they stop eating.**
   - They cook fewer sessions (composite meals), prefer quick-cooking foods, and undercook or skip meals.
   - Fuel-hungry foods (meat, fish, pulses) are cut first.
   - The evidence is mostly modern and regional, much of it from summaries (*uncertain for the past*).
5. **Quantities.**
   - Cooking on an open fire is reported as Specific Fuel Consumption, kilograms of wood per kilogram of cooked food. The few figures found are of the order of 0.5 kg/kg for one fried food. No clean figure for porridge or bread was found (*uncertain*).
   - 08-16 and 03-06 already give per-person daily use of about 1–2 kg in mild climates and up to 7–10 kg in Scandinavian estimates, and collection rates of a few kilograms an hour.

**Conclusion for TCE:** what the engine already does is grounded in kind:
- fuel is gathered as work, kept for a few days, and bought from neighbours, worth the hours it saves;
- food that waits on a fire is not food in hand.

The missing piece is the household's choice when both fuel and food run short. Evidence points to adaptation of cooking (fewer and quicker cooked meals) before hunger, and to fuel bought or given before food is given up. Fuel sold to works belongs to 08-16's hinterland and 07-12's industrial demand, not to the household note.

Throughout, **measured** or primary findings are kept apart from **estimates**. Most sources were reached only through search-engine summaries in this session. They are marked (*search summary*), and none is load-bearing.

## 1. Gathering

**Time.**
- A study reported by the Clean Cooking Alliance put the time women in India spend collecting firewood at 374 hours a year, about 7 hours a week (2015; *search summary*: [Clean Cooking Alliance](https://cleancooking.org/news/05-05-2015-women-spend-374-hours-each-year-collecting-firewood-in-india-study-finds)).
- Other figures cited by the same body are about 3.5 hours a day in Kenya and up to 6 hours a day in rural Uganda (*search summary*).
- The figures differ by a factor of several, by place, season and how "collection" is counted (*uncertain*).

**Who.** Women hold the primary responsibility for fuel collection in rural Ethiopia, by a gender-disaggregated time-allocation study (CGIAR working paper, 2015; *search summary*: [CGIAR](https://gender.cgiar.org/publications/time-allocation-energy-resource-collection-rural-ethiopia-gender-disaggregated-1)). A rural Nigerian project report found most sampled women collected twice a week, within 2 km, and that men did not normally collect (2018; *search summary*: [Rufford report](https://ruffordorg.s3.amazonaws.com/media/project_reports/21222-1%20January%202018.pdf)).

**Scarcity does not simply lengthen the walk.** In Madhya Pradesh villages with degraded forests, households did not spend longer searching but switched to wood from private trees. Distance and collection time "are not reliable indicators of fuelwood shortages" (*search summary* via [Springer, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10668-021-01443-8) and related household studies).

**Rates.** Research 03-06 §2.5 already records 5.7 kg per person-hour collected in rural Malawi (an estimated baseline). It also records 1.8 kg per person per day of domestic use in a Nepali winter sample.

## 2. Buying, and holding several fuels

- **Opportunity cost.** The World Bank and ESMAP study of household fuel use in Guatemala frames firewood demand by income, market prices and "the opportunity costs of firewood collection". It notes that many urban households keep using wood, "which is not a cheap fuel when it has to be purchased" (ESMAP, *Household Fuel Use and Fuel Switching in Guatemala*, 2003; *search summary*: [ESMAP](https://www.esmap.org/sites/esmap.org/files/03603.%20Household%20Fuel%20Use%20and%20Fuel%20Switching%20in%20Guatemala%20June%202003.pdf)).
- **Stacking, not a ladder.** In peri-urban Kathmandu, stacking several fuels is linked to the time cost of collecting firewood, to wealth and to women's empowerment (Duke Energy Access, 2022; *search summary*: [Duke](https://energyaccess.duke.edu/publication/fuel-stacking-implications-for-willingness-to-pay-for-cooking-fuels-in-peri-urban-kathmandu-valley-nepal/)).
- **Price shocks move time.** In Malawi, electricity price rises were associated with more time spent by women and girls collecting firewood (2025; *search summary*).
- **Measurement.** Studies usually proxy the value of collecting time with a wage rate, because measuring it directly is costly (*search summary*). TCE's own rule, fuel worth the hours of the household's own work it saves, is that proxy without the wage.

## 3. Custom and charity for the poor's fuel (England)

- **Rights of turbary and furze.**
  - Cottagers commonly cut turf, furze and wood on commons. The manorial court set how much could be taken (*search summary*: [Bournemouth heritage](https://www.streets-of-bournemouth.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/History.Heritage.pdf)).
  - At Christchurch, the enclosure commissioners assigned 424 acres in five areas as turbary to 86 qualifying cottages (same source).
- **Fuel allotments at enclosure.** Great Ryburgh's award (Norfolk, 1810) set land aside where the legally settled poor could cut turf, whins or furze. The rent from its herbage paid to cart fuel to those unable to cut it themselves (*search summary*: [St Andrew's Great Ryburgh](https://standrewsgreatryburgh.org.uk/a-cruciform-church-with-a-round-tower/meet-the-villagers-at-he-time-of-inclosure-1810/the-fuel-allotment-for-great-ryburgh.php)).
- **Charities.**
  - At Great Ellingham the overseers gave fuel allowances in 1804, and used the "Poor's Firing Land" income to buy coal in winter. This later became a fuel allotment charity (*search summary*: [Great Ellingham](https://greatellingham.org/?p=2928)).
  - Such allotments were still disputed in Parliament in 1909 (HC Deb 24 May 1909, "Poor's Furze, Berkshire": [Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/24/poors-furze-berkshire)).
- **Objection to enclosure.** At Mortimer (1801–1806) the vicar objected that the numerous poor had cut fuel on the common "from time immemorial" (*search summary*: [Mortimer village](https://www.mortimervillage.org.uk/sites/mvp/files/MTTA/The_Enclosure_of_Mortimer_Common_1801-1806.pdf)).
- **Budgets.**
  - Eden's *State of the Poor* (1797) records labourers' yearly expenses by item, fuel among them (full text: [Wellcome Collection](https://content.www.wellcomecollection.org/works/np3adwwu)).
  - One summary of his budgets gives fuel at about £1 to £1.70 a year against family incomes of tens of pounds, and notes that much fuel was gathered free (*search summary*: [johndclare.net](https://johndclare.net/KS3/3-1-1.htm)).
  - A Cumberland family's fuel is roughly a tenth of the items listed there (*derived from a summary, uncertain*; Gazeley and Verdon, *Explorations in Economic History* 51 (2014), compare Eden's and Davies's budgets and were not read).

These are local examples, not a systematic survey. They show the shape: fuel for the poor came by right, by allotment and by charity as well as by purchase, and a polity (parish) could provide it.

## 4. Fuel against food

- **Cooking less often.** In Tigania, Meru County (Kenya), about 81 % of 239 surveyed households answered energy shortages by cooking composite meals in fewer sessions. They preferred quick-cooking foods, often of lower nutritional value (2016–2017 survey; *search summary*: [TTU repository](https://ir.ttu.ac.ke/handle/123456789/73)).
- **Skipping and undercooking.**
  - Reporting that cites a WFP analysis says households facing dear fuel trade food for fuel, skip meals or undercook, and cut protein first, since meat and fish need more energy (*search summary*: [UCL opinion, 2022](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2022/may/opinion-cooking-fuels-become-more-expensive-people-are-turning-dirtier-alternatives)). The primary WFP source was not read.
  - In Nairobi's Mukuru settlement (2022), gas-cooking days fell from 19 to 8 a month after price rises, and half of families skipped more meals. This is a preprint reported in the press (*search summary*: [Down to Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/food/drought-and-the-war-in-ukraine-changed-what-families-in-kenya-could-afford-to-eat-research-tracked-food-and-cooking-gas-use)).
- **Fuel given as food is.** The HAPIN trial, in which households received a stove and free fuel for 18 months, concludes that energy security can be "nutrition-sensitive" (*search summary*: [medRxiv, 2023](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/medrxiv/early/2023/07/12/2023.07.09.23292423.full.pdf)).
- **For the past:** no direct pre-industrial evidence was found in this session. That households adapted cooking before going hungry is an inference from modern studies (*uncertain*).

## 5. Quantities

- **Fuel per food.**
  - Specific Fuel Consumption is the standard measure: wood burned over the weight of cooked food (FAO; *search summary*: [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/Q1085e/q1085e0b.htm)).
  - One study of a fried sweet found about 0.5 kg of fuelwood per kg of food (*search summary*), the order of TCE's 0.5 kg of fuel per kg of flour baked (a tuning value).
  - No figure for porridge or bread on an open hearth was found (*uncertain*).
- **Fuel per person per day.** About 1 kg a day in southern Europe against 7–10 kg in Scandinavian estimates (research 08-16's table, after Sieferle, Malanima and Warde). TCE's 0.7–1.5 kg by month is inside the mild end.
- **The share of a budget.** Fuel was a modest item in late-18th-century English labourers' budgets, of the order of a tenth where bought (§3; *derived, uncertain*).

## 6. TCE rules (proposed)

- **Kept as built:** fuel is gathered as work; a household keeps a few days of it (`fuel_target_days`); it offers what it holds beyond that; and one short of it buys from neighbours, worth the hours of its own work saved (§2: the opportunity-cost rule without a wage).
- **Fuel against food (the "Not yet"):** a household short of both weighs fuel by the meals it makes possible, not only by hours saved. Food that waits on a fire counts as food only with the fuel to cook it. Before going hungry, a household cooks fewer and quicker meals: a lower fuel per day when fuel is short, down to a floor (*design prior*; §4).
- **Custom and the polity:** fuel by right on a common, a fuel allotment or a polity's store giving fuel as relief are lawful forms the polity may make (§3), not authored. They are for later slices.
- **Not the household's:** charcoal and coal sold to smithies and works, and fuel carted between settlements, belong to 08-16's hinterlands and 07-12's industrial demand.
- **Uncertain throughout:** collection times and the choices in §4 are modern and regional. Treat every figure as a *design prior* and every rule as plausible, not proven.
