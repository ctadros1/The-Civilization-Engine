# Fuel by dish on an open hearth: wood per kilogram of food and per meal, for The Civilization Engine

## Executive conclusion

**What a dish costs in wood on an open fire is measured in modern cooking tests, and only in outline for the past.** This short note answers one gap (plan §8, question 20, item 6). Research 08-23 §5 found no figure for porridge or bread on an open hearth. Question 20's item 11 (a meal tied to its fuel, then a hearth that cooks leaner when fuel is short) needs one.

The standard measure is **Specific Fuel Consumption (SFC)**: the dry wood burned to cook a batch, over the weight of the cooked food, in kilograms per kilogram. It is lower for large batches, for dishes that cook quickly, and for enclosed stoves.

1. **Quick starch dishes cost least; pulses and meat cost most.** Measured on three-stone fires in Kenyan school kitchens, for about 150 eaters a batch (Adkins et al. 2010):
   - Maize stiff porridge (ugali) took 0.13–0.59 kg of wood per kg cooked and about an hour.
   - Thin porridge took 0.35 kg/kg.
   - Greens took 0.20–0.46 kg/kg.
   - Maize and beans boiled together (nyoyo) took 0.53–0.65 kg/kg and about four and a half hours.
   - Beef took 0.76 kg/kg.
   - All foods together averaged 0.45–0.46 kg/kg.
2. **A household meal costs about a third of a kilogram of wood per person.** A complete meal for about five people took 1.56 kg of dry wood (95 % interval 1.16–1.96) and 50 minutes on a three-stone fire in Malawi (Malakini and Maganga 2011). The meal was maize stiff porridge, cabbage and small fish. A rocket stove took 0.69 kg.
3. **Bread in an oven costs 0.5–0.9 kg of wood per kilogram of flour.**
   - Measured in Mozambican bakeries, it was 0.55 kg/kg in indirect ovens and 0.90 kg/kg in semi-direct ones (Manhica, Lucas and Richards 2012).
   - FAO's rough rural figures are higher: 1.0–2.0 kg per kg of bread, back-calculated from totals.
   - An oven is costly to heat once: experimental clay ovens take two to four hours to come to heat. The Haithabu reconstruction concluded such ovens were kept going for many uses rather than lit for one bake.
4. **Smoking and drying with fire is the dearest use of all.**
   - Traditional fish smoking takes 2–12 kg of wood per kg of fish (FAO), about 2.3 kg/kg in one measured traditional session.
   - Improved kilns bring this below 1 kg/kg.
   - Parboiling rice (2–5 kg/kg) and roasting gari (3–4 kg/kg) are of the same order (FAO, rough).
5. **For the past, nothing per dish was found.** Experimental archaeology measures hearths and ovens (temperatures, hours to heat, wood over a day), not wood per pot of pottage. That households cooked as modern open-fire cooks do is an assumption (*uncertain*).

**Conclusion for TCE:** a meal's fuel can stand on a figure.
- **Boiled grain, pottage and greens:** about 0.3–0.5 kg of wood per kg of cooked food on an open fire.
- **Pulses, or grain and pulses together:** about 0.6 kg/kg, taking about four times as long.
- **Meat:** about 0.75 kg/kg.
- **Per person per meal:** about 0.3 kg.
- **Two meals a day:** about 0.6–0.7 kg a person, if both are boiled dishes. That is of the order of TCE's summer burn (0.7 kg a person a day), but TCE's people do not eat that way. *Corrected 2026-11-20, measured in a lived world:* over four years of river_valley seed 1, households ate about 55 % of their food as bread (which carries its own fuel in its recipe), 23–51 % as provisions eaten as they are, and 5–21 % as groats. The boiled dishes' wood at these figures was 6–13 % of what the hearth burned, month by month. The hearth's daily figure is mostly the fire's warmth and other uses, not two cooked meals (plan §9, "A meal tied to its fuel, measured and not built").
- **Bread:** TCE's 0.5 kg of wood per kg of flour baked sits at the low end of what bakeries measure.

Throughout, **measured** findings are kept apart from **estimates** and from figures reached only through search-engine summaries, which are marked (*search summary*). The three measured studies were read in full in this session; FAO's table was read on its page.

## 1. The measure

- **Specific Fuel Consumption** is the dry-equivalent wood burned for one batch over the weight of the cooked food. It is the standard output of the Controlled Cooking Test (Bailis 2004), and it is dimensionless (kg/kg) (Adkins et al. 2010; Malakini and Maganga 2011).
  - It counts the water in the food, so a porridge, which is mostly water, scores low per kilogram cooked.
  - Per kilogram of dry grain, a porridge's figure is several times higher (*derived*: by the porridge's water, about three to four parts in four).
- **What moves it:**
  - batch size (a large pot loses less heat per kilogram);
  - cooking time (pulses and tough meat need long simmering);
  - the stove (an open three-stone fire is about 10–15 % efficient; enclosed stoves save 15–50 %);
  - the cook's tending of the fire, which makes three-stone figures vary widely between tests.
- **What it does not count:** wood burned for warmth, light or water heating outside the dish. Daily totals per person (research 08-23 §5, 08-16, 03-06) include those.

## 2. Boiled dishes on an open fire (measured)

**Adkins et al. (2010), *Energy for Sustainable Development*, rural Kenyan schools (Millennium Villages).** Three-stone fires against rocket stoves, cooking for about 150 pupils a batch. Mean SFC on the three-stone fire, kg of wood per kg of cooked food:

| Dish | Paired tests | Unpaired tests | Cooking time, paired (min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nyoyo (maize and beans) | 0.65 (n = 8) | 0.53 | 262 |
| Ugali (maize stiff porridge) | 0.13 (n = 8) | 0.59 | 57 |
| Beef | 0.76 (n = 4) | – | 142 |
| Kale | 0.46 (n = 10) | – | 46 |
| Vegetables | – | 0.20 | – |
| Tea | – | 0.48 | – |
| Porridge | – | 0.35 | – |
| All foods | 0.46 | 0.45 | 123 |

- Rocket stoves cut SFC by about a third (all foods 0.28 paired, 0.17 unpaired), with no longer cooking.
- Institutional batches are larger than a household's, so a household pot burns more per kilogram (*derived*).
- Ugali's two figures differ fourfold between the paired and unpaired sets (n = 8 against 2). Quick dishes are the least well measured.

**Malakini and Maganga (2011), MPRA paper 33866, Lilongwe District, Malawi.**
- **The meal:** a complete meal for an average household of about five, cooked three times on each stove: nsima (maize stiff porridge), cabbage and *usipa* (small fish).
- **Three-stone fire:** 1,558 g of equivalent dry wood (95 % interval 1,156–1,960 g), 50 minutes (90 % interval 43–56).
- **Other stoves:** Chitetezo mbaula 902 g and 46 minutes; rocket stove 689 g and 43 minutes.
- **Per person:** about 0.31 kg of wood per meal on the open fire (*derived*).

**Beans and other slow dishes (*search summary*).**
- Unsoaked dry beans take one to three hours of boiling, and longer at altitude (extension guides).
- Long cooking limits how often beans are eaten where wood is scarce.
- In Rwanda, a faster-cooking bean cultivar needed 16 % less firewood and 8 % less time, and a hay-basket cooker 40 % less wood (a Michigan State dissertation).
- Research 08-23 §4 records households cutting the fuel-hungry foods first.

## 3. Bread and the oven

- **Measured (Manhica, Lucas and Richards 2012, *Applied Thermal Engineering* 47: 63–72; Mozambican bakeries, 15 semi-direct and 3 indirect):** 0.55 kg of wood per kg of wheat flour baked in indirect ovens and 0.90 kg/kg in semi-direct ones.
- **FAO's rural figures (*rough, back-calculated*):**
  - bread 1.0–2.0 kg of wood per kg of bread;
  - tortillas 1.25–2.5 kg/kg;
  - Sudanese brick ovens about 0.8 kg per kg of flour (*search summary*).
  - Energypedia gives more than 0.5–1 kg per kg of flour for traditional ovens (*search summary*).
  - FAO's table warns that its figures "have not been thoroughly measured" and were "mostly calculated backward" from totals.
- **Heating an oven (experimental archaeology):**
  - The Haithabu clay oven reconstruction (Lindenberg 2020, *EXARC Journal* 2020/2) took about four hours to reach 360 °C and burned 45.1 kg of lime wood over a 24-hour cycle of use. It never fell below about 130 °C while in use.
  - The author concludes that lighting such an oven for one bake is "very ineffective", and that ovens were kept going for many purposes: baking, slow cooking, and drying grain, fruit and herbs overnight.
  - A reconstructed Late Iron Age oven at the Scottish Crannog Centre took about 8 kg of wood and two hours to reach 250 °C, then held heat for most of the day (museum blog, *search summary*).
- **For TCE:**
  - Hearth baking at 0.5 kg of wood per kg of flour (`bake_bread`) is at the low end of the bakeries' measured range.
  - An oven's 3 kg a session (`bake_bread_oven`) stands for heating once and baking a batch, which the oven experiments support in kind. Neither gives a per-loaf figure.

## 4. Smoking, drying and processing with fire

- **Smoked fish:**
  - FAO's table gives 0.2–16 kg of wood per kg of fish, and the commonly cited traditional range is 2–12 kg/kg, smoking and drying over 10–20 hours in mud ovens of 50–600 kg.
  - One measured traditional session in southwest Cameroon's mangroves burned 1,205 kg of wood for 528 kg of fish (about 2.3 kg/kg), against 122 kg for 160 kg (about 0.76 kg/kg) in a cinder-block oven (*Tropical Conservation Science* 12, 2019; *search summary*).
  - FAO's FTT-Thiaroye kiln is reported to bring 5 kg/kg down to 0.8 (*search summary*).
- **Other processing (FAO, rough):** parboiled rice 2.0–5.0 kg/kg; roasted gari 3.0–4.0 kg/kg; beer 0.4–1.0 kg of wood per litre.
- **For TCE:** `dry_fish` and `dry_meat` charge 4 kg of firewood a session. A session's fish at the table's ranges would take several times that. TCE's drying is a session's smoke over a share of the catch, not a full smoke-cure, so the figure is a tuning choice, not a contradiction (*a reading, uncertain*).

## 5. The past

- **No pre-industrial per-dish figure was found** in this session. Experimental archaeology of hearths and cooking pots reports temperatures, durations and pot behaviour:
  - Iberian Iron Age hearths kept a pot boiling and food in it for at least six hours (EXARC, *search summary*);
  - stone-boiling was faster to a boil but took more fuel and attention than a pot on the fire (*search summary*).
- Pottage simmered for hours on a fire kept in for warmth is the European pattern. The marginal wood of the dish is then small against the fire's own burn (*inference, uncertain*).
- What open-fire cooking costs depends on the pot, the batch and the cook more than on the era. Modern three-stone figures are the best stand-in (*uncertain*).

## 6. TCE rules (proposed)

- **A meal's fuel by its dish** (q20, item 11): a cooked meal takes wood by its dish, of the order of these design priors:

  | Dish | Wood per kg of cooked food | Time |
  |---|---|---|
  | Porridge, pottage or greens | 0.35 kg | about an hour |
  | Pulses, or grain and pulses together | 0.6 kg | about four hours |
  | Meat boiled | 0.75 kg | |
  | Bread from the oven or the hearth | 0.5–0.9 kg per kg of flour, as built | |

  A household of five's meal is about 1.5 kg on an open fire.
- **What the hearth keeps:** `fuel_kg_per_person_day` (0.7 kg in summer, 1.5 in winter) is the fire's warmth and its other uses, with a small part for the boiled dishes (6–13 % in a lived world; §1's correction). The warmth part stays a daily burn by the weather. Only the cooking part could follow the dish.
- **Fewer and quicker meals when short** (08-23 §4): a household short of fuel cooks the dishes that cost least first (porridge before pulses or meat), and fewer of them. The burn then falls by what the dishes skipped would have taken, not by a share fixed in advance.
- **Uncertain throughout:** the figures are modern, tropical and mostly from institutional or test cooking. Treat every figure as a *design prior* and every rule as plausible, not proven.

## 7. The dishes' figures checked, once built (2026-11-20)

TCE's core content now charges a boiled dish its wood by the dry kilogram eaten (`cook_wood_kg`; plan §9, "Dishes skipped when the hearth is short"): groats 1.4, meat 0.75, fish 0.6. Each was read again against §2 and one new measure.

- **Groats, and four parts cooked to one dry.**
  - Porridge's water was measured by energy. USDA's plain oatmeal cooked with water gives 71 kcal per 100 g, against 379 kcal for the dry oats (FoodData Central 173905 and 173904; read through a recipe database's copy of the records). A thin porridge is about **5.3 parts cooked to one dry**.
  - A stiff porridge such as ugali is about 2.5–3 parts to one, and whole grain boiled about 3. Those are cooks' and food tables' usual figures (*not re-read this session*).
  - Four to one lies between a stiff porridge and a thin one. At §2's measured range (ugali 0.13–0.59 kg a kilogram cooked, thin porridge 0.35), a dry kilogram takes about **0.4–1.9 kg** of wood (*derived*).
  - TCE's 1.4 kg sits inside that range, toward a household's smaller pot (*holds*).
- **Meat.** Beef boiled took 0.76 kg of wood a kilogram cooked (Adkins et al. 2010). TCE's 0.75 kg a kilogram of meat matches it.
  - A stew weighs more than the raw meat in it, so per raw kilogram the figure would be no lower (*derived*). Meat *holds*.
- **Fish.** No measured figure for fish boiled or stewed on an open fire was found. The only fish measures in reach are for smoking (§4) and for fish stoves (an SNV Ghana controlled cooking test, whose numbers were not read).
  - Fish cooks in a quarter to half an hour, like greens (0.20–0.46 kg a kilogram, 46 minutes for kale), not like beef (142 minutes).
  - So cooking time argues for about **0.4** rather than TCE's 0.6 (*inference, uncertain*).
  - With nothing measured to correct it to, the 0.6 stays as a design prior and is marked unmeasured.

**For TCE:** groats and meat stand on measured ranges. Fish is a prior with no measure behind it, and the evidence of time is that it is on the high side.
