# State formation and political evolution for The Civilization Engine

## Executive conclusion

**TCE should make states emerge from durable relationships of obligation, protection, coordination, and enforcement—not from population milestones or an institution technology tree.**

The strongest generalization is conditional: larger populations and territories create opportunities for specialization, but maintaining political integration requires organizations that can obtain resources, process information, resolve disputes, and survive changes of leadership. Those organizations can develop through cooperation, coercion, or mixtures of both. They can also fail to develop, remain decentralized, or disappear. Comparative research supports recurring relationships among these dimensions, not one inevitable trajectory. [DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-39d5-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)

For TCE, use the following **working definitions**, rather than treating disputed anthropological categories as natural laws:

| Concept | Operational meaning for TCE |
| --- | --- |
| **Governance** | Rules, expectations, arbitration, and collective action. It can exist without a central government. |
| **Chiefdom-like organization** | Several communities recognize a continuing central leadership, often embedded in kinship, personal allegiance, and privileged access to resources. |
| **State-like organization** | Durable governing offices administer obligations and disputes beyond a single community, mobilize resources regularly, and exercise territorially differentiated authority. |
| **State capacity** | What those institutions can actually accomplish: collect obligations, deliver decisions, maintain infrastructure, supply forces, and secure compliance. |
| **Regime** | Who controls institutions, how officeholders are selected, and how they are constrained. |

These definitions deliberately separate **political scale, administrative capacity, and concentration of power**. A large tributary alliance need not possess strong administration; a small polity can have specialized offices; an effective public organization need not be an autocracy.

“Starting without government” should therefore mean **no standing central offices or compulsory central treasury**, not people without social relationships, customary expectations, or ways to settle disagreements.

## 1. Mechanisms: why chiefdoms and states form

### 1.1 Surplus supports specialists; appropriability determines who can command it

Producing more than immediate consumption requirements makes sustained non-food-producing work possible. But productive land alone does not explain why a particular household, council, temple, or ruler gains control.

Mayshar, Moav, and Pascali’s *The Origin of the State* argues that the crucial distinction is **appropriability**: cereal harvests are comparatively observable, concentrated in time, and storable, making them easier to collect than dispersed or continuously harvested resources. Their empirical argument favors this mechanism over agricultural productivity alone. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/718372?journalCode=jpe)

This is **contested, not settled**. Cook and colleagues’ 2026 comment finds that the result is narrower than originally claimed and sensitive to a small number of outlying observations. TCE should incorporate appropriability as a plausible mechanism, not encode “cereals cause states.” [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/740225)

**Implementable rule.** Track separately:

* physical production;
* household consumption, seed, and reserves;
* visibility of production to potential collectors;
* storage and transport costs;
* ownership and access rights;
* collectors’ practical ability to intercept goods or labor.

For a household, define an accounting quantity:

\[
S\_h = Y\_h-C\_h-\text{seed}\_h-\Delta\text{desired reserves}\_h
\]

Here \(S\_h\) is a **potentially transferable balance**, not an inviolable tax ceiling. An institution can demand more than this balance, but doing so must reduce consumption, seed, reserves, or productive assets and generate downstream consequences.

**Political implication:** access to productive resources can fund followers; followers can enforce access; this feedback can concentrate power. Conversely, households can pool surplus to support a constrained public office.

### 1.2 Circumscription matters through the cost of exit

Carneiro’s circumscription theory links warfare and political subordination to situations in which defeated groups cannot easily relocate. Barriers may be environmental, such as surrounding desert, or social, such as neighboring occupied territories. Its useful contribution is conditional: military defeat produces more durable subordination when escape is costly. It is not a universal explanation of state origins. [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.169.3947.733)

**Implementable rule.** Give households and communities an explicit outside option:

\[
U\_{\text{exit}} =
U\_{\text{best reachable alternative}}
-\text{moving costs}
-\text{lost local assets}
-\text{separation costs}
-\text{migration risk}
\]

Compare this with remaining under existing obligations.

Circumscription should emerge from **reachable land, carrying capacity, access rights, competitors, transport, and social ties**. Do not use a single “circumscribed valley” bonus.

The same defeat can consequently produce flight, temporary tribute, permanent subordination, or negotiated federation. The difference lies in alternatives and enforcement, not a scripted outcome.

### 1.3 Conflict creates incentives for integration—but can also destroy it

Military competition can favor larger coalitions, reliable provisioning, delegated command, and resource collection. Turchin and colleagues’ large comparative analysis identifies agricultural productivity and military technologies as important predictors of increasing social complexity. However, these are model-based comparative results, not proof that every state originated in war; the importance of iron and cavalry for later Eurasian expansion cannot become prerequisites for American state formation. [Peter Turchin](https://peterturchin.com/publications/disentangling-the-evolutionary-drivers-of-social-complexity-a-comprehensive-test-of-hypotheses)

There is also more localized archaeological evidence. Stanish and Levine associate destruction and decline at Taraco with the expansion of Pukara in the northern Titicaca Basin, interpreting the sequence as evidence for warfare’s role in early state formation. The interpretation is strong enough to inform a mechanism, but destruction evidence does not independently establish every political step. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51520572_War_and_early_state_formation_in_the_northern_Titicaca_Basin_Peru)

**Implementable rule.** Distinguish four activities:

1. raiding for movable resources;
2. collective defense;
3. imposing recurring tribute;
4. governing conquered communities.

Each has different staffing, information, and supply requirements. A successful army should not automatically create functioning administration.

A prospective conqueror should evaluate expected tribute **after occupation, transport, enforcement, and coalition-maintenance costs**. Local leaders may remain as intermediaries; that reduces immediate administrative costs but preserves potential secession leaders.

### 1.4 Coordination can create government without an initial conquering elite

Allen, Bertazzini, and Heldring use river shifts in ancient southern Iraq as a natural experiment. When a river moved away, communities lost convenient irrigation and needed coordinated canal construction. Such shifts were followed by government formation, canals, and tribute, supporting a cooperative origin in that setting. This is unusually valuable evidence because it distinguishes competing mechanisms more directly than a simple cross-sectional correlation. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20201919)

But irrigation does not necessarily require a centralized ruler. Lansing and colleagues’ work on Bali demonstrates how local scheduling decisions and water–pest feedbacks can produce coordinated irrigation patterns without a central optimizer. That does **not** mean Bali lacked states; it means that successful irrigation coordination is not itself evidence of despotic control. [Stockholm Resilience Centre](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/publications/publications/2018-06-28-adaptive-self-organization-of-balis-ancient-rice-terraces.html)

**Implementable rule.** Let agents propose arrangements for shared problems: canal maintenance, defense, storage, markets, or arbitration. An arrangement persists when enough participants expect its benefits to exceed its costs and when it can manage nonparticipation.

Possible outcomes should include rotating coordinators, councils, associations, hereditary managers, and coercive offices. Successful coordination creates an opportunity for power accumulation—not a guarantee of it.

### 1.5 Trade can finance political integration and create alternative power centers

Research on Taraco links local and long-distance exchange to its development as an early political center. This supports trade as one contributor, alongside conflict, rather than an exclusive explanation. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260943616_Trade_and_Early_State_Formation_in_the_Northern_Titicaca_Basin_Peru)

**Implementable rule.** Political opportunities arise where actors can provide or control valuable services:

* protection and dispute settlement;
* access to a port, crossing, or market;
* reliable weights, storage, and credit;
* distribution of scarce prestige goods.

Income from these activities can fund retainers and offices. However, merchants can also finance councils or rival centers. A route’s relocation or a successful bypass should weaken organizations that depend on its revenues.

Avoid a generic “trade increases centralization” coefficient. Model **who captures the returns and what alternatives traders possess**.

### 1.6 Revenue sources influence bargaining and accountability

Blanton and colleagues’ comparative research emphasizes that some premodern governments depended heavily on broadly distributed taxpayer production and reciprocal public services, while others relied more on concentrated or externally controlled revenues. Their framework connects this difference to bargaining, accountability, and the possibilities for collective government. Such arrangements were not confined to modern Europe. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.568704/full)

**Implementable rule.** Identify whose cooperation an institution needs to survive. Those contributors acquire bargaining power when withholding contributions is credible.

A ruler funded by many independent households faces a different negotiation problem from a ruler controlling a mine, trade bottleneck, or conquered tributary. Neither arrangement mechanically determines its regime, but it changes the coalition necessary to govern.

Track legitimacy **by constituency and institution**. A court may remain trusted after a ruler loses support; merchants and cultivators may evaluate the same government differently.

### 1.7 Expansion creates an information and delegation problem

Spencer’s territorial-expansion account proposes that extending control beyond a ruler’s practical reach encourages specialized delegation. Delegating all authority to a subordinate risks creating an independent rival; delegating limited functions can extend administration while reducing that risk. His comparison concerns six regions of primary state formation. The direction of causality remains debatable, but the organizational problem is useful. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43130989_Territorial_expansion_and_primary_state_formation)

**Implementable rule.** Government generates work: assessing obligations, recording deliveries, hearing disputes, inspecting infrastructure, carrying messages, and organizing forces.

For each service:

\[
B\_{t+1}=\max(0,\ B\_t+A\_t-Q\_t)
\]

where \(B\) is backlog, \(A\) is newly arriving work, and \(Q\) is completed work, in compatible task units.

Rising backlogs should create incentives to hire specialists, improve records, standardize procedures, delegate locally, or abandon some responsibilities. Every solution has costs and political consequences.

**Do not give “bureaucracy” a universal efficiency multiplier.** Make it a set of staffed processes that can improve some tasks while introducing overhead, corruption, and coordination failures.

## 2. Institution sequences: a partial order, not a ladder

### What Seshat establishes—and what it does not

Turchin and colleagues’ foundational Seshat analysis examined **414 societies in 30 regions**, using **51 variables** grouped into broader characteristics. A dominant statistical dimension explained approximately **75% of variation**. This supports substantial co-variation among scale and institutional characteristics; it does not establish a universal order of invention. [DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-39d5-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)

Shin and colleagues find a recurring pattern of **growth in political scale, followed by greater information-processing and economic sophistication, followed by renewed scale growth**. Their thresholds concern multivariate developmental trajectories, not a rule such as “writing appears at 10,000 people.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32409638/)

Seshat also separates variables that games often conflate: professional soldiers, military officers, full-time bureaucrats, judges, courts, formal legal codes, scripts, and nonwritten records. TCE should retain these distinctions. [Seshat DB](https://www.seshat-db.com/core/polity/134)

### Recommended institutional dependency structure

The following is a **modeling synthesis**, not an empirically universal chronology.

| Institutional function | Plausible early form | What encourages specialization | What should **not** be mandatory |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dispute resolution | Negotiation, respected mediators, assemblies | Repeated disputes, appeals, strangers interacting, enforcement problems | Writing, a courthouse, or a king |
| Collective defense | Temporary coalition and mobilized households | Repeated threats, longer campaigns, specialist weapons and command | A standing army |
| Resource mobilization | Gifts, subscriptions, project contributions, tribute | Recurring expenditure and enforceable obligations | Coinage or a sophisticated census |
| Leadership | Task-specific organizer or coalition broker | Repeated success, control of assets, durable followership | Hereditary succession |
| Accounting | Witnesses, tallies, tokens, remembered obligations | More contributors, commodities, transactions, and administrative levels | Full phonetic writing |
| Administration | Household retainers or part-time officers | Workload, territorial reach, accumulated procedures | Merit examinations or salaried careers |
| Courts and legal administration | Regular hearings and recognized jurisdiction | Case volume, appeals, specialization, stronger enforcement | A comprehensive written code |
| Professional military | Retainers, guards, specialist officers | Reliable recurring revenue and provisioning | Full professionalization of all combatants |
| Territorial government | Tribute agreements and delegated local authority | Benefits from regular assessment and policy implementation | Uniform laws or complete central control |

The key dependency is usually **a funded and socially supported function**, not another named institution.

### Historical checks against rigid sequencing

**Accounting is older than many forms of writing.** Schmandt-Besserat traces Near Eastern accounting tokens to approximately **8000–3500 BCE**, followed by transformations into tablet-based recording in the fourth millennium BCE. The exact developmental interpretation should not become a universal writing model, but the existence of administrative recording before mature script is a useful distinction. [University Blog Service](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/the-evolution-of-writing/)

**Complex administration need not use conventional writing.** Urton and Brezine’s analysis of **21 Inka khipu from Puruchuco** identifies accounting relationships across administrative levels. TCE should model information media by their capabilities, not make a phonetic script the sole gateway to advanced administration. Whether particular khipu conveyed additional linguistic information is a separate debate. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7660516_Khipu_Accounting_in_Ancient_Peru)

**Agricultural intensification can follow political organization.** A study of **155 Austronesian-speaking societies** finds reciprocal relationships between intensive agricultural investments and social hierarchy; political complexity was more a driver than a result of intensification in the modeled relationship. Therefore, irrigation and terracing can be consequences of organization as well as causes. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1714558115)

**Internally developed institutions and imported institutions need different pathways.** Allow neighboring organizations, migrants, conquerors, and returning specialists to transmit office designs or administrative practices. An externally introduced institution should still require local staffing, resources, and acceptance before it functions.

## 3. Parameters and calibration

### 3.1 Empirical anchors: useful numbers with important limits

Confidence below refers to **portability into TCE**, not merely whether a number appears in a publication.

| Quantity | Published value or range | Units and interpretation | Source; portability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Population increase associated with another hierarchy category | **4.47**, interval **3.57–5.44** | Ratio of geometric-mean population sizes across categories | Hamilton et al. 2020; **low–medium** |
| Geographic area increase across categories | **2.01**, interval **1.66–2.42** | Area ratio—not radius or travel distance | Hamilton et al. 2020; **low–medium** |
| Population density increase across categories | **2.25**, interval **1.81–2.80** | Density ratio | Hamilton et al. 2020; **low–medium** |
| Proposed practical reach of relatively undelegated central authority | Approximately **25–30 km one way** | Roughly half a day’s travel in the argument’s setting | Spencer 2010; **low**, technology- and terrain-dependent |
| Estimated population of the Erlitou capital in one archaeological phase | Approximately **18,000–30,000** | People in the capital, **not total polity population** | Discussed by Spencer 2010; **low–medium**, reconstruction |

The scaling estimates come from **1,121 ethnolinguistic populations** linked to traditional political classifications. These are not equivalent to contemporaneous unified polities. They should be used as broad comparative checks, not thresholds for founding governments. The distributions overlap substantially. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0234615)

The territorial estimate is best translated into **travel-time accessibility**, not a universal circular border. Navigable water, roads, seasonal conditions, and delegation can change its significance. Likewise, the Erlitou estimate illustrates why capital population must be kept distinct from population under political authority. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43130989_Territorial_expansion_and_primary_state_formation)

**There is no defensible universal minimum population or territory for statehood in these sources.** For TCE, rising population should increase workloads, opportunities, and coordination problems—not trigger a state directly.

### 3.2 Parameters from an existing early-polity model

Gavrilets, Anderson, and Turchin’s *Cycling in the Complexity of Early Societies* provides useful experimental settings. These are **published modeling choices**, only partly anchored in particular historical estimates. [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5536t55r/qt5536t55r.pdf)

| Parameter | Values explored | Correct interpretation | Confidence as a general historical parameter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct subordinate span | **5, 6, 7** | Subordinate units per superior | **Low** |
| Expected chief tenure | **5, 10, 20 years** | Leadership-turnover setting | **Low** |
| Upward tribute fraction | **10%, 20%, 30%** | Remittance between hierarchical levels | **Low** |
| Resource variation | Coefficient of variation **0.3–0.5** | Spatial variation in modeled village resources | **Low**, landscape-specific |
| Strength advantage in warfare | Exponent **1 or 2** | Alternative sensitivity of victory probability to relative strength | **Model assumption** |

The paper cites narrower contextual estimates of approximately **16–22%** for tribute transfer and **0.34–0.48** for resource variation. **The tribute parameter is not a universal harvest tax or share of GDP**: its accounting includes transfers through hierarchy. [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5536t55r/qt5536t55r.pdf)

### 3.3 Explicitly uncalibrated TCE starting ranges

These are engineering suggestions for initial sensitivity experiments, not claims about historical averages.

| Proposed input | Initial sweep | Units | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agricultural levy demanded | **0–30%** | Share of assessed harvest | Test collection, evasion, hunger, and political response |
| Labor obligation demanded | **0–60** | Days per eligible adult per year | Test projects against agricultural opportunity costs |
| Institutional reserve target | **3–12** | Months of recurring material obligations | Test resilience to interrupted revenues |
| Routine political review interval | **7–30** | Simulation days | Separate strategic reconsideration from daily activity |
| Persistent interpersonal ties | **16–64** | Stored ties per person | Bound computational cost; **not a claim about human cognitive limits** |

Do not treat the top of a sweep as sustainable. Evaluate obligations against actual crop calendars, household composition, goods availability, travel, and enforcement.

Derive staffing endogenously from work and productivity. A fixed “one bureaucrat per 100 citizens” ratio would conceal the difference between a tribute collector, a cadastral administration, and a government providing extensive services.

These should be **environmental and institutional configurations**, not chronological stages every TCE world must traverse.

| Context | Evidence and variation | TCE implication |

| **Mobile foraging and flexible residence** | Mobility, dispersed resources, and shared access can constrain lasting hierarchy; comparisons within Aboriginal Australia also show substantial variation in power rather than uniform equality. | Model relocation, changing group membership, and social constraints on domination. Do not assume either universal harmony or permanent chiefs. [The Australian National University](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/constraints-on-the-development-of-enduring-inequalities-in-late-h/) |
| **Stored-resource foragers and fishers** | Northwest American comparisons connect storage defense, seasonality, territorial strategies, and political differences. Farming is not required for defendable wealth or organized competition. | Permit political concentration around fisheries, storage, and access rights without cereal agriculture. [WSU Research Portal](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Storage-defense-Expansive-and-intensive-territorialism/99900586062801842) |
| **Early farming: southern Mesopotamia** | Irrigation coordination provides one empirically supported route toward government and tribute. | Public projects can create offices; those offices can subsequently acquire additional powers. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20201919) |
| **Early urbanism: Indus region** | Green argues that urban complexity coexisted with much less evidence for dominant managerial elites than standard models predict. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-020-09147-9) |

[ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43130989_Territorial_expansion_and_primary_state_formation) |
| **Andean political development** | Titicaca research identifies both exchange and conflict; Inka evidence later demonstrates extensive non-alphabetic accounting. | Support trade-financed centers, labor obligations, tribute networks, and alternative recording media. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260943616_Trade_and_Early_State_Formation_in_the_Northern_Titicaca_Basin_Peru) |
| **Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific** | Closely related societies developed different political structures; comparative phylogenetic work finds both increases and reversals. | Geography and inherited institutions should produce divergent outcomes, not an inevitable island-chiefdom progression. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47415596_Rise_and_Fall_of_Political_Complexity_in_Island_South-East_Asia_and_the_Pacific) |
| **Precolonial Africa** | Henn and Robinson’s 2026 reconstruction estimates about **45,000 independent polities around 1880**, with **at most 2%** classified as states. This is a new, definition-sensitive estimate, not a census. They emphasize decentralized organization as a viable political equilibrium. | A populated, productive region need not consolidate into states. Political boundaries should not be equated with ethnic groups. [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-economics-051624-070224) |

[Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0022050709000345/type/journal_article) |
| **Modern administrative states** | An Indian experiment covering **157 subdistricts and 19 million people** found that improved payment infrastructure could make benefit delivery faster and less corrupt. This concerns capacity within an existing state, not initial state formation. | Distinguish administrative reach, information quality, payment reliability, and corruption from regime labels. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20141346&utm_source=chatgpt.com) |

For later development, preserve the same core mechanisms but change their inputs. Better communications reduce message delay; improved transport changes effective reach; formal records alter what can be assessed; larger organizations create different coordination and monitoring problems.

Besley and Persson’s framework is especially useful here: fiscal and legal capacities are investments that constrain future policy and can complement one another. They depend on incentives to invest, not simply on possession of a technology. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.99.4.1218)

**Do not equate industrialization with democracy, bureaucracy with autocracy, or decentralization with institutional absence.** In TCE, those should be independently observable dimensions.

## 5. Reversals, fragmentation, and collapse

### 5.1 Model several kinds of failure

“Collapse” should not be a single event or universal population-loss percentage. Separate at least:

| Failure type | What changes | What may survive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dynastic failure** | Officeholder or ruling family loses power | Treasury, courts, local administration |
| **Coalition fragmentation** | Tributaries or member communities withdraw | Local institutions, production, population |
| **Fiscal-administrative failure** | Offices become unpaid or cease functioning | Informal governance, private organizations |
| **Territorial contraction** | Central authority loses distant communities | A viable smaller polity |
| **Urban abandonment** | Residents relocate from a center | People, skills, networks, successor settlements |
| **Demographic catastrophe** | Deaths sharply reduce population | Some institutions, claims, knowledge, infrastructure |

This is a proposed outcome taxonomy: use it to avoid interpreting every lost capital or dissolved kingdom as the disappearance of a society.

### 5.2 Succession exposes personal versus institutional authority

The early-polity model of Gavrilets and colleagues produces cycles through conquest, hierarchy, and secession, with leadership turnover a major influence. Its value is the explicit distinction between a leader’s temporary network and a more persistent organization. [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5536t55r/qt5536t55r.pdf)

**TCE rule:** on death, incapacity, deposition, or disputed succession, re-evaluate relevant relationships rather than deleting the government.

An official’s loyalty to a person, a household, an office, and a shared institution should be separate. A subordinate may recognize the office but reject its new occupant. A functioning selection procedure can reduce uncertainty without guaranteeing peace.

### 5.3 Fiscal and informational failures can reinforce each other

A useful proposed feedback is:

\[
\text{revenue shortfall}
\rightarrow \text{unpaid personnel}
\rightarrow \text{reduced enforcement/service}
\rightarrow \text{lower compliance}
\rightarrow \text{further revenue shortfall}
\]

Another is:

\[
\text{administrative overload}
\rightarrow \text{delays and unequal treatment}
\rightarrow \text{local workarounds}
\rightarrow \text{weaker central information}
\rightarrow \text{more overload}
\]

These loops allow institutions to unravel without a special disaster trigger. External shocks can accelerate them, but their consequences depend on reserves, redundancy, alternative revenues, and local cooperation.

Blanton and colleagues also argue that failures of leadership and reciprocal obligations can undermine otherwise capable collective governments. Treat this as a conditional hypothesis about trust and interdependence, not “good governments collapse harder” as a universal rule. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.568704/full)

### 5.4 Reversibility should be a normal possibility

Currie and colleagues’ analysis of **84 Austronesian-speaking societies** favors gradual increases in political complexity. Its best-fitting model also has incremental decreases; a close alternative allows larger downward changes. This supports reversibility while cautioning against claiming that all growth is gradual and all collapse abrupt. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47415596_Rise_and_Fall_of_Political_Complexity_in_Island_South-East_Asia_and_the_Pacific)

Do not assign every state a fixed lifespan. Let failure risks depend on current obligations, conflicts, personnel, and relationships.

After fragmentation, preserve surviving knowledge and infrastructure. Political reorganization is not necessarily technological regression.

## 6. Modeling recommendation for TCE

### 6.1 Represent government as people, offices, assets, and relationships

A suitable minimal representation is:

```
Institution
    purpose and jurisdiction
    membership and constituencies
    offices and officeholders
    appointment, removal, and succession rules
    obligations, exemptions, and enforcement procedures
    owned assets and material inventories
    records and administrative procedures
    legitimacy assessments by constituency

AuthorityRelationship
    superior and subordinate
    scope: tribute, defense, adjudication, appointments, or administration
    obligations owed in both directions
    enforcement arrangements
    autonomy retained
    recognition of current officeholders
    grounds and practical options for withdrawal
```

This is deliberately **not just a government object attached to a territory polygon**.

An agent can simultaneously belong to a household, lineage, neighborhood, ritual organization, irrigation association, and military coalition. Those organizations can disagree about obligations and jurisdiction.

Maintain separate representations of:

* **claimed authority**;
* **recognized obligations**;
* **actual performance and compliance**.

A map colored as “controlled” should not silently imply that every household is taxed, protected, and subject to the same court.

### 6.2 Let offices arise from proposals and support

A proposed office should specify a task, occupant-selection rule, powers, compensation, and required contributions. It is established when a sufficient coalition agrees—or when coercive actors can impose it—and when it can actually operate.

Agents need not conduct sophisticated constitutional reasoning. They can compare a bounded set of familiar templates:

* appoint a respected mediator;
* rotate maintenance supervision;
* retain a successful war leader;
* pay a permanent storekeeper;
* delegate tax collection;
* create a council to constrain a collector.

Templates define **available arrangements**, not guaranteed historical outcomes.

An office can accumulate additional responsibilities when existing actors find that arrangement useful or cannot resist it. It can also lose responsibilities to rival organizations.

### 6.3 Connect institutional capacity to visible daily life

For every functioning institution, there should be actual activity:

* collectors travel and receive goods;
* storekeepers count and preserve them;
* messengers carry instructions;
* petitioners and judges spend time on cases;
* workers maintain canals and roads;
* soldiers consume supplies and train or patrol.

A material budget can be represented as:

\[
\mathbf{T}\_{t+1} =
\mathbf{T}\_{t}
+\mathbf{receipts}
-\mathbf{provisions}
-\mathbf{transfers}
-\mathbf{project\ inputs}
-\mathbf{losses}
\]

Bold quantities are **vectors of goods**, not a universal money balance. Labor obligations belong in scheduling ledgers; unused labor cannot be stored like grain.

Early governments can provision workers directly. Monetization changes accounting and exchange possibilities, not the need for real resources.

### 6.4 Give agents limited information and meaningful alternatives

Political behavior should respond to personal experience, network information, expectations, and perceived risks—not omniscient access to polity statistics.

A household may underestimate a ruler’s weakness. A collector may know local production better than the center. A chief may overestimate a subordinate’s loyalty.

For compliance, compare the perceived consequences of paying, bargaining, concealing, relocating, seeking another protector, or joining resistance. Keep these choices distinct: failure to deliver an obligation may reflect inability rather than opposition.

### 6.5 Keep labels downstream of behavior

Compute a political profile from observables such as:

| Dimension | Example diagnostic |
| --- | --- |
| Territorial integration | Share of settlements recognizing a common superior |
| Fiscal reach | Share of assessed obligations actually delivered |
| Administrative specialization | Staff time devoted to differentiated governing functions |
| Judicial integration | Cases accepted and enforced across communities |
| Military organization | Standing personnel, mobilization reliability, supply capacity |
| Institutional persistence | Continuity of offices and procedures across leadership changes |
| Accountability | Effective appointment, removal, appeal, or budget constraints |
| Local autonomy | Responsibilities retained by communities and subordinate organizations |

Then infer “chiefdom-like,” “bureaucratic state,” “tributary confederation,” or another label with uncertainty.

**The label must never grant the capabilities used to classify it.**

### 6.6 Respect TCE’s population scale

With **10,000–50,000 genuinely individual people**, TCE can represent a region containing multiple communities, competing chiefdom-like organizations, and small states. It cannot simultaneously represent million-person empires at one person per agent.

For the initial implementation, I recommend keeping the simulated world politically regional. Later, add an explicitly separate offscreen demographic layer for larger-scale interactions.

Do not quietly multiply each visible person’s political or economic contribution. That would distort households, office staffing, armies, travel, and subsistence budgets.

### 6.7 Simplify computation, not causal dependencies

Run daily work and travel at the individual level, but reconsider political arrangements less frequently and in response to relevant events. Harvests, succession disputes, military threats, and major defaults should trigger focused updates.

Use sparse social and institutional graphs, cached settlement-to-settlement travel costs, and event queues. Avoid testing every agent against every possible coalition.

Preserve a compact causal log: an office was created because disputes accumulated; contributions failed because harvests fell; a subordinate withdrew because protection ceased. These explanations make emergence inspectable.

### 6.8 Existing models and games worth borrowing from

**Gavrilets, Anderson, and Turchin: *Cycling in the Complexity of Early Societies*.** The closest precedent here for conquest, nested political relationships, and fragmentation. Its agents are communities, not individually simulated people; use it as a comparative macro-model rather than a complete kernel design. [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5536t55r/qt5536t55r.pdf)

**Lansing and colleagues’ Bali models.** Useful for demonstrating that decentralized decisions can produce large-scale coordination. Borrow the local interaction and ecological-feedback structure, not the assumption that every coordination problem has a similarly successful equilibrium. [Stockholm Resilience Centre](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/publications/publications/2018-06-28-adaptive-self-organization-of-balis-ancient-rice-terraces.html)

**Songs of Syx.** Its developer describes detailed individual simulation at large city scale, coupled to broader political and military play. It is an engineering and presentation reference—not empirical validation of how governments originate. [Songs of Syx](https://songsofsyx.com/)

## 7. Stylized facts and validation tests

The goal is to reproduce **distributions and conditional relationships**, not a predetermined sequence of civilizations.

| Validation target | Evidence anchor | Test for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Larger populations tend to accompany more hierarchy, but overlap remains substantial | Broad comparative scaling patterns | Same-sized populations must be capable of different political structures. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0234615) |
| Several dimensions of complexity often develop together | Approximately **75%** dominant-dimension variance in the foundational Seshat study | Check covariance among scale, specialization, records, and infrastructure without directly coupling their scores. [DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-39d5-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b) |
| Growth can encounter information-processing constraints | Seshat scale–information–scale pattern | Expansion should sometimes stall until staffing, delegation, or record systems improve. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32409638/) |
| Political development is reversible | Austronesian comparative transitions | Generate fragmentation and institutional loss without requiring demographic extinction. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47415596_Rise_and_Fall_of_Political_Complexity_in_Island_South-East_Asia_and_the_Pacific) |
| Coordination need not imply centralized command | Bali irrigation research | Some public-good systems should persist through associations or local agreements. [Stockholm Resilience Centre](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/publications/publications/2018-06-28-adaptive-self-organization-of-balis-ancient-rice-terraces.html) |
| Intensive production and political organization can reinforce each other | **155-society** Austronesian study | Allow organizational changes to precede agricultural investment, as well as the reverse. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1714558115) |
| Administrative functions can use different information media | Inka khipu evidence | Non-phonetic record systems must support meaningful administrative capacity. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7660516_Khipu_Accounting_in_Ancient_Peru) |
| Better delivery systems can improve capacity within an existing state | Indian payment-infrastructure experiment | Capacity can change without a new regime, population threshold, or “era.” [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20141346&utm_source=chatgpt.com) |

Add controlled simulation experiments to test the proposed mechanisms:

**Exit experiment.** Open an accessible settlement frontier while holding other conditions similar. Coercive organizations should face more difficulty retaining contributors when households have credible alternatives. Cooperative associations need not respond identically.

**Revenue experiment.** Shift income from broadly distributed household contributions to a concentrated controlled resource. Observe changes in bargaining and coalition dependence, without hard-coding a regime transition.

**Succession experiment.** Remove comparable leaders from personal and office-based organizations. Their continuity should depend on recognized procedures and relationships, not the label assigned to the polity.

**Administrative overload experiment.** Expand jurisdiction without additional staff, transport, or record capacity. Observe backlogs, delegation, noncompliance, or contraction rather than a free increase in state power.

These experiments validate the **internal consequences of the model’s assumptions**. They do not independently prove those assumptions historically.

## 8. Sources, datasets, and evidence discipline

### Most useful reusable datasets

| Resource | Best use | Principal caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Seshat Global History Databank** | Polity scale, institutions, hierarchy, infrastructure, records, and temporal comparisons | Preserve certainty tags and date ranges; an inferred presence or unknown value is not an exact founding date. [Seshat DB](https://www.seshat-db.com/core/polity/134) |
| **D-PLACE and its underlying ethnographic datasets** | Cross-cultural comparisons of political organization, subsistence, and social arrangements | Societies have specific observation periods; account for shared ancestry, contact, and historical context. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/about) |
| **Hamilton et al. supporting data** | Testing broad scale–hierarchy relationships | Ethnolinguistic population and territory are not automatically polity population and territory. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0234615) |
| **Allen, Bertazzini, and Heldring replication package** | Testing a specific coordination mechanism against archaeological spatial and temporal evidence | A strong regional design does not identify universal causal coefficients. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20201919) |
| **Dincecco historical revenue series, distributed through Global Price and Income History** | Later fiscal centralization and administrative development, **1650–1913** | European coverage and revenue definitions limit transfer to early agrarian societies. [GPIH](https://gpih.ucdavis.edu/Evidence.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com) |

### How to use the literature without hard-coding its biases

Store every calibration record with its **geographic scope, period, unit of observation, measurement definition, uncertainty, and source**. Separate a capital’s population from the population of a polity, and distinguish settlement hierarchy from administrative hierarchy.

Do not convert unknown institutional evidence to absence. Seshat’s Ghur record, for example, distinguishes unknown earlier bureaucratic evidence from inferred later presence. Coding that as a precisely dated invention would create a spurious sequence. [Seshat DB](https://www.seshat-db.com/core/polity/134)

Fit or evaluate against held-out regions, not randomly mixed observations from the same historical trajectories. Test alternative models for contested mechanisms. The appropriability debate is a good example of why a plausible explanation should not become an unquestioned prerequisite.

Finally, the sources reviewed do **not** justify universal annual probabilities for founding a chiefdom, inventing courts, developing writing, or collapsing. Such rates would require a separately defined estimation exercise with interval-censored dates and explicit observation models.

**The best first TCE implementation is a network of funded offices and negotiated obligations, subject to real workloads, material limits, and succession. Chiefdoms and states should be descriptions of networks that have become durable—not upgrades that make them durable.**
