# Legitimacy and authority: a simulation-ready report for TCE

**Recommendation:** Represent legitimacy as an individual’s belief that a particular authority is entitled to make particular decisions—not as a single national resource, a synonym for happiness, or the inverse of rebellion risk.

A citizen can dislike a ruler while respecting the office, accept a court while distrusting tax collectors, or obey an occupying army without accepting its right to rule. Scholarship on legitimating beliefs distinguishes willing deference from obedience obtained through other means; research using African surveys also treats acceptance of police, courts, and tax authorities as distinguishable outcomes. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764209338797)

For TCE, the central relationship should therefore be:

\[
\text{Experience and social interpretation}
\rightarrow
\text{authority-specific legitimacy}
\rightarrow
\text{decisions about compliance and opposition}
\]

Coercion, material interests, personal morality, organizational capacity, and expectations about others must enter alongside legitimacy.

**Evidence boundary:** Modern surveys and experiments provide useful quantitative benchmarks. Historical and ethnographic research provides broader mechanisms and institutional variation. Neither supplies a universally calibrated “legitimacy meter,” a standard recovery time after conquest, or a legitimacy threshold below which revolution necessarily occurs.

## 1. Mechanisms: what makes authority legitimate?

### 1.1 Weber’s types describe justifications, not historical stages

Weber distinguished three ideal types of legitimate domination. Actual political orders combine them; they are not a progression from primitive tradition to modern rationality. [Internet Archive](https://dn790009.ca.archive.org/0/items/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation.pdf)

| Type | What followers recognize | TCE implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Traditional** | The authority of established customs and those entitled to rule through them. | Evaluate customary succession, recognized privileges, inherited obligations, and continuity of institutions. Mere institutional age is insufficient. |
| **Charismatic** | An individual’s exceptional qualities, mission, or achievements. | Attach much of the assessment to the person. Require socially recognized demonstrations of exceptional standing; do not make popularity or fame identical to charisma. |
| **Legal-rational** | Valid rules and the authority of properly constituted offices. | Evaluate appointment, jurisdiction, rule-following, and institutional procedure separately from affection for the officeholder. This does not require democracy. |

Performance, procedural fairness, religion, and collective identity cut across this classification. A hereditary ruler can be judged by performance; a council can invoke sacred law; a revolutionary party can combine ideological, charismatic, and bureaucratic claims.

Religious authorization should not be a fourth mutually exclusive Weberian type. Research on 97 Austronesian societies finds interdependent development of religious and political authority, but no evidence establishing a universal long-term tendency toward their separation or unification. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357777/)

### 1.2 Implementable mechanisms

The following rules are a synthesis for TCE. They identify processes to implement, not fixed historical coefficients.

**Performance matters through expected obligations and attribution.**  
Assess whether an authority fulfills what the agent believes it owes: protection, dispute resolution, subsistence security, infrastructure, or restraint in extraction. Compare outcomes with expectations, not simply absolute wealth. Scott’s *The Moral Economy of the Peasant*, developed around Southeast Asian agrarian societies, emphasizes subsistence guarantees and violations of accepted obligations as politically consequential. This is an influential interpretation, not a universal account of peasant behavior. [Yale University Press](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300021905/the-moral-economy-of-the-peasant/)

Implementation: a harvest failure produces hardship directly. It damages legitimacy separately, according to whether people blame the ruler, consider relief an obligation, observe favoritism, or believe available remedies were neglected. A prosperous season should not automatically generate legitimacy for every authority.

**Procedure can make an unfavorable decision acceptable.**  
Model opportunities to speak, consistency, impartiality, respectful treatment, explanations, and correction of mistakes. These are not equivalent to getting one’s preferred outcome. Modern policing research finds a substantial association between perceived procedural justice and legitimacy, while also finding important contextual variation. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-023-09595-5)

Implementation: record the outcome and the process as different observations. An agent can lose a land dispute, suffer a material loss, and nevertheless strengthen its acceptance of the court.

**Custom constrains rulers as well as authorizing them.**  
A ruler’s claim depends on conformity to the particular customs followers recognize—not on belonging to a government labeled “traditional.” Weber’s traditional category concerns recognized customary authority, not unconstrained personal discretion. [Internet Archive](https://dn790009.ca.archive.org/0/items/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation.pdf)

Implementation: evaluate succession, taxation, access to common resources, consultation, and office privileges against locally transmitted expectations. A claimant who promises restoration of customary rights may gain support against an established incumbent.

**Sacred and ideological claims require receptive audiences and credible interpreters.**  
Religious institutions and political institutions may reinforce each other, but their relationship is variable rather than mechanically fixed. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357777/)

Implementation: a coronation, oath, doctrinal claim, ancestral narrative, or clerical endorsement affects only agents who recognize the relevant tradition and interpreter. The same normative system must also permit condemnation of rulers who violate its obligations. Building a temple should not produce an unconditional, recurring legitimacy bonus.

**Representation and belonging affect whose authority feels rightful.**  
People can interpret the same institution as representing their community or as imposing an outside hierarchy. Contemporary research finds social identification associated with police legitimacy independently of procedural fairness, although the relationship is not identical across settings. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-023-09595-5)

Implementation: derive identification from membership, treatment, language access, shared institutions, recruitment, and historical relationships. Avoid permanent ancestry-based loyalty modifiers. Identity should be socially maintained and potentially changeable.

**Intermediaries transmit authority, but their support is not identical to popular acceptance.**  
Baldwin’s research, particularly in Zambia, shows how traditional chiefs can serve as locally embedded brokers for public goods within democratic politics. Traditional authority and electoral institutions need not simply displace one another. [Political Science Department](https://politicalscience.yale.edu/publications/paradox-traditional-chiefs-democratic-africa)

Implementation: councils, chiefs, guilds, religious authorities, and respected households can endorse or contest a ruler. Their influence depends on their own credibility and relationships. An elite bargain changes elite incentives immediately; its effect on ordinary people must travel through actual networks and experiences.

**Private belief and public expression must be separate.**  
Kuran’s account of the 1989 East European revolutions emphasizes preference falsification: outward conformity can conceal opposition, and changes in perceived public support can produce surprising cascades. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/now-out-of-never-the-element-of-surprise-in-the-east-european-revolution-of-1989/B947420222BF565D0B2D93099E704BF2)

Implementation: public praise depends on private belief, audience, sanctions, and social expectations. Rumors and visible dissent update beliefs about others. Do not let the simulation infer genuine acceptance directly from attendance at ceremonies or absence of demonstrations.

**Repair must change credible expectations, not merely display an apology.**  
For TCE, make compensation, removal of abusive officials, functioning appeals, restored customary rights, and repeated delivery of promised services potential repair mechanisms. Their effects should depend on perceived sincerity, implementation, and attribution.

Crucially, repair can be institution-specific. An Indonesian field experiment found that changing project selection procedures improved acceptance of those projects without improving approval of the president or village head. This supports modeling limited spillovers rather than automatically applying every local experience to the entire regime. [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/direct-democracy-and-local-public-goods-indonesia)

### 1.3 Legitimacy after conquest

**Military victory changes control immediately; it does not establish an equally immediate change in perceived rightfulness.**

A conquest model should evaluate what happens to land, security, offices, local institutions, religious practices, and recognized rights. Different groups can regard the same conquest as liberation, restoration, profitable accommodation, or dispossession.

Historical scholarship on Qing imperial ideology emphasizes the incorporation of disparate political traditions into imperial authority. The useful modeling lesson is that a conqueror can construct different justifications for different constituencies rather than relying on one universal claim. [University of California Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/a-translucent-mirror/paper)

Modern wartime evidence also warns against symmetric updates. Lyall, Blair, and Imai studied **2,754 male respondents in 204 Afghan villages**. Harm attributed to international forces was associated with reduced support for those forces and increased support for the Taliban; Taliban victimization did not produce an equivalent transfer of support to international forces. The endorsement experiment helped measure sensitive attitudes, but victimization itself was not randomly assigned. [Kosuke Imai's Homepage](https://imai.fas.harvard.edu/research/afghan.html)

For TCE, therefore:

* Update victims, beneficiaries, brokers, and unaffected households differently.
* Do not transfer every loss of incumbent legitimacy into equal support for a rival.
* Let preserved institutions and credible new arrangements create acceptance through experience—not through an automatic “occupation penalty expires” timer.

### 1.4 Legitimacy after revolution

A revolutionary coalition should not receive a uniform legitimacy reset. Its members can agree on removing the incumbent while disagreeing about property, religion, representation, or the distribution of rewards.

Represent promises and expectations by constituency. Distinguish a founder’s personal standing from acceptance of the successor office. Revolutionary achievements can become enduring narratives, but institutionalization requires rules, organizations, and succession arrangements.

Also separate revolutionary legitimacy from revolutionary **durability**. Levitsky and Way identify strong ruling parties, destruction of independent power centers, protection against coups, and coercive capacity as explanations for the survival of revolutionary regimes. Survival consequently cannot serve as a direct measure of popular acceptance. [Journal of Democracy](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-durability-of-revolutionary-regimes/)

## 2. Parameters, measurement, and quantitative anchors

### 2.1 What can actually be measured?

The most useful attitudinal indicators ask whether an institution has the right to make binding decisions or deserves deference—not merely whether respondents like its current performance. Levi, Sacks, and Tyler explicitly connect legitimating beliefs to willingness to accept the authority of particular state institutions. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764209338797)

For validation, keep three classes of observations distinct:

| Measurement class | Examples | Principal limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct legitimacy indicators** | Recognized right to tax; acceptance of an adverse judgment; obligation to obey within an institution’s jurisdiction. | Wording can blur moral obligation, resignation, and fear. |
| **Potential antecedents** | Perceived fairness, competence, corruption, representation, customary validity. | These help explain legitimacy; they are not automatically legitimacy itself. |
| **Behavioral outcomes** | Low-enforcement compliance, cooperation, evasion, petitions, protest, defection. | Each also depends on costs, ability, morality, coercion, and opportunity. |

In TCE’s internal diagnostics, retain both the simulated private belief and what an observer could infer from behavior. This makes it possible to reproduce misleading public appearances without making the kernel itself confused.

### 2.2 Empirical anchors

**These are calibration targets or constraints—not coefficients to copy directly into the simulation.** A correlation between survey scales is not a causal effect of adding one unit to an internal legitimacy variable.

| Quantity and setting | Reported value and units | Appropriate use and confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procedural justice–police legitimacy association | Pooled **\(r=0.407\)**; **0.397** after removing influential cases. The overall review includes 123 studies, 159 samples, and 200,966 participants; this association uses 152 samples. | Moderate confidence in a substantial positive modern association; considerable heterogeneity. The two values are sensitivity results, **not a confidence interval**. Very low confidence in direct historical transport. Chan, Bradford & Stott. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-023-09595-5) |
| Social identification–police legitimacy association | Pooled **\(r=0.392\)** across 22 samples; **0.358** after removing an influential case. | Supports a separate identification channel. Observational association, not a universal identity multiplier. Chan et al. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-023-09595-5) |
| Competence assessments and acceptance of authority in African surveys | Authors’ modeled probability contrasts associated with positive administrative-competence assessments: **+16 percentage points** for tax authorities and police; **+20 points** for courts. | Useful performance benchmark, but cross-sectional and model-dependent—not an intervention effect or an annual increase. Levi, Sacks & Tyler. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764209338797) |
| Participatory decision procedure, Indonesia | Randomized experiment in **49 villages**: direct project-selection elections increased **stated willingness to contribute by 17 percentage points**. Participation was nearly **80%**, about **20 times** meeting attendance in comparison villages. | Stronger causal evidence for this intervention and context. Willingness is not actual contribution, general regime legitimacy, or tax compliance. Olken. [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/direct-democracy-and-local-public-goods-indonesia) |

Legitimacy predicted compliance; the direct procedural-justice–compliance relationship was not reliably established in the longitudinal subset. | Moderate confidence in the pathway’s direction; insufficient basis here for one transferable compliance coefficient. Walters & Bolger. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2) |

There is a consequential asymmetry in the evidence: we have better estimates of **associations among modern attitudes** than of the rate at which a historical population’s legitimacy changed following famine, succession, religious condemnation, conquest, or reform.

### 2.3 Proposed starting parameters for TCE

The following values are **engineering priors proposed here**, not measured historical constants. Their purpose is to create testable behavior while preserving sensitivity analysis. Historical calibration confidence is low.

The adjustment half-time means the time required to move halfway toward a sustained stream of contrary, fully credited evidence. It is **not automatic forgetting when nothing happens**.

| Parameter | Starting value | Sensitivity range and units | Intended role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Performance-evidence adjustment half-time | 1 year | **0.25–2 years** | Responsiveness to sustained provision, security, and extraction experiences. |
| Procedural/institutional adjustment half-time | 3 years | **1–5 years** | Accumulation of repeated encounters with courts, officials, and appointment procedures. |
| Customary/sacred-background adjustment half-time | 20 years | **10–40 years** | Persistence of inherited expectations under ordinary evidence; major shocks can override. |
| Salient-event learning fraction, \(\eta\_e\) | 0.15 | **0.05–0.30**, fraction of distance toward the event assessment | A major betrayal, successful defense, credible restitution, or succession event. |
| Adverse-to-favorable evidence weighting | 1 | **1–3**, dimensionless ratio | Test symmetric learning first; stronger negative updating is an optional hypothesis, not an established universal law. |
| Legitimacy-score sensitivity, \(g\) | 4 | **2–6**, dimensionless | Controls how strongly the evidence mixture translates into bounded legitimacy. |
| Legitimacy coefficient in a compliance decision, \(\beta\_L\) | 2 | **1–3 log-odds per full legitimacy unit** | Tune against actual decision outcomes; also run a zero-legitimacy-effect ablation. |

For scale intuition, with jurisdiction recognition equal to one, the proposed compliance coefficient means a **0.1 increase in legitimacy** moves a baseline compliance probability of 0.50 to approximately **0.525–0.574**, holding everything else constant. This is arithmetic from the proposed model—not an observed historical effect.

Do not estimate all learning rates, source weights, and behavioral coefficients from one aggregate revolt series. Many parameter combinations could reproduce that series while implying very different societies.

## 3. Variation across eras and world regions

TCE should vary the **institutions, audiences, information channels, obligations, and opportunities** that generate legitimacy—not select a legitimacy formula from an era label.

| Setting | Grounded pattern and evidentiary limits | Implication for TCE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Foragers and other small-scale societies** | Boehm’s reverse-dominance account emphasizes collective constraints on would-be dominators. Research among Tsimane forager-horticulturalists and Nyangatom pastoralists connects leadership with solving collective-action problems. Neither population is an unmodified proxy for prehistoric foragers. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/204166) | Permit respected coordinators without sovereign offices. Authority can be task-specific; followers can refuse, constrain leaders, or leave. Do not make all foraging societies identical or automatically egalitarian. |
| **Early farming communities** | Direct evidence of ordinary people’s legitimating beliefs is especially thin. Comparative work supports investigating coordination, public goods, ritual institutions, and political–religious relationships, but does not establish one obligatory institutional sequence. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283314868_Leadership_solves_collective_action_problems_in_small-scale_societies) | Treat lineage standing, ritual authorization, councils, resource management, and emerging offices as competing hypotheses. Farming must not automatically create hereditary chiefs or divine kings. |
| **Preindustrial states and empires** | A comparative study of 30 premodern states examines public goods, moral obligations, and institutional constraints. Its cases include Ming China, Mughal India, and Venice, illustrating variation beyond a simple despotism–democracy contrast. Archaeological work interprets Tlaxcallan as a pre-Hispanic republican polity, though architecture is not direct evidence of mass consent. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.568704/full) | Support layered authority: ruler, court, temple, local community, corporate body. Councils and constraints must not require industrial technology; sacred and hereditary claims need not exclude obligations to subjects. |
| **Industrializing and mass-politics societies** | Modern regime-claims research records performance, personal, legal-rational, and ideological justifications rather than a single modern type. Revolutionary-regime research emphasizes organizational and coercive structures alongside the founding struggle. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/claiming-the-right-to-rule-regime-legitimation-strategies-from-1900-to-2019/6394D262B708B6199B115DFFEE11BE7C) | As communication and organization become cheaper, expand the reach of parties, associations, public narratives, and shared expectations. Do not force hereditary or religious claims to disappear. |
| **Contemporary societies** | Indonesian local participation, African traditional brokerage, and heterogeneous policing research demonstrate coexistence of procedural, institutional, identity-based, and customary channels. [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/direct-democracy-and-local-public-goods-indonesia) | Allow modern institutions to coexist with local and traditional authorities. Calibrate differences between constituencies and institutions, not merely national averages. |

Several regional lessons deserve explicit implementation.

**China and Inner Asia:** imperial authority can address multiple political traditions simultaneously. A single ruler may therefore have different legitimacy profiles among constituencies without the model assigning one “Chinese” or “nomadic” coefficient. [University of California Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/a-translucent-mirror/paper)

**South and Southeast Asia:** moral obligations concerning subsistence and extraction offer a different route to contestation from modern electoral accountability. These expectations should be authored as locally maintained norms, not as a universal agrarian psychology. [Yale University Press](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300021905/the-moral-economy-of-the-peasant/)

**Sub-Saharan Africa:** local traditional authorities can remain consequential inside electoral states. Model their relationships with citizens and state officials rather than treating them as obsolete institutions awaiting replacement. [Political Science Department](https://politicalscience.yale.edu/publications/paradox-traditional-chiefs-democratic-africa)

**The Pacific and Austronesian world:** religious and political authority can coevolve in different institutional configurations. There should be no inevitable secularization schedule. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357777/)

Across every setting, record **whose acceptance is being discussed**. Court texts, elite endorsements, tax records, archaeology, and modern surveys observe different populations and behaviors. In particular, elite acquiescence must not be substituted for the beliefs of women, subordinate communities, dependent laborers, or conquered populations.

## 4. Stylized facts and validation targets

A correct model should reproduce these patterns without scripting the historical cases themselves.

| Pattern to reproduce | Evidence or theoretical basis | Simulation acceptance test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Obedience does not uniquely identify legitimacy.** | Legitimating beliefs concern willing deference; outward behavior can also reflect fear and concealed preferences. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764209338797) | Construct two settlements with similar compliance but different legitimacy and enforcement costs. Removing enforcement should expose the difference. |
| **Procedure can affect cooperation without changing general ruler approval.** | Indonesia: **+17 percentage points** in stated willingness to contribute, without an accompanying increase in approval of national or village leaders. [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/direct-democracy-and-local-public-goods-indonesia) | A fair local decision can strengthen cooperation with that project without uniformly increasing all authority scores. |
| **Institutional acceptance can move substantially over years.** | Tanzania’s acceptance of tax authorities’ mandate rose **14 percentage points over nine years**. [Afrobarometer](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/afrobriefno122.pdf) | Permit persistent movement through repeated experiences and social learning, without forcing that particular annual rate. |
| **Harm does not produce symmetric transfers of support.** | Afghanistan’s victimization–support relationships differed by perpetrator. [Kosuke Imai's Homepage](https://imai.fas.harvard.edu/research/afghan.html) | Equivalent material losses can produce different political updates because attribution, identification, and alternatives differ. |
| **The same average dissatisfaction can produce different collective outcomes.** | Granovetter’s threshold model shows why the distribution of participation thresholds matters. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/226707) | Hold average attitudes constant while changing networks and participation thresholds; protest outcomes should differ. |
| **Visible stability can end abruptly without an equally abrupt change in underlying hardship.** | Kuran’s preference-falsification account and Epstein’s civil-violence model provide distinct mechanisms for discontinuous collective action. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/now-out-of-never-the-element-of-surprise-in-the-east-european-revolution-of-1989/B947420222BF565D0B2D93099E704BF2) | Produce long quiet intervals followed by coordination cascades after credible information, organizer emergence, or enforcement changes. |
| **An unpopular regime can survive.** | Revolutionary-regime durability can reflect organizational strength and coercive structures. [Journal of Democracy](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-durability-of-revolutionary-regimes/) | Low popular legitimacy must not automatically dissolve a cohesive, supplied coercive organization. |
| **Religion and political authority do not follow one universal trajectory.** | The 97-society Austronesian study does not establish inevitable convergence or separation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357777/) | Across runs, generate both integrated and differentiated religious–political institutions without an era-driven transition. |

These are joint validation targets. Matching revolt frequency while producing implausibly universal public support, costless taxation, or instant ideological conversion is not sufficient.

## 5. Recommended agent and institution model

### 5.1 State representation

The following is a proposed TCE specification, not a fully estimated model from the literature.

For each agent, store beliefs about a **small set of salient authorities**. Typical targets might include the village council, a court, a religious institution, the ruler’s office, its current occupant, and a rival claimant.

Keep the person and the durable office as different entities.

For each person–authority relationship, retain six evidence assessments:

\[
x\_{iak}\in[-1,1]
\]

Here \(i\) is the person, \(a\) the authority, and \(k\) one of:

**performance/reciprocity; procedural fairness; customary validity; sacred or ideological fit; collective identification; personal charismatic standing.**

Also retain confidence in the assessment and recognition of the authority’s jurisdiction. Someone who has never heard of a distant claimant is not equivalent to someone who has considered that claimant and feels ambivalent.

At the institutional level, store the actual authored rules: selection procedure, jurisdiction, obligations, privileges, accountability arrangements, and claimed justifications. The authority’s **claim to legitimacy** must remain separate from whether citizens believe it.

### 5.2 Constructing a bounded legitimacy quantity

A manageable initial formulation is:

\[
L\_{ia}
=
\sigma\left(
b\_{ia}
+
g\_i\sum\_{k=1}^{6}w\_{ik}x\_{iak}
\right)
\]

where:

\[
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
\qquad
w\_{ik}\geq0,
\qquad
\sum\_k w\_{ik}=1.
\]

Here \(L\_{ia}\in[0,1]\) is the strength of perceived rightfulness, \(b\_{ia}\) is a prior, \(w\_{ik}\) represents the agent’s weighting of different justifications, and \(g\_i\) controls sensitivity.

**Interpretation:** \(L=0.7\) is an internal normalized assessment. It is not a claim that the authority is objectively 70% legitimate, that 70% of citizens support it, or that rebellion has a 30% probability.

Weights should emerge from socialization, institutional participation, and experience. Author cultural starting distributions, but allow variation within cultures and change across generations.

Do not require legitimacy scores for different authorities to sum to one. A citizen may regard both a village council and a royal court as rightful within their respective jurisdictions.

### 5.3 Updating beliefs from events

For a perceived event \(e\), use:

\[
x'\_{iak}
=
x\_{iak}
+
\eta\_{e,k}\,c\_{ie}\,a\_{iae}
\left(y\_{ie,k}-x\_{iak}\right)
\]

where:

* \(y\_{ie,k}\) is the agent’s assessment of the event, between −1 and 1.
* \(c\_{ie}\) is credibility of the information.
* \(a\_{iae}\) is attribution or relevance to authority \(a\).
* \(\eta\_{e,k}\) is the learning fraction.

For an ordinary sustained evidence stream, derive a timestep-consistent learning rate:

\[
\eta\_k(\Delta t)=1-2^{-\Delta t/h\_k}.
\]

The half-time \(h\_k\) applies to fully credited evidence. Weak credibility or uncertain attribution slows effective adjustment.

**No new evidence should not automatically pull legitimacy toward neutrality.** Beliefs may persist, while family teaching, rituals, repeated official encounters, and competing narratives provide new reinforcement or challenge.

Give events provenance. A rumor heard from five people who all heard it from one witness is not five independent observations. Preserve uncertainty about events the agent did not observe.

Attribution should allow local failures to affect an official, the office, or the regime differently. A functioning appeal that overturns an abusive judgment could lower confidence in the original judge while strengthening acceptance of the appellate institution.

### 5.4 Entering the law pipeline

Evaluate compliance at a meaningful decision opportunity—not by rerolling every citizen every day.

The pipeline should first establish whether the agent knows the rule, believes it applies, and can comply. Inability to pay a tax is different from rejection of the tax authority.

A starting decision equation is:

\[
P(\text{comply}\_{i\ell})
=
\sigma\left[
\beta\_0
+
\beta\_L q\_{ia\ell}L\_{ia}
+
\beta\_M M\_{i\ell}
+
\beta\_S S\_{i\ell}
+
\beta\_D D\_{i\ell}
-
\beta\_C C\_{i\ell}
\right].
\]

Here:

* \(q\_{ia\ell}\): recognition that authority \(a\) may regulate this matter.
* \(M\_{i\ell}\): personal moral agreement with the rule.
* \(S\_{i\ell}\): perceived social expectations.
* \(D\_{i\ell}\): expected cost of punishment for violation.
* \(C\_{i\ell}\): relative cost of compliance.

Convert material costs and sanctions into the agent’s existing utility system before combining them. Expected punishment should depend on perceived detection and enforcement, not omniscient knowledge of the kernel’s probabilities.

This separation permits important cases: moral opposition to a valid law, obedience to an illegitimate ruler out of fear, voluntary compliance when enforcement is absent, and refusal of an order that exceeds an otherwise respected institution’s jurisdiction.

The empirical justification is strongest for keeping legitimacy as one contributor rather than the only contributor: Walters and Bolger find support for the legitimacy–compliance pathway but not a universally strong direct procedural-fairness effect on behavior. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2)

### 5.5 Opposition and revolution

Use a broader action set than “obey or rebel”:

\[
A=
\{
\text{comply, evade, petition, exit, protest, join opposition, revolt}
\}.
\]

A softmax over action utilities is sufficient initially:

\[
P(a)=\frac{\exp(U\_a/\tau)}{\sum\_{b\in A}\exp(U\_b/\tau)}.
\]

For opposition actions, include expected benefits, perceived chances of success, coordination costs, danger, organizer credibility, and the perceived legitimacy of alternatives.

Petitioning can be encouraged by confidence that an institution will provide redress. Joining a rival depends on the rival’s attractiveness, not merely dislike of the incumbent. Violent revolt requires feasible organization and opportunity as well as motivation.

Military and police agents need their own beliefs about the ruler, office, commanders, and political order. Pay, supplies, personal ties, and anticipated punishment remain separate motivations. This is how TCE can generate a popular regime losing control of its military, or an unpopular regime retaining a cohesive coercive apparatus.

### 5.6 Scale and performance

For 10k–50k people, use sparse relationships and event-driven updates.

A practical starting budget is **4–8 active authority relationships per agent**, with ordinary reviews every **7–30 simulated days** and immediate updates for salient personal events. These are computational design choices, not estimates of human social behavior.

At 50,000 agents, six authorities each, and six `f32` evidence values per relationship, the evidence arrays alone require:

\[
50{,}000\times6\times6\times4
=
7.2\text{ MB}.
\]

That excludes identifiers, confidence, jurisdiction data, networks, and event histories, but illustrates that the core belief state need not dominate memory.

For the UI, show the mean **and distribution** by settlement and constituency, alongside ruler–office differences and coercive reliance. Do not weight ordinary citizens out of the legitimacy display simply because elites matter more to immediate political survival; calculate political leverage separately.

### 5.7 Existing models and games

| Model or precedent | What to borrow | What not to inherit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Epstein, “Modeling Civil Violence” (2002)** | A compact agent-based account connecting hardship, legitimacy, perceived risk, enforcement, and intermittent unrest. Its grievance term is \(G=H(1-L)\). | Its legitimacy variable is global and externally specified. Replace it with heterogeneous, endogenous relationships; retain separate institutional and organizational mechanisms. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC128592/) |
| **Granovetter, “Threshold Models of Collective Behavior” (1978)** | Heterogeneous participation thresholds and attention to their distribution. | A single fixed percentage of unhappy people that universally triggers revolt. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/226707) |
| **Kuran, “Now Out of Never” (1991)** | Separate private preferences, public expression, and perceived support for opposition. | The assumption that every expression cascade necessarily ends in successful revolution. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/now-out-of-never-the-element-of-surprise-in-the-east-european-revolution-of-1989/B947420222BF565D0B2D93099E704BF2) |

### Recommended data sources

| Source | Best use in TCE research | Important boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Afrobarometer** | Authority-specific attitudes, including recognized rights of tax authorities, courts, and police; subgroup and temporal comparisons. | Responses are not identical to actual compliance, and fear or interpretation can affect answers. The Tanzania series is one useful worked benchmark. [Afrobarometer](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/afrobriefno122.pdf) |

[ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256051732_Trust_in_Justice_Topline_Results_from_the_Round_5_of_the_European_Social_Survey) |
| **Tannenberg et al., “Claiming the Right to Rule”** | Authored regime strategies: performance, personal leadership, legal-rational, and ideological claims. Published coverage: **183 countries, 1900–2019**. | Expert-coded ruler claims are **not measurements of citizens’ acceptance**. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/claiming-the-right-to-rule-regime-legitimation-strategies-from-1900-to-2019/6394D262B708B6199B115DFFEE11BE7C) |

[PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357777/) |
| **Seshat Global History Databank** | Historical institutional context, political organization, and religion; useful for constructing comparative scenarios. | Do not convert institutional complexity into popular legitimacy. The core scholarly reading sequence is **Weber** for ideal types; **Levi, Sacks, and Tyler** for operationalizing legitimating beliefs; **Walters and Bolger** and **Chan, Bradford, and Stott** for quantitative modern evidence; **Olken** for a causal procedural intervention; and **Granovetter, Kuran, and Epstein** for collective-action dynamics. For historical variation, add **Scott**, **Crossley**, **Baldwin**, and the comparative work of **Blanton and colleagues** and **Sheehan and colleagues**, cited above.

### What remains contested or thin

**Construct definition remains consequential.** “Legitimacy” sometimes includes obligation, shared values, legality, trust, and acceptance in different combinations. The policing meta-analysis documents substantial operational variation. TCE should choose an explicit construct and keep its antecedents and outcomes separately observable. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-023-09595-5)

**Causal estimates are limited.** Cross-sectional associations cannot establish how much fair treatment or better performance will change beliefs. Even interventions that improve willingness to cooperate do not necessarily establish effects on actual behavior or acceptance of the whole regime. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2)

**Historical explanations are not calibrated psychological laws.** Comparative studies of moral obligations, public goods, and state failure are valuable for generating mechanisms, but retrospective institutional coding cannot establish each subject’s beliefs or a universal collapse threshold. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.568704/full)

**Recovery times and succession transfers are particularly weakly quantified.** Treat the proposed learning times, event magnitudes, and person-to-office spillovers as hypotheses. Test them against multiple outcomes and historical scenarios rather than presenting them as established facts.

For validation, fit distributions of attitudes, low-enforcement compliance, institutional cooperation, public–private gaps, and opposition behavior together. Hold out societies or institutional settings, vary uncertain parameters broadly, and run ablations removing legitimacy, social information, or institutional differentiation.

**The central design principle is that legitimacy should change how people understand authority, how costly governing becomes, and which alternatives they consider. It should not directly decide whether a society survives.**
