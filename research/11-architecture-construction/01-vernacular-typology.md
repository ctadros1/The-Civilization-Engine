# A global taxonomy of vernacular architecture for TCE

## Main conclusion

**TCE should represent an architectural tradition as a learned repertoire of compatible construction recipes—not as a biome-specific appearance or a collection of independently randomized sliders.**

A building’s appearance should emerge from its activities, occupants, available materials, construction knowledge, environmental exposure, institutions, and repair history. This matches the ICOMOS conception of vernacular architecture as an evolving community practice encompassing construction, use, meaning, and transmitted knowledge—not merely an old-fashioned visual style. [ICOMOS](https://www.icomos.org/images/DOCUMENTS/Charters/vernacular_e.pdf)

The essential separation is:

> **Social organization determines what spaces are needed; construction knowledge determines how they can be made; resources and environment alter costs and performance; cultural conventions distinguish among the remaining possibilities.**

“Full vocabulary” is best understood here as an **extensible functional taxonomy covering the major worldwide families**. A finite catalog cannot exhaust every local name, hybrid, or regional detail. Nevertheless, the taxonomy below is sufficient to organize a substantial authored library without restricting TCE to European houses with alternative textures.

## 1. Mechanisms: why particular forms are built

The rules below are **proposed simulation mechanisms**, informed by the cited evidence. They are not claims that historical builders consciously optimized a universal equation.

| Driver | Implementable rule | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Available construction knowledge** | Generate candidates from recipes known to the household, its builders, and reachable contacts. Acquiring a material does not automatically reveal its construction techniques. | Craft transmission is itself an institution. Mongol ger construction, for example, combines household teaching, specialist woodworking, and collective production of components. [UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-craftsmanship-of-the-mongol-ger-and-its-associated-customs-00872) |
| **Material suitability** | Describe resources by usable properties: timber length and straightness, stone bedding, soil grading, available fibers, durability, and processing requirements. Match these to recipes. | “Earth available” is insufficient. Rammed earth, adobe, and cob require different preparation and construction processes; the same soil need not suit all three without modification. [Earth Auroville](https://dev.earth-auroville.com/traditional-rammed-earth/) |
| **Labor organization** | Price construction in work, food, transport, reciprocal obligations, and specialist compensation—not money alone. Allow communal work parties and household production. | Samoan house building could entail gifts to a master builder and feeding workers. A seemingly inexpensive local material can require substantial social mobilization. [Te Papa Collections](https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/112633) |
| **Rain and moisture** | Increase expected damage with wetting duration, splash, poor drainage, and slow drying. Eaves, raised bases, coatings, roof pitch, and maintenance reduce different parts of that damage. | Do not implement “earth forbidden in rainy climates.” Protection and maintenance are part of the assembly; unprotected and protected earth are different propositions. [YourHome](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/materials/mud-brick) |
| **Thermal conditions** | Evaluate solar exposure, ventilation, insulation, heat storage, night temperatures, and occupation schedules separately. | **Thermal mass is not insulation.** High mass can moderate day–night swings but also retain unwanted heat when nights remain warm. [YourHome](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/thermal-mass) |
| **Structural loading and hazards** | Evaluate spans, roof mass, bracing, wall continuity, connections, foundation behavior, and deterioration together. | Neither “stone” nor “round” guarantees safety. Nepalese rubble buildings and Indian bhonga houses demonstrate the importance of complete assemblies and workmanship. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf) |
| **Mobility and tenure** | Short expected occupation raises the value of portability, reusable components, and low sunk investment. Secure occupation can justify heavier construction. | Mobile does not mean technically crude: a ger is a reusable construction system. Conversely, permanent structures must not require a cereal-agriculture technology gate. [UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-craftsmanship-of-the-mongol-ger-and-its-associated-customs-00872) |
| **Household and ritual organization** | Generate a graph of activities, access rights, privacy relationships, storage, and shared facilities before drawing rooms. | At Tiébélé, different household roles are associated with different plans within the same settlement. Climate cannot explain that variation by itself. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1713/) |
| **Production inside the home** | Give workshops, crop processing, livestock, storage, and seasonal industries spatial requirements that compete with sleeping and reception space. | Gassho-style houses in Japan accommodated sericulture in their upper spaces; their form cannot be reduced to snow shedding alone. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/734/) |
| **Institutions and coercion** | Let authorities, lineages, religious organizations, landlords, and craft groups regulate construction, access, orientation, ornament, and permissible alterations. | Architectural change is not always voluntary diffusion. In the Rio Negro region, missionary opposition contributed to abandonment of communal houses, followed in some contexts by their revival. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AEtnias_do_Rio_Negro) |
| **Imitation and identity** | Households preferentially copy familiar, prestigious, successful, or socially appropriate examples, with occasional modification. Builders transmit correlated packages of choices. | Borrowing need not erase social organization. Kulina accounts describe adoption of regional house forms while incorporating them into existing practices. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AKulina) |
| **Maintenance and accumulated change** | Repair individual components; retain older foundations, walls, or plans when roofs, finishes, and openings change. | Vernacular buildings are often layered products of repeated adaptation. Their current appearance need not correspond to one construction date or one coherent “era.” [ICOMOS](https://www.icomos.org/images/DOCUMENTS/Charters/vernacular_e.pdf) |

### Separate physical feasibility from perceived safety

A useful choice model is:

\[
P(b\mid h,s,k,i)\propto
\exp\!\left[
\beta\left(
V\_{\text{activities}}+
V\_{\text{identity}}+
V\_{\text{status}}-
C\_{\text{construction}}-
C\_{\text{upkeep}}-
L\_{\text{expected}}
\right)\right]
\]

Here \(b\) is a candidate building, \(h\) its occupants, \(s\) the site, \(k\) accessible knowledge, and \(i\) institutional conditions. Normalize terms into comparable utility units before combining them.

The candidate must have a possible load path and obtainable inputs. However, **expected losses should reflect imperfect local beliefs**, not an omniscient modern engineer. Otherwise TCE will systematically eliminate the vulnerable buildings documented in real housing surveys. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf)

The coefficients, imitation strength, and risk perceptions are **calibration parameters**, not historically established universal constants.

## 2. The building vocabulary

### 2.1 Organize the taxonomy into independent—but connected—layers

The following is a recommended TCE ontology, not a claim that every culture explicitly classified buildings this way.

| Layer | Vocabulary to support | Parameters or relationships |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Spatial organization** | Single space; subdivided house; longhouse; clustered compound; courtyard compound; row; tower; house-plus-outbuildings; seasonally occupied shelter | Activity graph, household sharing, access restrictions, expansion rules |
| **Plan geometry** | Circular, semicircular, oval, apsidal/rounded-ended, polygonal, rectangular, irregular, composite, partially or fully courtyard-enclosing | Dimensions, curvature, angles, court fraction, orientation |
| **Structural system** | Posts and beams; stacked logs; load-bearing earth or masonry; framed infill; lattice or bent-pole frame; compression vault or dome; tensile shelter; hybrids | Members, spans, supports, joints, bracing, load paths |
| **Envelope** | Separate walls and roof; continuous roof-to-ground enclosure; open-sided canopy; partly screened enclosure; earth-covered construction | Layers, thickness, permeability, exposed area, closure states |
| **Ground interface** | Excavated, ground-level, plinth-raised, pier-raised, piled, floating, rock-supported | Elevation, support spacing, bearing area, moisture exposure |
| **Openings and circulation** | Doors, hatches, shutters, screens, glazed openings, roof apertures, stairs, ladders, porches, galleries | Area, position, operation, access graph, ventilation paths |
| **Finish and ornament** | Plaster, wash, paint, carving, patterned masonry, textiles, finials, relief, inscriptions, symbolic arrangements | Technique, placement, palette, motif grammar, eligibility |
| **Lifecycle** | New construction, extension, reroofing, replastering, conversion, dismantling, reuse | Component age, condition, maintenance knowledge, ownership |

D-PLACE supplies useful cross-cultural categories for plan, floor level, wall material, roof shape, and covering. GEM’s engineering taxonomy adds a crucial separation between material, structural system, roof form, and connections. Neither should be adopted unchanged: D-PLACE is too coarse for construction mechanics, while an engineering taxonomy does not encode household meaning. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA079)

### 2.2 Roof forms

**Roof geometry, supporting structure, and covering must be separate fields.** A curved roof can be a bent timber frame, thatch over poles, a masonry vault, or a modern shell; these are not interchangeable mechanically. GEM explicitly separates roof shape, covering, structural system, tie-downs, and wall connections. [GEM Taxonomy](https://taxonomy.openquake.org/?cat=r)

| Geometric family | Variants worth authoring | Principal controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low-fall roof / terrace** | Single plane; parapeted terrace; earth-covered roof; stepped terraces | Drainage falls, outlets, parapet height, occupation load |
| **Mono-pitch / shed** | Lean-to; detached single slope; unequal linked slopes | Pitch, high and low edge heights, attachment |
| **Gable** | Symmetric, asymmetric, cross-gabled, offset ridge, curved eaves | Two slopes, ridge position, end-wall treatment |
| **Hip and pyramid** | Four-sided hip; pyramidal; half-hip; gablet | Ridge length, hip geometry, clipping of ends |
| **Cone and faceted cone** | True cone; polygonal approximation; truncated cone | Radius, apex height, central opening |
| **Rounded longitudinal roof** | Barrel-like; arched; rounded-ended | Cross-section curve, length, end treatment |
| **Dome, beehive, pointed dome** | Hemisphere; elongated dome; pointed or ogival profile | Curvature, rise, base shape, crown opening |
| **Saddle or upswept roof** | Concave longitudinal profile; raised ends; curved eaves | Ridge curve, eave curve, overhangs |
| **Broken-pitch roof** | Gambrel-like, mansard-like, multiple slope segments | Break heights and successive pitches |
| **Compound and tiered roof** | Linked pavilions; stacked roofs; clerestory bands | Adjacencies, tier count, roof gaps, valleys |
| **Tensioned shelter** | Ridge tent; conical tent; multiple-pole canopy | Anchors, pole arrangement, membrane tension |

The major rounded, conical, flat, shed, gabled, and hipped families occur in the Ethnographic Atlas roof coding. The additional subdivisions above are **authoring refinements** intended to avoid one mesh per named tradition. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA082)

For every family, retain separate parameters for pitch, eaves, overhangs, ridge or crown openings, drainage, and structural supports. For curved roofs, one “pitch” is inadequate: use a profile or several control points.

**Covering vocabulary:** grass, straw, reed, palm and other leaves; bark; wood shingles and shakes; split bamboo; hides; felt and cloth; earth and turf; stone slabs and slate; fired-clay tiles; metal sheets; later membranes and composite products. Snow and ice also belong in a global envelope vocabulary, although they are specialized materials rather than a default Arctic house type. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA083)

**Supporting vocabulary:** rafters, purlins, ridge poles, internal posts, tied beams, trusses, bent poles, lattice shells, corbelled courses, true arches/vaults, and tensile networks. Do not require timber centering for every masonry vault: Nubian-vault construction provides a documented counterexample using particular earth-brick laying methods. [La Voûte Nubienne](https://www.lavoutenubienne.org/en/processus-de-construction)

### 2.3 Wall systems

| System | Construction identity | What TCE must distinguish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Timber or bamboo frame** | Posts and beams carry the principal loads; walls may be independent infill | Joint type, bracing, member dimensions, bay spacing, replaceable infill |
| **Log construction** | Horizontal logs or timbers form load-bearing walls | Corner joints, log dimensions, settlement, opening reinforcement |
| **Wattle and daub** | Woven or interlaced members receive an earthen coating | Structural frame versus wattle; coating thickness; cracking and repair |
| **Lightweight panel enclosure** | Planks, bark, mats, woven screens, leaves, hides, felt, or cloth | Weather barrier versus structure; permeability; portability |
| **Adobe** | Molded, dried earth units laid in courses | Unit size, mortar, bond, wall thickness, curing and moisture protection |
| **Rammed earth** | Moist earth compacted in formwork | Lift dimensions, compaction, formwork, reinforcement or stabilization |
| **Cob / stacked earth** | Plastic earth mixture placed in successive masses or lifts | Fiber, drying intervals, lift geometry; not an adobe block wall |
| **Cut earth or sod blocks** | Blocks cut from suitable ground or turf | Root reinforcement, weather exposure, roof/wall interface |
| **Stone masonry** | Rubble, coursed stone, or dressed blocks; dry or mortared | Stone shape, through-bonding, mortar, corner connection, thickness |
| **Fired-brick masonry** | Kiln-fired units in bonded courses | Firing supply chain, mortar, bond, arches, wall support |
| **Hybrid systems** | Masonry or earth combined with timber frames, lacing, lighter upper floors, or reinforced elements | Different systems by storey and wall; connections between them |
| **Later industrial systems** | Light standardized framing, blockwork, reinforced concrete, steel, manufactured panels | New production capabilities, not an automatic replacement of inherited layouts |

The distinctions among adobe, rammed earth, cob, and wattle-and-daub are especially important: “mud wall” is not an adequate construction class. Auroville Earth Institute’s technique inventory explicitly treats these as different processes. WHE additionally documents log and mixed-material systems in real buildings. [Earth Auroville](https://dev.earth-auroville.com/technique-overview/)

A **wall assembly** should therefore be something like:

`supporting frame → woven substrate → earth coat → finish`

—not simply `material = mud`.

Also allow **no distinct wall at all**. D-PLACE explicitly includes open-sided buildings and buildings whose walls merge into their roofs. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA081)

### 2.4 Foundations, floors, and ground contact

At minimum, author earthfast posts; posts on pads; masonry piers; rubble or masonry strip foundations; raised earth or stone plinths; piles; excavated floors; ground-bearing floors; suspended timber or bamboo floors; and later rafts or slabs. Keep **foundation type separate from occupied-floor elevation**: a raised floor does not necessarily imply deep piles. WHE records both strip foundations and individual post supports, while D-PLACE separately codes subterranean, ground-level, platform-raised, and substantially raised floors. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf)

Recommended state variables are soil bearing class, wetness, frost exposure, scour exposure, ground-contact decay, support spacing, and floor height. Foundations should interact with the settlement’s drainage and terrain model rather than use one global depth.

Floors themselves need material and use states: beaten earth, plastered earth, stone or brick, boards, bamboo, mats, and layered or insulated constructions. A storage platform, sleeping platform, veranda, and fully enclosed storey are different functional objects.

### 2.5 Openings, thresholds, and ornament

Do not reduce openings to “window size.” Support open perimeters, removable screens, shutters, lattices, translucent panels, glazing, slit openings, roof holes, smoke vents, clerestories, and specialized ventilation shafts. Record **area, height, orientation, operability, privacy, weather protection, and connection to an airflow path**. Yazd demonstrates the interaction of wind-catchers, courtyards, underground spaces, and substantial earthen construction; the opening is part of a spatial system. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1544/)

Thresholds should include porches, verandas, galleries, vestibules, covered passages, stairs, ladders, and roof access. Their geometry changes how people meet, work, guard entrances, and move between inside and outside.

For ornament, separate:

| Dimension | Examples of parameterization |
| --- | --- |
| **Technique** | Painting, incising plaster, carving, relief, woven pattern, patterned masonry |
| **Location** | Door surround, wall band, gable, ridge, post, interior partition |
| **Visual grammar** | Repetition, symmetry, border hierarchy, figure-ground pattern, palette |
| **Meaning and permission** | Household, lineage, status, office, religious affiliation, restricted motif |
| **Production** | Maker’s skill, labor, pigment or tool requirements, renewal interval |

Tiébélé is particularly useful: surface decoration is transmitted through practice and social occasions, not generated merely by purchasing a higher ornament level. Its locally specific division of construction and decorative labor should be represented as a learnable institution, not imposed as a universal gender rule. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1713/)

## 3. Quantitative parameters and their limits

### Evidence labels

**O** = reported observation or local building profile.  
**G** = technical guidance or representative engineering value.  
**P** = proposed TCE prior.

**H confidence** means the stated value or count is well supported in its stated scope. **M** means a useful descriptive or engineering anchor with limited transferability. **L** means an uncalibrated modeling choice. These are not statistical confidence intervals.

### 3.1 Dimensions and assembly characteristics

| Parameter | Reported value | Scope and interpretation | Evidence / confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adobe wall thickness | **0.30–0.80 m** | Peruvian adobe housing profile; not a global earth-wall distribution | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf) |
| Roof or floor span | **3–6 m** | Same profile; distinguish structural span from total building width | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf) |
| Opening proportion | **30–40% of wall length** | Sum of door and window widths in that profile; **not window-to-wall area ratio** | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf) |
| Rubble strip-foundation depth | **At least 0.40 m reported** | Local Peruvian practice, **not a universal safe minimum** | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf) |
| Nepalese rubble-wall thickness | **0.45–0.60 m** | Uncoursed stone with mud mortar | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf) |
| Nepalese cross-wall spacing; storey height | **4–6 m; about 2.2 m** | Same typology, commonly two storeys | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf) |
| Bhonga internal diameter; wall height | **3–6 m; about 2.5 m** | Kutch, India; circular earthen house with conical roof | O / M. [EERI](https://eeri.org/lfe/pdf/india_traditional_rural_house.pdf) |
| Malawi pole-and-mud house diameter; wall height | **3–4 m; generally under 2 m** | Particular reported rural typology | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100075.pdf) |
| Malawi main wall poles | **0.05–0.07 m diameter; about 1.5 m spacing** | Same profile; not a generic timber-frame specification | O / M. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100075.pdf) |
| Kulina occupied-floor elevation | **1–2 m above ground** | Ethnographically described raised houses in western Amazonia | O / M. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AKulina) |
| Kulina leaf-roof pitch | **About 45°** | Same account; approximate, not a surveyed distribution | O / M. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AKulina) |
| Matsés communal-house entrance height | **About 1.25 m** | Reported openings at corridor ends | O / M. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AMats%C3%A9s) |
| Mandan–Hidatsa–Arikara earth-lodge diameter | **30–60 ft ≈ 9.1–18.3 m** | Overall diameter; internal posts mean this is **not** a clear-span requirement | O / M. [National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/places/mandan-hidatsa-arikara-tourism-earth-lodge-village.htm) |

These values are suitable as **recipe-specific anchors**. They should not become global minima, maxima, or random distributions.

### 3.2 Roof behavior and component lifetimes

| Parameter | Value | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grass or palm thatch pitch | **45° minimum in the cited guide** | G / M; guidance for its described assemblies, not every worldwide thatching technique |
| Untreated wood shingle/shake pitch | **45° in the guide’s introductory guidance** | G / M; details and treatments change requirements |
| Wet thatch covering mass | **Approximately 40 kg/m²** | G / M; covering only, not the complete supporting roof |
| Coconut-leaf covering life | **1–2 years in an Indian example; up to 8 in East Africa** | Reported local examples |
| Rice-straw covering life | **About 6 years, Bangladesh** | Reported example |
| Wheat-straw covering life | **About 30 years, England** | Reported example |
| Water-reed covering life | **60+ years, northern Europe** | Reported example |

Source: BASIN, *The Basics of Biomass Roofing*. Lifetimes are **context-dependent guide examples**, not controlled comparisons, global averages, or whole-building lifetimes. Maintenance and material preparation matter. [EcoSur](https://ecosur.org/index.php/es/publicaciones-2/ecomateriales?download=50%3Abiomass-roofing)

For quantities, use geometry rather than arbitrary “steep roof cost” multipliers. For a planar roof face:

\[
A\_{\text{surface}}=\frac{A\_{\text{horizontal projection}}}{\cos\theta}
\]

Thus a 45° face has approximately **1.414 times** its horizontal projected area. Include overhangs in the projection and calculate compound faces separately.

### 3.3 Thermal properties

| Material | Representative density | Representative specific heat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adobe | **1,550 kg/m³** | **0.837 kJ/(kg·K)** |
| Rammed earth | **2,000 kg/m³** | **0.837 kJ/(kg·K)** |
| Sandstone | **2,000 kg/m³** | **0.900 kJ/(kg·K)** |

These are representative values in the Australian government’s *YourHome* guidance, not immutable properties of every historical material. Density, moisture, composition, and construction alter performance. The same guidance uses approximately **≤6°C**, **6–10°C**, and **>10°C** daily temperature ranges as broad low-, moderate-, and high-mass design contexts—not as rules predicting cultural choices. [YourHome](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/thermal-mass)

For TCE, store thermal conductivity separately from density and heat capacity. A wall’s thickness should affect resistance and storage through different calculations.

### 3.4 Explicitly provisional authoring ranges

Where evidence is missing, use labeled priors rather than invented historical statistics.

| Candidate-generation parameter | Initial exploration range | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-fall roof drainage angle | **1–5°** | P / L; recipe and drainage dependent |
| Domestic eave overhang | **0.2–1.2 m** | P / L; allow zero and exceptional larger values |
| Typical enclosed-storey height | **1.8–3.5 m** | P / L; local profiles override |
| Enclosed-facade opening area fraction | **0–0.35 m²/m²** | P / L; open pavilions require a different class |
| Small-building structural bay | **2–6 m** | P / L; not total building width |

These are debugging and authoring domains, **not recommendations to sample uniformly**. Condition dimensions on the recipe, household program, structural supports, wealth, and site.

## 4. Regional exemplars and temporal variation

### 4.1 Regional recipes—not regional stereotypes

Each row is a particular documented tradition or building family. None represents every society in its region.

| Region and exemplar | Key elements for a recipe | What it demonstrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Arctic Alaska: Nunamiut shelters** | Willow-frame skin tents; winter sod structures in other occupation contexts | Seasonal mobility and occupation strategy can produce several architectures within one society. [National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/nunamiut-caribou-skin-clothing-tents.htm) |
| **North American Plains: earth lodges** | Large round plan; timber posts and rafters; layered vegetation and earth covering | Communal households and internal supports permit large overall buildings without enormous unsupported spans. [National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/places/mandan-hidatsa-arikara-tourism-earth-lodge-village.htm) |
| **Amazonia: Marubo maloca** | Oblong communal space; palm covering extending toward the ground; internal household zones | A house may combine many domestic and collective functions; nearby raised buildings may be stores rather than additional households. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AMarubo) |
| **Amazonia: Matsés communal houses** | Elongated hexagonal plan; continuous thatched enclosure; central corridor and screened compartments | The wall–roof distinction and modern room vocabulary can both be misleading. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AMats%C3%A9s) |
| **Andes and coastal Peru: adobe housing** | Thick earth-unit walls; local roof variants; possible lighter quincha upper construction | Material systems may differ by storey within one building. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf) |
| **Iran: Yazd** | Earthen walls, vaults and domes; courtyards, underground spaces, wind-catchers | Cooling is an interaction among building section, openings, outdoor spaces, and urban infrastructure. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1544/) |
| **Yemen: Shibam** | Dense, multistorey mud-brick tower houses | Earth construction must not have a universal one-storey limit. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/192/) |
| **West Africa: Tiébélé** | Earthen compounds; figure-eight, quadrangular, and circular plans; decorated surfaces | Household roles and social meaning select among forms under similar environmental conditions. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1713/) |
| **Nubian-vault tradition and Sahelian adaptations** | Earth-brick compression vaults; substantial supporting walls; specific laying sequence | Knowledge can substitute for scarce roofing timber. Contemporary dissemination is an adaptation, not evidence that the system was historically universal across Africa. [La Voûte Nubienne](https://www.lavoutenubienne.org/en/quest-ce-que-la-voute-nubienne) |
| **Southeastern Africa: Malawi pole-and-mud profile** | Small round plan; light pole structure; earthen enclosure; conical thatch | Thin structural members and protective coatings should be modeled separately. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100075.pdf) |
| **Western India: Kutch bhonga** | Circular earth walls; conical thatched roof; compact single-storey form | Hazard performance belongs to the complete, maintained system—not to circular geometry alone. [EERI](https://eeri.org/lfe/pdf/india_traditional_rural_house.pdf) |
| **Himalaya: Nepalese rubble houses** | Thick stone-and-mud walls; timber floors; varied coverings; verandas | Wall bonding, floor connections, and workmanship can dominate vulnerability. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf) |
| **China: Fujian tulou** | Circular **or square** enclosed compounds; earthen external walls; inward-facing occupation; tiled roofs | Collective organization and defense can produce very different massing from an isolated household house. Some examples accommodated up to 800 people—an exceptional capacity, not a default. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1113/) |
| **Japan: gassho-zukuri** | Steep thatched gables; substantial timber construction; productive upper spaces | Climate, household industry, and collective maintenance jointly shape architecture. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/734/) |
| **Central Asia: Mongol ger** | Demountable round frame; roof poles; felt/canvas; ropes; decorated wooden components | Portability, skilled fabrication, and strong visual identity are compatible. [UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-craftsmanship-of-the-mongol-ger-and-its-associated-customs-00872) |
| **Island Southeast Asia: Toraja settlements** | Distinctive saddle-profile roofs; paired arrangements of houses and granaries; orientation conventions | Settlement-level relationships belong in the style system. The cited Indonesian nomination describes particular sites, not a universal Toraja dimensional standard. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5462) |
| **Pacific: Samoan faletele** | Large timber-framed, leaf-thatched meeting house; specialist builder and collective provisioning | Public hospitality and political life require building programs beyond private bedrooms. [Te Papa Collections](https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/112633) |
| **Australia: Gunditjmara/Budj Bim** | Stone house sites within an engineered wetland and aquaculture landscape | Permanent architecture cannot be gated solely on plough agriculture or European categories of farming. Do not assign the aquaculture system’s earliest date to every surviving house site. [Budj Bim](https://www.budjbim.com.au/visit/cultural-sites/kurtonitj/) |
| **Northern Eurasia: Russian log housing** | Horizontal logs, corner connections, timber roofs, foundations | “Wood construction” includes load-bearing wall systems, not only frames with infill. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100022.pdf) |
| **Portugal: Pombalino rebuilding** | Masonry combined with an internal timber cage; regulated reconstruction | Disasters and institutions can deliberately transform construction recipes. This is a useful policy precedent rather than an untouched folk tradition. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/category/reports/vernacular/) |

### 4.2 Eras should change capabilities, not unlock appearance presets

The following is a **simulation mapping**, not a universal historical sequence.

| Context | Capabilities and pressures to emphasize | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Foraging and mobile livelihoods** | Portable assemblies, seasonal occupancy, storage and processing sites, reusable materials; permanent investment where resources support it | “All foragers live in temporary huts” |
| **Early farming** | Increased storage, processing, animal accommodation, repeated investment at occupied sites, household expansion | An obligatory circular-to-rectangular transition |
| **Pre-industrial specialization** | Skilled builders, organized labor, kilns, quarrying, joinery traditions, multistorey construction, regional trade | Treating all societies as knowing every available technique |
| **Industrial production** | Standardized sections, machine production, inexpensive fasteners, transported coverings, changing labor costs | Replacing the whole house whenever one industrial material arrives |
| **Modern settings** | Manufactured assemblies, reinforced systems, mechanical services, regulation, informal adaptation and heritage revival | Treating vernacular practice as extinct |

Two documented checks are especially useful. Coast Salish woodworking included sophisticated tools and techniques before imported iron, so advanced wooden construction should not require a simplistic “metal age” gate. Nunamiut accounts describe changes from skin coverings toward canvas and later building forms, illustrating component and livelihood transitions rather than a single instant upgrade. [Burke Museum](https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/heritage/artscultures/coast-salish-art/carving-tools-technologies)

## 5. Stylized facts a correct simulation should reproduce

### 5.1 Geometric diversity is fundamental, not decorative variation

Calculated from the displayed D-PLACE/Ethnographic Atlas category counts:

| Variable | Result | Appropriate use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prevailing dwelling plan | **513/1,197 circular: 42.9%** | Circular plans deserve first-class support |
| Prevailing dwelling plan | **582/1,197 rectangular or square: 48.6%** | Rectangles are common, not universal |
| Wall–roof distinction | **292/1,082 merged or indistinguishable: 27.0%** | Test continuous-envelope buildings |
| Prevailing roofing material | **780/1,160 grass, leaves, brush, or thatch: 67.2%** | Organic roofing needs substantial material and maintenance diversity |

These are **shares of coded society observations**, not shares of the world’s people or housing stock. Different variables have different denominators. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/parameters/EA079)

Most D-PLACE observations describe particular places and periods, commonly in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They are neither a random prehistoric sample nor independent observations free of shared history. Use them to test representational breadth and conditional patterns—not to force every generated world toward these percentages. [D-PLACE](https://d-place.org/about)

### 5.2 Other important reproduction targets

**Similar environments should support multiple traditions.** Tiébélé’s different socially associated plans provide a within-settlement example. A climate-only generator should fail this test. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1713/)

**The same material should support very different scales and organizations.** Compare compact adobe houses, Shibam’s towers, and Fujian’s collective compounds. “Earth house” is not a single size, plan, or household type. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100014.pdf)

**Building count should not directly imply household count.** A communal house may shelter several families, while a household may use several structures. Marubo descriptions explicitly distinguish a multifunctional maloca from nearby storage buildings. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AMarubo)

**Change should be uneven across components.** Replacement of a covering, alteration of a threshold, and persistence of a household’s social arrangement can coexist. Kulina architectural borrowing is a useful qualitative check. [Povos Indígenas Brasil](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AKulina)

**Traditional buildings should sometimes perform poorly.** Poor maintenance, inadequate connections, unsuitable alterations, and limited resources must produce vulnerability. A world in which every inherited tradition is automatically safe and thermally optimal would contradict the engineering evidence. [World Housing](https://world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/WHEReports/wh100083.pdf)

**Architectural clusters should persist without becoming genetically fixed.** In TCE, persistence should result from learning networks, institutions, builder availability, and replacement costs. Migration, trade, coercion, and successful experiments should allow hybrids and discontinuities.

## 6. Recommended implementation for TCE

### 6.1 Four authoritative data objects

| Object | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| **Material** | Resource source; usable dimensions; density; conductivity; heat capacity; moisture and decay response; combustibility; processing requirements |
| **Assembly recipe** | Structural roles; components and joints; geometric constraints; material alternatives; labor operations; curing or drying; repair operations |
| **Construction tradition** | Weighted recipe repertoire; correlated parameter preferences; household and ritual conventions; ornament grammar; permitted users; transmission relationships |
| **Building instance** | Activity/access graph; assembly instances; actual dimensions; owners and occupants; construction phases; component ages; damage and alterations |

Attach provenance to numerical fields: source, locality, period, measurement definition, confidence, and whether the value is observed, inferred, or proposed.

A tradition should specify conditional relationships such as:

```
roof covering -> suitable slope and supporting members
wall system -> opening support and storey options
household program -> room adjacency and access restrictions
social role -> preferred plan and ornament permissions
site exposure -> protective details and expected maintenance
```

It should not simply contain independent distributions for roof, wall, and decoration.

### 6.2 Building generation as a sequence of decisions

For a new household or collective building:

1. **Create the activity program.** Sleeping, cooking, storage, work, animals, reception, ritual, and circulation; assign sharing and access rights.
2. **Retrieve known recipes.** Use household experience, available builders, neighboring examples, and institutional knowledge.
3. **Generate a small candidate set.** Combine compatible assemblies and fit them to the parcel, terrain, and program.
4. **Estimate obligations and performance.** Materials, transport, work, food, upkeep, comfort, risk, identity, and status.
5. **Choose under bounded knowledge.** Include preference variation and uncertainty rather than always selecting the numerical optimum.
6. **Build through tasks.** Acquisition, processing, drying, erection, enclosure, and finishing become visible work.
7. **Learn from occupation and repair.** Outcomes affect owners and builders who actually observe them, not every person worldwide.

The sequence is a design recommendation. The evidence supports its component mechanisms, not a particular number of candidate buildings or universal behavioral coefficients.

### 6.3 Let style evolve at different rates

Maintain separate update processes for **surface treatment**, **covering**, **structural assembly**, **plan**, and **institutional meaning**. For example, a household may adopt a new roofing material while retaining its courtyard organization and inherited entrance restrictions.

Track component vintages rather than one building age. Replastering should not reset the foundation’s age; replacing a roof should not require reconstructing a sound wall.

This also gives TCE visible historical depth without authored eras: old walls, changed openings, repaired roofs, and additions become evidence of past decisions.

### 6.4 Simplify physics without severing causality

For the Rust kernel, I recommend:

* **Thermal behavior:** a small air-and-mass model, cached by assembly and exposure, rather than room-scale fluid simulation.
* **Structural behavior:** authored capacities and vulnerability classes based on span, mass, bracing, connection quality, and condition—not finite-element analysis per building.
* **Moisture and maintenance:** exposure-driven condition updates, accelerated by specific failures such as a leaking covering or damaged base.
* **Geometry:** generate and validate at construction or alteration events, not continuously during individual-agent ticks.

These are proposed engineering trade-offs, not measured performance guarantees.

Keep Rust authoritative over dimensions, room connectivity, construction state, and recipe identity. UE5 can derive meshes, instances, textures, and decorative detail from stable identifiers and seeds. Cache distant geometry and simplify hidden interiors without allowing the renderer to invent doors or supports that contradict simulation state.

### 6.5 Existing precedents

| Model or game | Useful contribution | What TCE still needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Müller et al., “Procedural Modeling of Buildings” (2006)** | CGA shape grammar: hierarchical generation of building massing and architectural detail | A causal process selecting and changing the grammar, rather than merely applying it. [Emeritus](https://emeritus.icu.ee.ethz.ch/research/demos/procedural-modeling.html) |
| **Merrell, Schkufza and Koltun, “Computer-Generated Residential Building Layouts” (2010)** | High-level requirements, probabilistic architectural programs, and optimized layouts | Programs reflecting globally varied households and activities, not automatic transfer of modern residential assumptions. [Vladlen Koltun](https://vladlen.info/publications/computer-generated-residential-building-layouts/) |
| **Foundation** | Officially described autonomous home building, organically developing settlement features, and modular construction | A much broader repertoire and independently evolving cultural and technical traditions. Its authored medieval setting is a precedent for interaction, not a global ethnographic model. [Polymorph Games](https://www.polymorph.games/presskit/) |

A sensible first implementation would prioritize **a modest number of deeply parameterized assembly families** over hundreds of fixed named house models. Add regional exemplars as tested combinations with provenance, then permit historically plausible recombination through construction knowledge.

### Claims to flag as contested or weakly supported

**Environmental determinism:** neither climate alone nor a universal progression from “simple” to “advanced” forms adequately specifies the observed vocabulary. Treat climate as changing performance and costs, not uniquely assigning style.

**Automatic sustainability or superiority:** vernacular does not inherently mean low-impact, socially equitable, comfortable, or safe. Vellinga’s *“The noble vernacular”* critiques such idealization; housing vulnerability reports provide concrete reasons not to encode it as a bonus. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13602365.2013.819813)

**Global construction productivity and maintenance costs:** the reviewed material does not provide a consistent worldwide dataset of person-days per square meter, crew composition, transport distance, seasonal opportunity cost, and maintenance frequency. A reported “ten-day build” cannot be converted to person-days without knowing the workforce and what preparation it excludes.

**Causal behavioral coefficients:** there is no validated global number for how strongly households prefer familiar roofs, how quickly techniques diffuse, or how much prestige offsets thermal discomfort. These need sensitivity analysis and case-specific calibration.

**Survival bias:** standing heritage buildings disproportionately represent maintained, valued, or unusually durable examples. They should not define the lifespan distribution of all ordinary historical buildings.

For validation, compare a climate-only version of TCE with versions adding resource costs, social programs, institutions, and transmission. Test whether the additional mechanisms produce persistent local traditions, variation within settlements, hybridization after contact, and plausible change after shortages or disasters—not merely more attractive screenshots.

## Bottom line

**Make construction knowledge inheritable, buildings repairable, space socially organized, and physical performance consequential.** Roofs, walls, openings, and ornament can then remain combinable without becoming arbitrary. The resulting architecture will record each world’s accumulated decisions rather than announce a predetermined civilization style or technological era.
